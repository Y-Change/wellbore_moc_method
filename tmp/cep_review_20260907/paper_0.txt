ABSTRACT
Full waveform inversion (FWI), a computational wave imaging technique that fully incorporates 
the physics of wave propagation, enables high-resolution quantitative reconstruction of inho -
mogeneous media and promotes effective application in non-destructive testing (NDT). However, 
its widespread adoption is hindered by the intrinsic challenges of solving a nonlinear, ill-posed 
inverse problem, resulting in a high computational cost considering the iterative implementa -
tion, sensitivity to the initial model, and the possible convergence to a local minimum solution. 
Recent advances in data-driven FWI frameworks using neural networks have improved both 
accuracy and efficiency, yet challenges in robustness and generalization persist. Neural operators, 
an emerging paradigm in scientific machine learning, aim to learn mappings between infinite- 
dimensional function spaces, offering superior generalization and scalability for complex phys -
ical systems. Motivated by this, we propose a neural-operator-based framework for direct 
inversion of ultrasonic full-waveform data based on the Unet Fourier Neural Operator (U-FNO), 
extending the neural operator paradigm to ultrasonic NDT. By leveraging parameterized Fourier 
kernels to globally capture intricate wave physics, U-FNO demonstrates improved inference ef -
ficiency relative to classical FWI, and improved fidelity and robust reconstruction compared with 
representative data-driven models and total focusing method (TFM). Numerical and experimental 
results demonstrate that the proposed framework provides a promising direction for next- 
generation computational wave imaging technology in ultrasonic NDT.
1. Introduction
Acoustic imaging seeks to infer hidden internal structures and unknown physical properties of materials by analyzing measured 
wave signals [1] . It plays a critical role in a wide range of applications, including seismic inversion, underwater object detection, 
biomedical imaging, and non-destructive testing (NDT). This task typically involves solving ill-posed and nonlinear inverse problems. 
In particular, sensor data are generally available only at the surface or boundaries of an object, leading to a dimensional mismatch 
between the limited observed data and the unknown parameter space. Additionally, the inherent inhomogeneity of the medium gives 
rise to complex wave phenomena such as diffractions, scattering, and reflections. As a result, a single set of observations may

To address these challenges, various imaging methods have been developed, as shown in Fig. 1 . Early approaches, primarily based 
on ray approximations, are computationally efficient and stable. Representative techniques include the synthesis aperture focusing 
(SAFT) [2] , the total focusing method (TFM) [3] , the probabilistic diagnostic imaging (PDI) [4] , and plane wave imaging (PWI). 
However, these methods are inherently limited to qualitative reconstruction, as the resulting images only approximate the spatial 
variation of acoustic parameters. Ray-based tomography [5] , which estimates the sound velocity distribution of a medium based solely 
on travel time, offers coarse quantitative analysis. It ignores wave diffractions and scattering, restricting resolution to the width of the 
first Fresnel zone [6,7] . To improve ray-based methods while maintaining computational efficiency, wavefield extrapolation methods 
such as phase shift migration (PSM) [8] and Stolt migration [9] adopt a one-way wave approximation to partially account for 
diffraction effects. Diffraction tomography, leveraging linearized scattered field approximations (e.g., Born or Rytov approximations), 
accounts for both diffraction and scattering [10] and enhances the resolution for simple defects. However, its applicability remains 
confined to weak scattering and fails for complex defects with high velocity contrasts [11] .
Full-waveform inversion (FWI) has emerged as a powerful method for quantitative subsurface reconstruction with high resolution 
and accuracy by minimizing the mismatch between observed and synthetic data [12,13] . Its high resolution potential is due to the 
numerical solution of the exact wave equation, which describes more completely the physics of wave propagation in heterogeneous 
media, and can be realized with a resolution of half the wavelength of the dominant frequency [14,15] . However, directly transferring 
FWI to ultrasonic NDT is challenging due to differences in frequency range, wave propagation characteristics, and the high acoustic 
impedance contrast of materials. As a result, although FWI holds great promise for advancing ultrasonic NDT, its application has thus 
far been restricted to a small number of preliminary studies. For instance, Rao et al. [16] developed an acoustic multiparametric FWI 
method for the reconstruction of velocities and densities of metallic components. Further, they extended it to quantitative imaging of 
guided-wave corrosion damage [17] . In addition, He et al. [18] investigated the application of FWI for reconstructing complex metal 
components in a water bath environment. Their numerical evaluation demonstrated FWI has significant potential for accurately 
recovering complex structural features. To further validate these findings, experimental studies on homogeneous materials were 
subsequently conducted using accurate transducer modeling [19] . Bürchner et al. [20] quantitatively compared TFM, RTM, and FWI 
methods for phased array full matrix capture (FMC) data across multiple metrics, highlighting the powerful potential of FWI for defect 
identification and sizing. However, the FWI approach is computationally expensive, as each iteration requires forward modeling the 
full wavefield in a nonlinear optimization framework. Moreover, its inherent nonlinearity and ill-posedness often lead to convergence 
to local minima, making reconstructions highly sensitive to the quality of the initial model [13] . To address the aforementioned issues, 
several physics-based approaches have been developed. These approaches encompass the development of regularization methods [21] , 
the utilization of low-frequency information to construct an improved initial model [22] , the refinement of the convexity of the 
objective function [23] , and Multi-scale optimization strategies [24] . Despite these advancements, physics-based FWI has not consis -
tently succeeded in ultrasonic NDT inversion, underscoring the need for alternative solutions.
Motivated by the rapid development of deep learning, which has demonstrated a strong ability to establish nonlinear mappings 
between different data domains, researchers have suggested data-driven direct inversion methods to alleviate the optimization burden 
of conventional FWI [25,26] . The primary objective of these methods is to transform the optimization problem of FWI into a supervised 
learning task. This task involves constructing an inverse mapping from observed data to the Measurement structure by training neural 
networks on paired data of waveforms and medium acoustic parameters, also referred to in the literatures as data-driven FWI [27 – 30] . 
Data-driven direct inversion methods based on different neural network architectures have achieved notable success in seismic 
exploration, particularly in terms of computational efficiency and inversion accuracy [25 – 31] . More recently, such methods have been 
explored in ultrasound imaging. Suo et al. [32] developed an improved dual-encoder-based Unet with high-frequency feature 
enhancement for the quantitative imaging of bone sound velocity and density, which addresses the highly nonlinear challenge of FWI 
posed by high-contrast bone models. Lozenski et al. [33] further highlighted the computational advantages of learning FWI using CNN, 
achieving reconstruction speeds three orders of magnitude faster than traditional FWI. Ren et al. [34] proposed a physics-embedded 
neural network framework for accurate quantitative reconstruction of tumors in brain tissue by training forward convolutional net -
works and inverse sub-neural networks to replace the finite-difference modeling and iterative optimization process of FWI. Rao et al.
[35] proposed a full convolutional network (FCN) tailored to quantitatively detect defects in multi-layered bonded composites using 
FMC. Liu et al. [36] developed an Unet-based method to fully utilize echo space represented by FMC to achieve super-resolution limit 
and sub-wavelength defects visualization. Data-driven methods may be superior to traditional physics-based methods when the

Recently, a new paradigm known as neural operators has been developed to directly learn mappings between infinite-dimensional 
function spaces [38 – 40] . Neural operators have been proven to have the powerful ability to accurately approximate both nonlinear 
continuous functions (mapping from a function space to real numbers) and nonlinear operators (mapping from one function space to 
another) [38] , thereby accelerating scientific simulation and engineering design in fields such as weather forecasting [41] and carbon 
dioxide storage [42] . Among various neural operator architectures, the Fourier neural operator (FNO) [40] , which uses the fast Fourier 
transform as an integral operator to learn the integral kernel in Fourier space from data, has demonstrated superior accuracy and 
computational efficiency compared to other neural operator frameworks across a range of benchmark problems [43] . Unlike CNNs 
restricted to local convolutions on discretized grids, FNO leverages global neural integral operators to learn continuous function 
instead of discretized vectors, as shown in Fig. 2 . This property underpins its strong generalization, high accuracy and resolution- 
invariance [40] . in wave propagation modeling, effectively capturing complex physical phenomena such as nonlinear effects [44] , 
viscoelasticity [45] , and quantum mechanics [46] . Despite these advantages in forward modeling, the application of neural operators to 
inverse problems remains relatively underexplored. Initial efforts have recently begun to investigate their potential in seismic 
inversion. Lu et al. [47] incorporated FNO as the decoder within DeepONet to perform seismic inversion with variable source pa -
rameters, demonstrating strong robustness to Gaussian noise in observed data. More recently, Huang et al. proposed a neural operator- 
based FWI framework that employs a pretrained FNO as a surrogate forward model [48] and integrates wave-equation constraints into 
the inversion objective, improving reconstruction quality while maintaining computational efficiency [49] .
Inspired by these developments, we further advance the neural operator paradigm to ultrasonic NDT, addressing the highly ill- 
posed inverse problem of FWI using phased array FMC data. We propose a U-FNO framework that integrates kernel parameteriza -
tion in the Fourier domain to capture global wave defect interactions while retaining localized feature recovery through a U-Net path. 
This design enables the accurate mapping from FMC datasets, including scattering, diffraction, and reflection signals, to the longi -
tudinal wave velocity distribution of complex medium, thereby accelerating inversion efficiency. Compared with existing data-driven 
FWI methods such as InversionNet [30] , U-FNO achieves higher fidelity and robustness in reconstructing defects with high contrast and 
complex geometries.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 provides background information on physics-based and data-driven 
FWI, followed by details on the design of the U-FNO. Section 3 presents the results of complex geometric defect inversion imaging and 
compares the reconstruction accuracy and robustness of U-FNO and InversionNet. Section 4 validates the method with experimental 
data and compares the reconstruction results with InversionNet and TFM. Section 5 discusses the limitations and directions for future 
research. Finally, we share our conclusions in Section 6.

2.2. Data-driven FWI (Data-driven direct inversion)
Ultrasonic FWI aims to reconstruct the acoustic parameters distribution of the object under test, often for the detection of defects 
such as holes or through-thickness cracks in metallic materials. These defects can be simplified as regions of sound velocity anomalies 
(e.g., air inside a hole, where the sound velocity reduces to about 340 m/s). By abstracting the complex defect structure into a non- 
uniform medium with varying sound velocity, the problem of defect detection is transformed into an inverse reconstruction of a high- 
contrast sound velocity model. Due to the high nonlinearity and ill-posedness of the objective function and its sensitivity to the initial 
model, the traditional physics-based FWI method is prone to falling into local minima and struggles to accurately invert high-contrast 
models, especially when low-frequency information is absent or the initial model is unreliable. In contrast, the data-driven approach 
addresses these challenges by transforming the problem into an end-to-end mapping task. The key idea of the data-driven approach is 
to create a training dataset of paired data consisting of velocity model m and corresponding waveform data d . This training dataset is 
then used to train a deep neural network to learn the complex nonlinear mapping from d to m , allowing for direct estimation of the 
velocity distribution from the actual observed data on the measured object. This practice can be formulated mathematically as follows:

3. Numerical experiments
3.1. Simulation setup
The Extended Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (EMNIST) dataset comprises handwritten character digits 
derived from the NIST Special Database 19 and converted into a 28 × 28 pixel image format [55] . It serves as a core benchmark in 
machine learning, particularly due to its rich structural variability and visual complexity. Owing to these characteristics, the EMNIST 
dataset has also found widespread application in computational imaging tasks such as electromagnetic backscattering [56] and ul -
trasound imaging [57] , where it is commonly used to evaluate model performance in handling complex scatterers or targets. In this 
study, EMNIST patterns are employed to systematically generate geometrically diverse and topologically complex velocity distribu -
tions. Such a benchmark, characterized by substantial structural variability beyond simple parametric inclusions, enables rigorous 
evaluation of the inversion framework for reconstructing complex scatterers and provides a comprehensive assessment of its quan -
titative inversion capability, robustness, and generalization performance.
We constructed a sound velocity model dataset based on the letters subset of the EMNIST dataset, which includes only handwritten 
samples of 26 uppercase and lowercase letters, excluding numeric digits. To satisfy the spatial resolution requirements of forward 
modeling, each 28 × 28 pixel image was zero-padded and expanded to a resolution of 200 × 200 pixels. These high-resolution binary 
images were then mapped to sound velocity distributions, where pixel intensity values were linearly translated to sound velocities 
ranging from 1500 m/s to 5800 m/s. This mapping allows each pixel to represent local variations in acoustic properties, effectively 
simulating complex internal defect patterns typically encountered in industrial inspection scenarios. A total of 6000 sound velocity 
models were prepared. Among them, 4,500 were utilized for the training of the neural network, 500 were allocated as a validation set, 
and the remaining 1,000 samples were designated for testing purposes. The EMNIST data format guarantees that each sample is 
distinct and independent.
To generate the paired dataset ( m , d ) required for network training, forward wavefield simulations were conducted by solving the 
acoustic wave equation using a staggered-grid finite difference scheme, based on Eq. (1) . The computational domain was discretized 
with second-order temporal and eighth-order spatial accuracy, using a spatial grid step of 0.2 mm to ensure numerical stability and 
J. Lu et al.                                                                                                                                                                                                               Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing 251 (2026) 114179 
7 
precision [50] . The entire computational domain is 40 × 40 mm. Additionally, perfectly matched layers (PML) were implemented at 
the lateral boundaries to eliminate artificial reflections. As illustrated in Fig. 4 , a 64-element linear phased array with an inter-element 
spacing of 0.6 mm was placed along the model surface. Each array element was sequentially activated to emit a Ricker wavelet with a 
center frequency of 2.25 MHz, while all elements simultaneously recorded the resulting wavefield. For each transmission, 1000 time 
samples were collected, resulting in a final data cube of dimensions 64 (transmitters) × 64 (receivers) × 1000 (time steps). This full- 
matrix capture (FMC) dataset contains rich information about the scattered wavefield induced by complex internal structures. The 
time step was set to 2 × 10   8 s, and the total simulation time was 2 × 10   5 s. It is important to note that only longitudinal waves 
were excited and recorded, and potential mode conversions between longitudinal and transverse waves were not considered in this 
study.
3.2. Inversion results
To evaluate the proposed U-FNO network, we used InversionNet as the baseline model. InversionNet employs a fully convolutional 
network (FCN) consisting of an encoder and decoder to perform the inversion process, and has been proven to be an effective seismic

framework in the Python 3.9 environment, and trained using an Adam optimizer under the same parameter configuration as shown in 
Table 2 . The observed data and velocity model were normalized to a range between   1 and 1 during training and testing. All ex -
periments were completed on a computer equipped with an NVIDIA RTX A6000 graphics card (48 GB) and an Intel 4210R central

4.3. Generalization ability
Generalization ability is a critical yet challenging requirement for data-driven full-waveform inversion methods. It provides an 
inverse operator for a sample set rather than a single inversion. In order to validate the network's capacity for generalization in training 
datasets constructed using the EMNIST letters, a digital geometric defect specimen was specifically designed, as illustrated in Fig. 11
(g), (h), and (i), which compare the inversion results of U-FNO and InversionNet, respectively. The findings demonstrate the efficacy of 
the proposed method in accurately predicting the internal structure of the sample and capturing defects, and possess a degree of 
generalization capability in velocity models that extends beyond the confines of the training datasets features. It is imperative to 
emphasize that the proposed method is not merely concerned with the reconstruction of defect geometry, but rather with elucidating 
the intricate nonlinear mapping relationship between FMC and the velocity distribution of the medium. The defect is characterized as a 
velocity anomaly region, and the entire specimen is treated as an inhomogeneous medium in data-driven FWI methods.
5. Discussion and limitations
This study demonstrates that the proposed U-FNO outperforms InversionNet across multiple metrics, including MAE, RMSE, SSIM, 
and L
2 
relative error, on the publicly available EMNIST Letter dataset. While both methods achieve comparable performance on noise- 
free simulation data, InversionNet produces significantly inaccurate inversion results under noisy conditions (SNR = 6 dB). This 
limitation is further amplified when applied to real experimental data, where InversionNet fails to maintain robustness. In contrast, U- 
FNO consistently delivers superior inversion quality across both simulation and experimental scenarios. While the training of the U- 
FNO model entails considerable computational cost (~30  h), the trained model enables highly efficient inference, processing 1,000 
samples in just 49  s, thereby providing a substantial speedup over conventional FWI methods and supporting near real-time imaging in 
FMC-based ultrasonic systems. The enhanced robustness and generalization of U-FNO can be attributed to its dual-path: The trainable 
kernel R, parameterized in Fourier space with truncated high-frequency modes, captures global frequency-domain features, while the 
Unet path restores localized high-frequency details. This synergistic design enables proficient handling of complex data patterns and 
subtle correlations. Furthermore, unlike traditional physics-based FWI, U-FNO eliminates the need for an initial velocity model and 
circumvents cycle-skipping issues. The present work addresses three major challenges in computational wave imaging: accuracy, 
efficiency and robustness, particularly in the context of ill-posed inverse problems with data available only on single-sided boundaries. 
Although the NDT problem serves as the main application scenario in this study, the proposed method is generalizable to other 
computational imaging domains that face similar inverse problems, such as medical ultrasound tomography, radar imaging, and 
seismic exploration.
Despite the considerable progress represented by the current numerical findings and laboratory experiments, a number of inherent 
limitations must be acknowledged.
First, the current modeling configuration is restricted to a velocity range of 1500 – 5800 m/s, with validation primarily conducted on 
water-filled defects embedded in a solid host medium. Although this represents a practically relevant inspection scenario, it does not 
encompass more extreme impedance contrasts, such as air-filled defects, where the higher velocity contrast significantly increases the 
difficulty of quantitative inversion. In such cases, the stronger scattering and mode-conversion effects necessitate more accurate 
modeling, which may require solving the elastic wave equations to account for mode conversion, employing finer spatial discretization 
to capture scattering in low-velocity regions, and additional inversion strategies to mitigate local minima and stabilize convergence, 
inevitably increasing computational cost and further complicating the inversion process. Second, the most critical challenge lies in the 
generalization capacity of the neural network under the supervised learning paradigm. Although the proposed method consistently 
achieves high-fidelity reconstruction within the training distribution, significant deviations in acquisition settings, material properties, 
or structural configurations may degrade performance. Such sensitivity to distribution shifts is inherent to supervised, data-driven 
inversion approaches. Therefore, while robust results are expected within the learned parameter space, extrapolation to substan -
tially different scenarios remains challenging. To mitigate these limitations, several potential strategies have been proposed. First,

enhancing the diversity of the training dataset through mathematical randomization and geometric transformations, such as flipping, 
warping, and cropping of known velocity models may improve generalization. Second, transfer learning can be employed to accelerate

framework guided by the governing wave equations may reduce data dependency [49,59] , although this may compromise the benefits

Currently, this work is limited to 2D inversion using ultrasound phased array techniques. However, the use of idealized 2D wave 
propagation models and point-source transducers restricts its applicability to three-dimensional (3D) imaging scenarios. Extending the 
approach to 3D requires more accurate modeling of wave physics, including the conversion between longitudinal and shear modes and 
the directionality of the transducers to reduce image distortion. Moreover, 3D imaging involves substantially larger observed datasets, 
which impose significant demands on computational resources. Advanced hardware and algorithmic optimizations will therefore be 
essential to support efficient processing and high-quality 3D reconstructions. Recent studies on FNO-based 3D wave propagation 
modeling have demonstrated the potential of neural operators to address high-dimensional inverse problems [60] . These insights will 
guide future work toward a neural operator-based framework for more efficient and scalable ultrasound imaging solutions across 
diverse applications.
6. Conclusion
In conclusion, we developed a U-FNO framework for direct inversion of ultrasonic full waveform data. By learning a nonlinear 
inverse mapping from FMC data to the L-wave velocity field, the framework removes the need for repeated forward wave simulations 
during each inversion and eliminates the reliance on having an initial velocity model and effectively circumvents cycle-skipping issues. 
At the inference stage, it is far more efficient than classical FWI. Benefiting from this efficient inference mechanism, the trained U-FNO 
model supports near real-time imaging in FMC-based ultrasonic systems. Unlike convolutional neural networks constrained by local 
receptive fields, U-FNO leverages Fourier-space kernel parameterization to capture global wave-physics features, thereby enabling 
high-fidelity reconstruction of complex geometric defects. Numerical simulations and experimental validations demonstrate that U- 
FNO consistently outperforms ray-based TFM and FCN-based InversionNet in terms of accuracy and robustness. Future work will focus 
on enhancing the generalization capability of the U-FNO framework, aiming to expand its applicability to a broader range of wave- 
based imaging scenarios.
