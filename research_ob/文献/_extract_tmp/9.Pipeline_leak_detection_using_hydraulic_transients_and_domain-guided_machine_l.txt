A B S T R A C T
In this paper, we present a novel domain-guided framework for training machine learning (ML)
models, particularly neural networks (NN), to detect multiple leaks in pipelines using hydraulic
transient datasets. Central to our approach is the utilization of a ‘leak function’ as the target
variable in the ML model, where leak properties exhibit a pulse-like signature. This framework
allows ML models to be trained without the typical constraints on the number of leaks and
avoids the necessity of retraining separate models for different scenarios. To enhance model ro-
bustness against operational uncertainties, we employ iterative refinement techniques. Starting
with a baseline NN model, each subsequent refinement incorporates factors such as valve closure
time, pre-transient flow properties, and the capacity to handle an increased number of leaks. We
validate the accuracy of our framework through numerical simulations and experimental data
from a laboratory-scale Viscoelastic pipe system. Furthermore, we demonstrate the framework’s
applicability to pipeline networks in two configurations: a series connection of two pipes
and a tree-type network comprising three pipes. Our results indicate satisfactory detection
accuracy and system adaptability, showcasing the potential of our framework for practical ML
applications in leak detection.
1. Introduction
Pressurized pipelines are vital for water distribution to cities and industries, yet they are vulnerable to leaks and other faults
(e.g., partial blockages, and wall deterioration). A leaking pipeline does not merely represent a physical fault in the system; it signifies
a contributor to non-revenue water (NRW) losses, waste of energy, and a burden on financial resources. Globally, NRW accounts
for approximately 346 million cubic meters per day [ 1]. A significant portion of this volume is attributed to leaks, reinforcing
the urgency for effective solutions. Thus, the development of robust leak (fault) detection methods has emerged as an active and
pressing area of research. The impetus for such advancements is twofold: on one hand, it addresses the immediate challenges faced
by water agencies tasked with the management and maintenance of these pipelines; on the other, it presents an opportunity for
broader economic benefits.

Current commercial methodologies for pipeline leak (fault) detection, including noise loggers [2], listening rods [3], ground
penetrating radar [4], the SAHARA system [5], and smart-ball [6], often face challenges with large-scale pipelines. These methods
tend to be disruptive and intrusive, necessitating significant operational and financial expenditure for water supply agencies. From
a management viewpoint, these methods are usually carried out by the supplier’s technicians, which can lead to waiting times and
delays. Moreover, the majority of these techniques are passive; they depend on the manifestations of existing leaks, such as noise
or flow disturbances, to signal their presence [7]. This approach inherently limits their effectiveness, as they react to symptoms of
leaks rather than actively detecting them. Consequently, despite the availability of these technologies, the persistent issue of NRW
losses indicates a gap in the ability of current methods to detect and mitigate leaks efficiently.
Transient-based leak (fault) detection methods [8] are receiving immense attention as a viable strategy for identifying leaks
and other faults. Leveraging mechanically induced hydraulic transient waves, which travel at the speed of sound – approximately
1000 m/s in metallic pipes and 400 m/s in non-metallic pipes [9] – this approach provides a non-disruptive and non-intrusive
alternative suitable for large-scale pipelines. As these waves travel along the pipeline, they reflect off physical discontinuities,
including leaks, sending back signals that carry distinctive information about these features. Precisely, leaks or junctions for side-
branches generate a negative pressure wave, while a partially closed valve introduces a positive pressure wave. The characteristic
pattern of an extended partial blockage is a positive wave followed by a negative one, forming a ‘positive bell’ profile. Conversely,
partial wall deterioration is indicated by a negative wave succeeded by a positive one, creating a ‘negative bell’ pattern. The time-
of-arrival and magnitude of these reflected pressure waves are associated respectively with the location and size of discontinuity.
Therefore, by analyzing the measured transient responses, it becomes possible to decode these signals, pinpointing the presence,
location, and size of leaks and other faults with precision. In addition, the short duration and simplicity of the transient tests give
the water system technicians complete autonomy and guarantee minimum costs. However, the task is not straightforward as the
measured signals are inevitably contaminated by environmental noise [10–14].
To deal with noise, the literature has traditionally focused on three primary approaches: model-based, data-driven (ML), and
hybrid approaches. Model-based methods rely on physics-driven and inversion-based techniques [15], including inverse transient
analysis [10,16–19], time-domain reflectometry [20], spectral-based techniques [21–28], and time-reversal methods [13,29,30].
Most of these methods have undergone rigorous testing predominantly in controlled laboratory environments with few attempts in
the real pipe systems [31–35].
While the research community continues to extend and enhance this modeling and inversion-based approaches, there is a parallel
and growing interest in utilizing data-driven machine learning (ML) for the same task. For instance, in 2019, Zhou et al. [36]
utilized resonant frequency responses to train a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) using a novel fusion-enhanced stochastic
optimization algorithm combined with a pooling network strategy. Their study successfully detected single leaks in a simple single
pipeline system, even in the presence of noise with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) as low as 0 dB. The approach was validated through
numerical experiments. In the following year, Bohorquez et al. [37] used time-domain pressure head signals to train both an Artificial
Neural Network (ANN) and a 1-D Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN) in a noise-free environment, aiming for a single leak
detection and recognizing sudden area changes in a single pipeline. This method was only validated numerically. In 2021, Bohorquez
et al. [38] presented another paper wherein the authors trained ANNs using burst-induced transient signals for the detection and
localization of single burst occurrences in a single pipeline system. Their method was validated using both numerical simulations
and laboratory experiments. In the same year, Liao et al. [39] adopted resonant frequency responses to train a multi-layer dense-net
(ANN) algorithm, which could detect a single leak in large-scale pipeline networks, with validation conducted through numerical
dataset. Recently, in 2022, Ayati et al. [40] classified leaks in pipeline networks by employing resonant frequency responses and
Neighborhood Component Analysis (NCA). They also focused on single leak detection and validated their approach both numerically
and in laboratory experiments.
In recent years, hybrid approaches, which combine physics-based models with data-driven techniques, have also shown promising
results. In 2022, Waqar et al. [41] harnessed the capabilities of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), integrating them for
the first time with the principles of hydraulic transients in pipeline systems. The model was informed by the 1-D wave equation
to predict the transient-wave propagation in a single pipeline. The study conducted a detailed sensitivity analysis regarding the
architecture of the neural network and the nature of the input training data, underpinned by numerical validation. In parallel, Ye
et al. [42] also employed a PINN, informed by the water-hammer equations with frictional and visco-elastic damping considerations.
In addition to numerical exploration, the authors validated their findings through laboratory experiments, providing the efficacy of
their findings.
These studies collectively underscore the progressive role of ML in enhancing the precision and reliability of leak detection
in pipeline systems, showcasing a blend of diverse ML algorithms with remarkable performances. However, it is found that the
majority of research efforts have concentrated on detecting a single leak in a given pipeline. Contrarily, the possibility of multiple
simultaneous leaks within a pipeline system cannot be overlooked, especially considering that the occurrence of one leak may
compromise the structural integrity of the system, thereby increasing the likelihood of subsequent breaches [43]. To address these
limitations, this paper makes the following key contributions:
1. We propose a novel domain-guided machine learning-based framework specifically designed for the simultaneous detection
and localization of multiple leaks in pressurized pipelines, overcoming the limitations of existing methods that primarily focus
on detecting single leaks.
2. The framework incorporates modeling uncertainties, such as valve closure time, pre-transient pressure head, and flow rate
variations, through a model refinement approach—an aspect not extensively addressed in previous studies.

2. Problem statement
In practical applications, water pipeline systems are often subjected to significant levels of noise, originating from a variety
of sources, including flow turbulence, mechanical vibrations induced by pumps and flow regulation devices, and fluctuations in
consumer usage and demand [29,44]. Additionally, the environmental context of the pipelines – whether they are above ground
or buried, and their proximity to highways, railway tracks, or other infrastructure – further contributes to noise characteristics. As
such, the measured transient signals are inevitably tainted with noise. Let ℎ𝑚(𝑡, 𝑠𝐿, 𝑥𝐿, 𝑛𝐿) be the measured transient response of the

Traditionally, retrieving defect information from noisy measurements is approached as an inverse problem. This process involves

noise in the measured signals. As such, the accuracy and reliability of the solutions derived from this model are heavily dependent
on the algorithm employed [10,45–47]. In many cases, the presence of noise can lead to ambiguous or multiple solutions, resulting
in false alarms [10,12].
In the literature, the use of a machine learning algorithm (e.g., ANN, CNN or NCA, as described in the previous section)
as a surrogate inverse model is proposed to map noisy measurements to specific defect information. The underlying concept is
mathematically represented as follows:

measurements ℎ𝑚(𝑡, 𝑥𝐿, 𝑠𝐿). The current approach to solve Eq. (5) presents distinct challenges such as:
1. In the current framework, where leak parameters are directly estimated as outputs, an ML model trained for a single leak is
predisposed to detect only one leak, whereas a model trained for two leaks will necessarily identify exactly two, regardless
of the actual conditions. This reveals a limitation of the existing framework’s adaptability to varying numbers of actual leaks.
Such a limitation originates from the ML model’s architecture, designed to detect only a specific number of leaks, 𝑛𝐿. As a
result, the model inherently biases its predictions towards the number of leaks for which it was initially trained. In practical
scenarios, where the actual number of leaks is undetermined, this bias leads to inaccurate predictions.
2. Adjusting the model to predict an alternate number of leaks would require changing the dimension of the output vector within
the ML architecture. However, the architecture imposes strict limitations on the dimensions of the output vector, making such
adjustments impractical. This inflexibility hinders the ability to fine-tune models for accurately detecting multiple leaks after
their initial training.

1. The ML model predicts an output function that indirectly represents leak properties, allowing for training across different
numbers of leaks. This flexibility mitigates the issue of discrepancies between the number of leaks in training and actual
scenarios, as long as the actual number of leaks is fewer than or equal to what the model was trained on. This limitation is
not inherent to the proposed framework but stems from a well-known constraint in ML models: their inability to generalize
beyond the scope of their training data.
2. The number of elements in the leak function, denoted as ̂ℒ, is determined by the discretization of the variable 𝑥, rather than
by the number of leaks. This design feature allows the model to adapt without the need to develop separate models for each
leak scenario. Moreover, consistent output size simplifies fine-tuning, enhances scalability, and improves efficiency, enabling
the model to handle varying datasets and uncertainties more effectively.
3. In addition, since the proposed method utilizes the full waveform, its performance is not hindered by blind spots – such as
zero-pressure points in resonant modes – where leaks may go undetected [48,49]. As such, given that the model is trained
on a comprehensive range of potential leak locations, it enables the detection of leak signatures across all areas, ensuring
robust performance even in scenarios where traditional spectral-based methods face limitations [49].
3.1. Methodology
3.1.1. General
The methodology flowchart is outlined in Fig. 2 and is divided into three main blocks, each of which is detailed in subsequent
sections.
3.1.2. Block 1: Development of the hydraulic twin model
The initial phase in this block involves selecting a pipeline system and designing experiments. This includes identifying access
locations, understanding pipeline geometry, and defining operational conditions such as flow rates, pressure heads, consumer
demands, locations for transient generation, and sensor installation. The next step is to develop and calibrate a numerical model—a
virtual and hydraulic transient twin of the real system.
To calibrate the hydraulic model using the experimental dataset, if data is available, model parameters such as wave speed,
damping factors, excited waveforms, bandwidth, and amplitude are iteratively tuned. This tuning process involves adjusting these
parameters based on the comparison between simulated pressure head signals and the measurements recorded from sensors installed
in the real pipeline system. The adjustments continue until the simulated results closely replicate the observed data, ensuring the
numerical model accurately captures the dynamics of the physical system. In cases where experimental data is not available, educated
estimates and engineering expertise are employed to tune the model based on known pipeline characteristics, such as material,
diameter, and layout, as well as historical performance data. This approach provides a reliable baseline, ensuring the model reflects
the expected behavior of the system.

3.1.3. Block 2: Data generation for ML model training
Data generation for training the ML model is executed using the calibrated hydraulic transient twin model. This process involves
simulating transient responses for varying numbers and sizes of leaks. The number of samples per leak number depends on the
computational complexity and the hydraulic engineer’s discretion. We recommend starting with simulations for small SNR values,
as smaller leaks are more challenging to detect. Leak locations should vary between 𝜆 and 𝐿 − 𝜆, where 𝐿 is the pipe length and 𝜆
is the wavelength of the injected transient signal. For post-simulation, the defect information (leak size, location, and number) for
each sample is transformed into a leak function, as described in Eq. (6). This forms the basis of our proposed framework, where the
ML model maps the measured transient response to the corresponding leak function, which could have 1, 2, or 𝑛𝐿 peaks.

3.1.4. Block 3: Development of the machine learning model
In this final block, a regression-based ML model is developed. The input to this model is the measured pressure head
(ℎ𝑚(𝑡, 𝑥𝐿, 𝑠𝐿, 𝑛𝐿)), while the output is the leak function ( ℒ (𝑥𝐿, 𝑠𝐿, 𝑛𝐿)). Our paper utilizes an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) as
a surrogate model for this mapping, although the process is applicable to other models. The simulated data is split into training,
validation, and testing segments. For testing purposes, a new dataset can be generated from the calibrated hydraulic model.
Moreover, the training dataset with different number of leaks can be simulated and used simultaneously for ML model training.
3.1.5. Performance indicators
To evaluate the effectiveness of the regression model, several parameters including Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean
Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), 𝑅2 (R-square), and Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) are utilized [50].
Ideally, MSE, RMSE, and MAE are error metrics that should be minimized; thus, lower values directly correlate to better model
performance. There are no upper bounds for these three indicators; they can range from 0 to infinity, where 0 represents a perfect
fit to the data. On the other hand, PCC and 𝑅2 are bounded metrics where higher values indicate better performance. The PCC has
a range from −1 to 1, with 1 being a perfect positive linear correlation, 0 indicating no correlation, and −1 signifying a perfect
negative linear correlation. The 𝑅2 metric is bounded between 0 and 1, where 1 indicates that the model explains all the variability
of the target variable around its mean, and 0 implies that the model fails to capture any of the variance of the target variable.
4. Numerical analysis
4.1. Training data set
Consider a reservoir-pipeline-valve system comprising a 500 m long pipe with an internal diameter of 150 mm and a wall
thickness of 5 mm. The pipeline is modeled as an elastic structure characterized by a Young’s modulus of 170 GPa and a Darcy-
Weisbach friction factor of 0.015. Consequently, the transient wave speed, denoted as 𝑎0, is 1.25 km/s. The system operates with a
pre-transient flow rate and pressure head of 2 LPS and 40 m at the downstream valve, respectively. This setup results in a Joukowsky
pressure head (𝐻𝐽 ) of approximately 14.4 m above the steady-state pressure head.
For the simulation, we consider 3 wave periods, equating to a total time of 𝑇 = 12 × 𝐿∕𝑎0 = 4.796 seconds. A sampling frequency
of 𝑓𝑠 = 250 Hz is utilized, yielding 1200 data points for each transient signal. The spatial resolution of the simulation is 𝛥𝑥 = 4.9783
m, allowing for a minimum distance of 𝛥𝑥 between two potential leaks. The transient wave is initiated by a valve maneuver with
a time of 𝑇𝑐 = 0.03 s, resulting in a minimum wavelength 𝜆min = 𝑎0 × 𝑇𝑐 = 37.3 m for the injected transient wave. Consequently, the
diffraction limit [51,52] is approximately 𝜆min∕2 = 18 .67 m. The transient signals recorded at the valve location are utilized for the
ML model development.

4.1.1. Dataset pre-processing
Data preprocessing is a critical aspect of our framework. The transformation of pressure head signals, which serve as the input
to the ML model, is achieved by converting step-responses into pulse responses. This conversion involves subtracting a delayed copy
of the original signals, a method outlined in [53]. Additionally, the leak properties, constituting the output of the ML model, are
represented through a leak function as defined in Eq. (6). Illustrative examples of these transformations are presented in Figs. 4 and
5. Fig. 4 displays the conversion of signals from step to pulse responses, while Fig. 5 depicts the leak function for different leak
scenarios (one, two, and three leaks in the top, middle, and bottom rows, respectively). In the latter plots, the horizontal axes are
normalized against the pipe length, and the vertical axes are normalized in relation to the pipe’s cross-sectional area.
The rationale behind transforming the signals into pulse responses is that pulses reflecting from leaks correlate more strongly
with their corresponding leak function than step-formatted reflections. This hypothesis was substantiated through the training of
the optimized neural network. It was observed that using pulse-type input signals yielded higher accuracy compared to step-like
signals. For brevity, the detailed analysis supporting this observation is not included here.

5. Experimental validation
5.1. System description
The test rig is located at the Water Engineering Laboratory of the University of Perugia, Italy. The schematic of the experimental
arrangement is delineated in Fig. 13. The configuration comprises an air vessel situated upstream, a horizontal pipeline made of High-
Density Polyethylene (HDPE), and a pneumatic valve positioned at the downstream terminus. A submerged pump, placed within the
recycling tank, ensures the stabilization of the pressure head within the air vessel. The pipeline’s specifications include a length ( 𝐿)
of 188.89 m, an internal diameter (𝐷) of 0.0933 m, a Darcy-Weisbach friction factor (𝑓) calibrated at 0.0196, and an elastic modulus
(𝐸) of the pipe material registered at 1.47 GPa. The Viscoelastic (VE) characteristics of the pipe walls are incorporated via five Kelvin-
Voigt model elements. The calibrated creep coefficients are delineated as ⃗𝐽 = [0.0657, 0.1054, 0.0091, 0.0261, 0.0746] × 10−9 Pa−1. The
corresponding retardation times are ⃗ 𝜏= [0.05, 0.5, 1.5, 5, 10] s. To facilitate leak simulations, two steel plates, each encompassing a
hole, are installed at locations 𝑥𝐿1 = 83.19 m and 𝑥𝐿2 = 110.53 m along the pipeline.
Owing to spatial limitations within the laboratory, the pipeline assumes a helical configuration, integrating both straight
and curved sections, each curved segment approximating an angular displacement of 180 degrees and a radius of curvature of
approximately 1.5 m. The pipeline is predominantly suspended approximately 1 foot above ground level, barring a few meters
adjacent to the upstream end. This elevation is supported by steel saddle clamps, which are affixed to a U-shaped hang-rail embedded
within the ground.
5.1.1. Transient generation and data acquisition
Transients are generated by rapidly and completely closing the initially open valve (in about 35 ms) located at the downstream
end. A piezoresistive transducer with a full scale of 0.4 MPa (2–4 bar) is placed at 𝑥𝑚 = 187 .66 m to measure the pressure head
response of the system. National Instrument cDAQ-9188 data acquisition system with a sampling frequency of 2048 Hz is used to
record the data.

model is applied to the entire dataset (18,000 samples) to ensure that NN weights are adequately adjusted without overlooking
earlier subsets. Care is taken to meticulously maintain sample indexing (for training, validation, and testing) during the model
development to prevent data leakage. The final training stage required approximately 3 h, culminating in a total training time of

6.3.1. System description
Consider a tree-type pipe network as shown in Fig. 16(b). The lengths of the pipes are 𝐿1 = 250 m, 𝐿2 = 300 m, and 𝐿3 = 250
m, with diameters of 150 mm, 140 mm, and 160 mm, respectively. The wall thicknesses are 5 mm for pipe 1, 4 mm for pipe 2, and
6 mm for pipe 3. All pipes have a modulus of elasticity of 170 GPa, resulting in wave speeds of 1244 m/s for pipe 1, 1218 m/s for
pipe 2, and 1234 m/s for pipe 3. The valve at the downstream end of pipe 3 is maintaining a steady-state flow rate of 2 LPS at a
pressure head of 40 m. This valve is used for transient generation.
6.3.2. Data generation and NN model development
The sampling frequency is set at 250 Hz. The total simulation time is defined as 𝑇 = 3 × 4(𝐿1∕𝑎1 + 𝐿2∕𝑎2), considering 𝐿2 > 𝐿 3.
Various scenarios are simulated, including a single leak in each of pipes 1, 2, and 3, as well as combinations of leaks across the
three pipes, and for two and three leaks simultaneously. In total, 24,000 samples (2000 per scenario) are simulated and compiled
into a dataset. The measurement station is located at the downstream end of pipe 3. The simulated pressure signals are not provided
here for the sake of brevity.
An optimized ANN model with a structure of [400, 400, 300, 300, 300] is employed. All other hyperparameters are consistent
with those described in Table 1. The dataset is pre-processed as outlined in Section 4.1.1. For training, the dataset is divided into
four subsets. The model, initially trained on subset 1, undergoes fine-tuning with subset 2, 3, and 4. Following that, the refined
model undergoes a final training phase over the entire dataset. The training, validation, and testing split is kept at a 70:20:10 ratio.
The entire model development process takes approximately 9.5 h. The subsequent section presents the model’s performance and
results.
6.3.3. Results
Fig. 18 provides a comparative assessment of the actual versus predicted leak properties by the ANN model for a tree-type pipe
system. Each row corresponds to a distinct dataset phase: training at the top, followed by validation, and testing at the bottom.
The columns represent three randomly selected examples, illustrating the model’s accuracy. It is evident that the ANN maintains
high precision in leak localization across all dataset phases. However, there are noticeable discrepancies in leak size estimation,
especially for adjacent leaks. Spurious minor lobes are also detected with minimal height in the training phase, and moderate height
in validation and testing, possibly indicating the need for denser model with more number of layers/neurons. These findings, in
conjunction with the regression analysis shown in Fig. A.22 (refer to Appendix), suggest that the proposed ANN framework is capable
and adaptable to complex pipeline systems. The presence of closely spaced peaks indicates that the ANN is sufficiently sensitive to
distinguish between proximate leak events, which is essential in dense pipeline networks. Nevertheless, as the complexity of the
system increases, there is an evident requirement for an ANN with a higher capacity – potentially more layers and neurons – to
effectively model the intricate relationships within the data without compromising the accuracy.
7. Conclusions
A novel domain-guided machine learning-based framework for the localization and sizing of multiple leaks in pressurized
pipelines is introduced. A comprehensive numerical dataset, generated using a time-domain hydraulic transient model with the
method of characteristics, is utilized to train a feedforward neural network (a variant of the Artificial Neural Network; ANN). This
network, featuring a five-layer architecture with 400, 400, 300, 300, and 200 neurons, processes pressure head signals corrupted with
Gaussian noise for leak detection. Systematic evaluation of numerical examples demonstrates the ability of the model to accurately
predict single and multiple leaks (up to four), amidst modeling uncertainties such as valve time closure, pre-transient pressure
head, and flow rate variations. The dataset, consisting of 2000 samples per scenario, is divided into training, validation, and testing
subsets in a 70-20-10 split, ensuring the model’s robustness and reliability. Experimental validation is performed using data from a
Viscoelastic pipe system. The trained ML model, further refined to adapt to various uncertainties, accurately identifies leaks in all test
cases, with minor errors in sizing due to modeling errors in the hydraulic transient solver. This validation underscores the model’s
accuracy and potential for real-world application. The initial training of the baseline model for both numerical and experimental
data sets is completed in approximately 2.5 h, with each subsequent refinement requiring an additional hour, indicating the method’s
efficiency. The computational speed of the model could be enhanced through the use of GPUs.
The proposed framework has also been extended to handle complex pipeline configurations, including systems with two pipes
connected in series and tree-type networks with three pipes. This extension involved generating datasets using the hydraulic transient
model, optimizing the neural network (NN) model architecture, and strategically training the NN model with small datasets instead
of using the entire dataset at once. The framework has shown adaptability to complex systems with moderately accurate results.
However, challenges were noted: as system complexity increases, the ANN model’s predictions tend to deviate from the expected
output. This issue might be addressed by increasing the number of layers and neurons. However, this approach requires careful
tuning of the neural network’s architecture and the selection of optimal hyperparameters, a process that can be both time-consuming
and intricate. Future efforts will focus on exploring more complex pipeline systems and incorporating real-field data, employing
diverse or hybrid Machine Learning models to more effectively tackle these challenges.
