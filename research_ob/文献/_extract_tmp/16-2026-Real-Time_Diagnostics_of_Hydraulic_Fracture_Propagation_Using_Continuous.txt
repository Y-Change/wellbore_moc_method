Summary
Hydraulic fracturing diagnostics are essential for the efficient stimulation of unconventional reservoirs. Treating pressure is an accessible 
field signal and provides a low- cost and nonintrusive basis for characterizing hydraulic fracture propagation. However, conventional time- 
domain analysis often fails to capture transient fluctuations associated with fracture propagation because of the interference from wellbore 
resonance, operational adjustments, and background noise. More valuable pressure fluctuation distributes across the various frequency 
components. In this paper, we propose a real- time diagnostic approach using the continuous wavelet transform (CWT) to extract rate- 
corrected transient pressure fluctuations intrinsic from the stress release and accumulation of hydraulic fractures propagation. It enables 
the dynamic classification of fracture propagation patterns, including primary fracture growth, complex fracture development, discontinu-
ities intersection, and fracture interruption. CWT in particular is used to decompose the treating pressure signal, and the fracture- induced 
fluctuations are characterized as broadband impulsive responses in the time- frequency energy spectrum. Wavelet coherence analysis 
between pressure and slurry rate is introduced to identify time- frequency regions minimally disturbed by variable rates. Results indicated 
that the 0.3–0.5- Hz frequency components are identified as an effective diagnostic band for data sampling of 1 Hz. Pressure fluctuation 
energy in the 0.4–0.5- Hz sub- band serves as a surrogate for instantaneous stress release of hydraulic fracture propagation and reflects 
the efficiency with which the hydraulic energy is converted into new fracture surfaces. The pressure fluctuation attenuation coefficient 
is defined from the pressure energy distribution contrast between high- and low- frequency components. Increased hydraulic- energy dis-
sipation along the fracture path and hindered stress reaccumulation produce frequency variation within the pressure fluctuation period, 
leading to a stronger transfer of pressure energy toward the 0.3–0.4- Hz sub- band. Based on these two quantified features, rule- based fuzzy 
inference is then used to classify the fracture propagation patterns into four categories on a 5- second window basis. Supporting evidence 
for the proposed method was provided by independent microseismic and distributed fiber- optic measurements. The fracturing stages 
dominated by complex fracture development are further supported by higher interstage tracer production. In addition, locally prolonged 
elevated energy near 0.35–0.4 Hz in the pressure time- frequency spectrum is identified as a potential indicator of enhanced fracture 
complexity associated with large- scale discontinuities activation and fracture branching. The proposed technique provides an automated, 
quantitative connection between treating pressure fluctuations and fracture propagation dynamics, offering a practical real- time alterna-
tive for the evaluation of hydraulic fracturing performance.
Introduction
Hydraulic fracturing diagnostics are essential for the efficient stimulation of unconventional reservoirs. Understanding fracture propaga-
tion behavior is critical for evaluating treatment performance and guiding real- time optimization of pumping parameters. As the routinely 
recorded field signal, treating pressure provides an economical and nonintrusive solution for diagnosing fracture propagation dynamics. 
It reflects the integrated response to treatment strategy, resonances in the fluid column from wellbore or fracture deformation, propagation 
of fluid connected fractures, and background noise (MacKay et al. 2018; Wei et al. 2025). Accurate characterization of pressure evolution 
associated with distinct fracture propagation patterns is fundamental to pressure- based diagnostics.
Nolte and Smith (1981) pioneered pressure- based diagnostics through log- log interpretation of net pressure vs. time. Subsequent stud-
ies extended this framework to pressure- trend classification (stable, fluctuation, rise, and drop) for identifying fracture propagation pat-
terns such as normal propagation, fracture- height growth, and proppant screenout (Chen et al. 2020
). However, the Nolte- Smith method 
generally relies on assumptions including continuous fracture propagation, fixed pumping rate, or prior knowledge of closure pressure, 
which may not hold during large- scale volume fracturing. Besides, compression of pressure data imposed by the log- log plot makes real- 
time interpretation hard. The Nolte- Smith framework was further extended by introducing intermittent fracture propagation with alternat-
ing growth and ballooning periods, which led to the moving reference point (MRP) method for real- time diagnosis of fracturing events 
(Soliman et al. 2014; Pirayesh et al. 2015). Although MRP does not require prior knowledge of closure pressure and presents results in 
Cartesian coordinates, it still depends on a relatively complex mathematical formulation for determining the moving reference exponent. 
Numerical simulations based on Biot’s theory indicate that hydraulic fracture propagation is inherently intermittent. Fracture opening 
releases stress and causes a rapid pressure drop, while subsequent fluid filling drives stress reaccumulation and pressure recovery until the 
next rupture ( Tzschichholz and Herrmann 1995 ; Milanese et al. 2016; Cao et al. 2018). As  a result, fracture growth leaves transient

true- triaxial hydraulic fracturing tests. Bian et al. (2016) related fluctuation frequency and amplitude on the treatment curve to fracture 
complexity and far- field fracture geometry. Abdelaziz and Grasselli (2024) observed the difference in pressure fluctuation between tensile 
and shear fractures, which provided key physical evidence for the characteristics of pressure fluctuation. Tensile fracture exhibits a sharp 
pressure drop with quick recovery, while shear fracture shows a slower pressure recovery. These observations indicate that pressure fluc-
tuations are informative indicators of complex fracture evolution. Owing to formation heterogeneity and varying fracture types, such 
fluctuations appear not as continuous water- hammer oscillations, but as frequency- varying transient responses.
Because fracture- induced pressure fluctuations are nonstationary and frequency- varying, time- domain analysis alone is often insuffi-
cient. Wavelet transform provides localized resolution in both time and frequency, enabling effective capture of abrupt singularities with 
less temporal smearing than Fourier- based global transforms (Mallat and Hwang 2002; Daubechies 1990; Addison 2005). It is therefore 
well suited for preserving rupture- related energy bursts and identifying frequency transitions in fracturing pressure signals. Accordingly, 
wavelet transform has recently been applied to pressure- based fracture diagnostics without requiring simplifying assumptions or sophis-
ticated models (Li et al. 2022a; Sheng et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2025). Wavelet transform mainly includes discrete wavelet transform (DWT) 
and CWT. DWT has been used in pumping pressure and diagnostic fracture injection test (DFIT) analysis to identify events such as lea-
koff, ballooning, and fracture closure, and to reduce rate- related interference through pressure- rate wavelet- energy comparison (Unal 
et al. 2018, 2019b; Hu et al. 2022). While DWT offers efficient computation, its fixed decomposition scales and substantial downsampling 
inherently limit frequency resolution in high- frequency components. By contrast, CWT provides superior frequency resolution through 
continuous decomposition scales, making it better suited for capturing the evolution of transient responses in nonstationary pressure 
signals (Arts and van den Broek 2022). Although the computational cost of CWT is more intensive, CWT maintains acceptable processing 
efficiency for field data with one- second interval while preserving high- frequency components resolution. Gabry et al. (2023a, 2023b) 
applied CWT to DFIT analysis and identified fracture closure from minimum stabilized signal energy during pressure falloff, with support 
from field- scale strain measurements. Wei et al. (2024) applied CWT to post- fracturing pressure falloff data to identify the nearly pure 
leakoff and invert fracture area, and the CWT- derived estimates correlated more strongly with stage production. Gabry et al. (2024a, 
2024b) also generated normalized CWT scalograms of pumping pressure to interpret 3D fracture- propagation modes in comparison with 
microseismic observations. In addition, Zhang et al. (2025) applied CWT- based time- frequency features of treating pressure to identify 
multifracture propagation modes and evaluate diverting effectiveness, capturing the transition from frequent pressure fluctuations under 
fracture competition to gradual stabilization as primary fractures became established.
Despite these advances, previous studies mainly focused on the propagation and closure behavior of primary hydraulic fractures, with 
limited attention to interactions between hydraulic fractures and discontinuities such as natural fractures, bedding planes, and interlayers. 
Fundamentally, fracture propagation emerges from competition between tensile and shear failure mechanisms under hydraulic loading. 
Primary hydraulic fractures are typically tensile- dominated, whereas interaction with pre- existing discontinuities promotes shear- 
dominated failure and complex fracture- network development (Warpinski and Teufel 1987; Wu et al. 2022; Li et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2026). 
These competing mechanisms also affect transient pressure fluctuations: Rapid pressure recovery indicates relatively simple fracture 
paths with efficient stress redistribution and limited hydraulic- energy dissipation, whereas slower recovery reflects increased dissipation 
and hindered stress reaccumulation associated with more tortuous propagation and complex discontinuity interaction. Therefore, estab-
lishing a quantitative link between fracture- propagation stress states and CWT signatures of transient pressure fluctuations is essential for 
expanding the diagnostics of fracture- discontinuity interaction.
To address the above issues, we develop a real- time diagnostic method that links transient pressure fluctuations to stress release and 
accumulation under different interaction scenarios between hydraulic fractures and natural discontinuities. Starting from routinely 
acquired treating pressure under standard 1- Hz sampling, rate- decoupled transient fluctuations are extracted in the time- frequency domain 
using CWT. Two key features are defined to quantify the transient pressure fluctuations intrinsic from the stress release and accumulation 
of hydraulic fractures propagation and are further input into a fuzzy inference system for dynamic classification of fracture propagation 
patterns (Fig. 1). The fracture- related physical processes underlying the proposed method are supported by independent field measure -
ments, including microseismic monitoring, distributed fiber- optic observations, and stage- level tracer production data. Overall, it would 
provide a low- cost, noninvasive tool for evaluating fracture propagation events and complexity during hydraulic fracturing.

Similarly, the global threshold baseline of the pressure fluctuation attenuation coefficient across different lithologies and formations can 
be determined as the minimum level of statistical distribution, consistent with its order of 25% quantile. Pressure fluctuation attenuation 
is defined as a quantitative proxy for the degree of hydraulic- energy dissipation along the fracture path within the wellbore/formation 
system. When the energy input is consumed by additional dissipation after the creation of new fracture surfaces, stress reaccumulation 
becomes slower, resulting in increased attenuation. Such dissipation is commonly associated with the influence of discontinuities, such as 
natural fractures, bedding planes, and interlayers.
Based on the two quantitative features, transient pressure fluctuations can be classified into four representative behaviors that interpret 
the tendency of hydraulic energy allocation within the wellbore/formation system and correspond to four common fracture propagation 
patterns (Fig. 3). (i) Primary fracture growth: Most hydraulic energy is efficiently converted into new fracture surfaces, with limited 
additional dissipation. Fractures propagate actively through the intact matrix under tensile opening, resulting in high pressure fluctuation 
energy and low attenuation. (ii) Complex fracture development: Hydraulic energy is partly converted into new fracture surfaces, but 
additional dissipation increases as fractures deviate along weak discontinuities, generating branches and tortuous paths. In this case, the 
competition of tensile and shear failure leads to elevated energy and attenuation. (iii) Discontinuities intersection: Hydraulic energy is 
preferentially captured by pre- existing discontinuities, making new fracture- surface creation difficult. Increased shear failure and hydrau-
lic energy dissipation retard stress release and reaccumulation, leading to reduced energy and higher attenuation. (iv) Fracture interrup -
tion: Hydraulic energy is further dissipated within nonfracture spaces of the wellbore/formation system, hindering hydraulic fracture 
propagation. Under increased leakoff and limited fracture volume, stress variations are too weak to sustain transient pressure fluctuations, 
so both energy and attenuation remain at low levels. These categories reflect hydraulic- energy allocation within the wellbore/formation 
system rather than unique fracture geometries. Accordingly, the represented scenarios are broader than the schematic interactions in 
Fig. 3: Fracture branching, redirection, and larger- scale fracture network may all be categorized as “Complex fracture development,” 
whereas nongeological dissipation, such as interstage communication, may also be interpreted as “Discontinuities intersection.”
Fuzzy Inference System of Fracture Propagation Patterns. With the two pressure- fluctuation features quantified, the remaining 
task is to translate these continuous metrics into interpretable fracture propagation patterns. Because field signals are noisy and the 
boundaries between fracture behaviors are not strictly separable, a soft- decision framework is preferred over hard thresholds. Fuzzy logic 
accommodates such uncertainty by representing inputs with membership functions and linguistic if- then rules. In this study, a Mamdani- 
type fuzzy inference system (Mamdani and Assilian 1975) is adopted for its transparency and ease of incorporating expert knowledge. 
The workflow includes fuzzification, rule- based inference, and defuzzification to obtain the final fracture- pattern label.

Diagnostic Sub-Band Selection and Feature Quantification. Using wavelet coherence analysis, the 0.3–0.5- Hz range is identified 
as the diagnostic band for fracture- induced transient pressure fluctuations. To further define physically meaningful sub- bands within this 
range, reported 3D finite- discrete element method simulation results are analyzed (Cheng et al. 2026). Spearman rank correlation (de 
Winter et al. 2016) between sub- band pressure energy within 0–0.5 Hz and incremental fracture volume increases markedly above 0.3 Hz 
(Fig. 7b). This indicates reduced low- frequency trend interference and stronger temporal localization of broadband impulsive responses, 
making the 0.3–0.5- Hz band more representative of fracture propagation. Together with true- triaxial experimental observations reported 
by Abdelaziz and Grasselli (2024), these results further support that transient pressure fluctuations reflect the inherently unstable stress 
state of fracture propagation and typically exhibit asymmetric drop- recovery half- cycles (Fig. 8). The pressure drop, triggered by rapid 
fracture opening and instantaneous stress release, is short and approaches the 0.4–0.5- Hz component under 1- Hz sampling. By contrast, 
the pressure recovery, triggered by hydraulic- energy supplement and progressive stress reaccumulation, is longer and contributes more 
strongly below 0.5 Hz. The dominant failure mechanism mainly affects the recovery stage: Tensile- and shear- dominated failures show 
comparable pressure- drop half- cycles, but shear responses exhibit markedly longer recovery half- cycles. This produces a larger energy 
contrast between the 0.3–0.4- Hz and 0.4–0.5- Hz sub- bands in shear responses. Accordingly, the 0.4–0.5- Hz sub- band energy is used as a 
surrogate for instantaneous stress release and defines the pressure fluctuation energy in Eq. 8, while the adjacent 0.3–0.4- Hz sub- band is 
used as a reference to quantify pressure fluctuation attenuation. Greater energy transfer toward 0.3–0.4 Hz indicates slower stress reaccu-
mulation and a higher likelihood of hydraulic fracture interaction with natural discontinuities.

Effect of Pressure Acquisition Conditions. Effect of the Pressure Measurement Location. In most field operations, wellhead pressure 
is readily available, whereas bottomhole pressure requires downhole gauges. Because the proposed attributes are extracted from 
broadband pressure transients, wellbore transmission can potentially filter or distort broadband responses through frictional losses, fluid 
compressibility, and wave propagation effects. Therefore, the influence of measurement location on the quantified pressure fluctuation 
features is evaluated in Fig. 16. Overall, both measurements exhibit synchronous temporal evolution in pressure fluctuation energy and 
attenuation within the diagnostic band, indicating weak sensitivity of the inferred fracture propagation patterns to measurement location. 
Bottomhole pressure more directly captures local fracture- induced fluctuations because it is inherently decoupled from hydrostatic head 
and wellbore friction variations. Under the proposed workflow, bottomhole fluctuations produce stronger 0.3–0.5- Hz responses and 
are less likely to be corrected due to prominent pressure/rate coherence, yielding higher cumulative pressure fluctuation energy and 
attenuation. By comparison, attenuation is less sensitive to measurement location than energy because location mainly affects the overall 
signal amplitude while weakly altering the intraband energy distribution. Thus, bottomhole pressure can improve robustness under 
frequent operational adjustments and reduce the risk of missing effective fracture- growth intervals caused by wellbore transmission 
effects. Nevertheless, given the limited deployment of downhole gauges, wellhead pressure remains the preferred and more practical input 
for routine field applications.

Effect of the Sampling Frequency. Standard 1- Hz field acquisition enables efficient CWT- based fracture propagation identification. 
Although higher sampling rates help capture broadband impulsive responses and improve the timing accuracy of fracture- event 
localization, their practical benefit is limited by wellbore resonance, which may dominate high- frequency pressure components and 
constrain the observable bandwidth of fracture- induced impulses.
Fig. 17 shows the time- frequency energy spectrum obtained from 10- Hz pressure data. The impulse bandwidth extends to approxi-
mately 1 Hz but is strongly masked by pronounced wellbore characteristic frequencies at higher frequencies, indicating that the coupled 
wellbore/fracture system becomes resonance- dominated beyond this range. Thus, increasing the sampling rate does not necessarily 
expand the usable diagnostic bandwidth. Nevertheless, high- rate acquisition remains advantageous because antialias filtering before 
downsampling suppresses resonant clutter and prevents its aliasing into the diagnostic band. As shown in Fig. 18, direct 1- Hz acquisition 
suffers from persistent contamination of wellbore resonance near 0.3 Hz, which may reduce the apparent fracture- induced energy while 
exaggerating attenuation. In contrast, antialias filtered downsampling from high- rate data mitigates this interference and yields clearer 
transient signatures for fracture propagation classification.
Discussion
Practical Applicability of the Proposed Method. With this paper, we present a CWT- based diagnostic method that links transient pressure 
fluctuations to stress release and accumulation of hydraulic fracture propagation. Under standard 1- Hz sampling, pressure fluctuation 
energy in the 0.4−0.5- Hz sub- band is used to characterize stress release and hydraulic energy conversion into new fracture surfaces, while 
attenuation toward the 0.3−0.4- Hz sub- band reflects enhanced hydraulic- energy dissipation and hindered stress reaccumulation along the 
fracture path. Based on these two quantified features, fracture propagation is dynamically classified into four patterns: primary fracture 
growth, complex fracture development, discontinuities intersection, and fracture interruption.
Strengths of the Method. The physical interpretation of the proposed method is supported by independent monitoring, including 
microseismic, DAS, and tracer observations. In the Marcellus Shale Energy and Environment Laboratory case, periods of accelerated 
cumulative seismic moment represent intensified fracture activity and more frequent stress- release events, which excite intense transient 
pressure drop and accelerated accumulation of pressure fluctuation energy. In addition, intervals with elevated b- values coincide with 
higher proportions of the “Complex fracture development” pattern, providing moderate support for the interpretation that more complex

Limitations. Despite these advantages, the proposed method should be regarded as a realistic but simplified system- scale classification 
of hydraulic- energy allocation rather than a geometric reconstruction of fracture propagation because pressure signals inherently lack 
spatial information. As a single- point response of the integrated wellbore/formation system, pressure cannot directly resolve fracture 
geometry, orientation, or stimulation location. Compared with microseismic and DAS measurements, pressure analysis can evaluate the 
efficiency of hydraulic- energy conversion, cluster- stimulation uniformity, and abnormal interstage communication, but cannot reconstruct 
3D fracture geometry or locate stimulation precisely. Spatially different effects, such as asymmetric fracture- wing growth and additional 
frictional losses along extended fracture paths, may be superimposed in the single- point pressure response, reducing observability and 
potentially weakening event detection. Therefore, complementary measurements, including offset- well pressure, microseismic, and dis-
tributed fiber optic, are valuable for providing spatial and directional constraints for a more comprehensive assessment of fracture geom-
etry, orientation, and complexity.
Another limitation is the reduced applicability during near- wellbore breakdown. In this stage, the pressure signal may be dominated 
by the overall trend, while transient fluctuations are weak, distorted by operational adjustments before the designed pumping rate is 
reached. In addition, fracture propagation develops the fracture process zone ahead of the fracture tip, where distributed microcracking 
and material softening reduce the effective cohesion and promote localized failure (Li et al. 2026). By contrast, breakdown is less favor-
able for generating frequent resolvable transient events because the rock remains largely intact and the threshold for hydraulic energy to 
be converted into new fracture surfaces is higher. As a result, diagnostic interpretation in this stage becomes less stable. A practical miti-
gation is to exclude the breakdown stage from the main diagnostic target and focus instead on the more stable fracture- propagation stage. 
When available, bottomhole pressure and stable hydraulic conditions can further reduce distortion caused by frequent operational adjust-
ments. In addition, wellbore resonance and background noise may affect the time- frequency response. Resonance contaminates specific 
frequency bands and can be mitigated by antialias filtered downsampling from high- rate data. Background noise reduces the overall 
signal- to- noise ratio across the time- frequency domain; in such cases, appropriate filtering strategies, such as wavelet denoising, may help 
to improve the robustness of the interpretation.
Comparison with Existing Pressure-Based Diagnostics. Compared with traditional time- domain pressure analysis, the proposed 
method better captures discrete fracture- propagation events and complexity. Classical approaches, such as the Nolte- Smith framework, 
mainly interpret monotonic net- pressure trends to infer fracture geometry evolution (Nolte and Smith 1981), which overlooks discrete 
transient fluctuations distributed throughout the treatment. Other fluctuation- based studies have related pressure amplitude and frequency 
to fracture complexity but often remain limited to 1D time- domain interpretation (Bian et al. 2016). In contrast, our method directly 
quantifies transient pressure fluctuations in the 2D time- frequency domain, avoiding idealized- fracture assumptions and the iterative 
computations required by moving pressure- reference points (Pirayesh et al. 2015). Combined with CWT multiresolution localization 
and wavelet- coherence screening, the workflow reduces rate- driven interference and enables time- resolved classification of fracture- 
propagation patterns. This improves diagnostic resolution and provides a quantitative and interpretable basis for future realization of 
intelligent fracture pattern diagnosis.
Recent CWT- based diagnostics have demonstrated the value of time- frequency representations for identifying general fracture propa-
gation modes (Gabry et al. 2024b). To provide supporting evidence for the physical consistency of our proposed diagnostic method, we 
benchmark our results against Case 3 (Stage 28, Well MIP- 3H) of Gabry et al. (2024b). In their study, high normalized CWT magnitudes 
of 0.8−0.95 were validated by MRP analysis and microseismic monitoring as periods dominated by fracture height breakthrough (Fig. 19). 
Our method observes a synchronous increase in effective fracture growth, defined as the sum of primary fracture growth and complex 
fracture development, during these periods of high normalized CWT values. This agreement supports a shared physical interpretation: 
The fracture height breakthrough or significant fracture expansion induces strong instantaneous stress release and elevated pressure 
energy in the time- frequency domain. Building upon these shared benchmarks, the fracture propagation patterns identified by our method 
can be further subdivided into dominant height growth or length extension. While both methods rely on CWT, they differ in signal decom-
position strategy. Gabry et al. (2024b) used a series of linear wavelet scales, which compress high- frequency components to provide a 
visual interpretation of general fracture modes. However, our workflow prioritizes the most diagnostic high- frequency components 
(0.3−0.5 Hz) and further enhances the spectral resolution using a linear- frequency binning strategy, thereby expanding the separable 
structure of high- frequency components that would otherwise be compressed. This refinement not only improves localization of fracture 
propagation patterns but also facilitates more reliable attenuation analysis of individual fluctuations. Notably, this attenuation analysis 
extends existing CWT- based approaches, which primarily focus on pressure fluctuation intensity. The proposed method provides a more 
detailed assessment of fracture complexity and supports real- time tracking of fracture dynamics.
Calibration and Future Work. In the current workflow, thresholds for pressure fluctuation energy and attenuation are derived from 
statistical distributions as practical global baselines for noise suppression and fuzzy classification. Future work could develop more 
physically grounded calibration by first establishing formation- specific thresholds from historical stages and then comparing stage- level 
cumulative pressure fluctuation energy, cumulative attenuation, and inferred fracture- pattern proportions with post- fracture pressure 
falloff estimates of fracture area, closure behavior, and fracture complexity (Liu et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2024; Zeinabady and Clarkson 
2023). Stages showing significant discrepancies could be used for local correction of the feature distributions and global thresholds. Post- 
fracture pressure falloff analysis provides a cost- effective pathway for post- fracture calibration because it uses routinely acquired pressure 
data without additional equipment deployment. Combining real- time transient- pressure diagnostics during pumping with post- fracture 
pressure falloff analysis would enable a unified pressure- based workflow across the full treatment cycle.

Conclusions
For this paper, we developed a real- time diagnostic method utilizing CWT to extract rate- corrected transient pressure fluctuations associ-
ated with hydraulic fracture propagation, enabling the dynamic classification of fracture propagation patterns, including primary fracture 
growth, complex fracture development, discontinuities intersection, and fracture interruption. The proposed method is supported by 
independent measurements from microseismic monitoring, distributed fiber- optic measurements, and stage- level tracer production data. 
It can be concluded that:
1. CWT effectively extracts transient pressure fluctuations intrinsic from the stress release and accumulation of hydraulic fracture propa-
gation, which manifest as broadband impulses in the time- frequency domain. Assisted by wavelet coherence analysis to exclude rate- 
coupled responses, the 0.3–0.5- Hz frequency components are identified as the optimal diagnostic window for standard 1- Hz sampling 
data.
2. Pressure fluctuation energy in the 0.4–0.5- Hz sub- band serves as a surrogate for instantaneous stress release of fracture opening and 
reflects the efficiency with which the hydraulic energy is converted into new fracture surfaces. Pressure fluctuation attenuation toward 
the 0.3–0.4- Hz sub- band reflects increased hydraulic- energy dissipation along the fracture path and hindered stress reaccumulation. 
Locally prolonged elevated energy near 0.35–0.4 Hz in the pressure/time- frequency spectrum serves as a potential indicator of en-
hanced fracture complexity and a diagnosis corroborated by the higher tracer production observed in stages dominated by complex 
fracture development patterns.
3. The diagnostic precision can be enhanced by mitigating wellbore transmission effects. Bottomhole pressure better preserves local pres-
sure energy by reducing the influence of hydrostatic head and wellbore friction filtering. Furthermore, a higher sampling rate enables 
antialias filtering that suppresses wellbore resonant contamination within the diagnostic band. This combination effectively isolates 
fracture- induced responses from wellbore system interference, leading to superior sensitivity to subtle fracture propagation events 
under frequent operational adjustments.
