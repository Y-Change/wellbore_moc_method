Summary
Hydraulic fracturing is a critical technology in reservoir modification design. Post- fracturing fracture geometry characterization is of 
essential importance for the design and optimization of hydraulic fracturing treatments. In recent years, fracture geometry evaluation 
methods based on water hammer pressure analysis have gained significant attention due to their cost- effectiveness and operational sim-
plicity. However, existing water hammer models based on equivalent circuit theory exhibit limited physical interpretability between mod-
el parameters and fracture geometry characteristics. In this study, we propose a novel water hammer modeling approach with enhanced 
physical interpretability of model parameters, and use experimental and field data to validate the model’s effectiveness in simulating 
water hammer pressure transients. First, accounting for fluid compressibility effects, we derive the governing equations for water hammer 
phenomena, including both the motion equation and continuity equation. Second, based on the mass balance principle and considering 
perforation friction and fluid filtration effects, we establish boundary constraints for the bottomhole fracture. Third, we develop a new 
water hammer pressure calculation model by integrating fluid compressibility, tubing friction, perforation friction, and fluid filtration 
effects within the hydraulic fracture, thereby enabling fracture geometry evaluation. Finally, using a field case study, we conduct a com-
parative analysis between the model’s fracture geometry results and those from microseismic monitoring. The average relative error of 
the fitted field- monitored water hammer pressure is approximately 0.5%. Compared with the microseismic monitoring results, the average 
relative errors in fracture half- length and height are 15.4 m and 4.2 m, respectively. Moreover, we highlight the influence of fracture and 
wellbore- related parameters on water hammer pressure. In this study, we propose a novel water hammer modeling approach capable of 
fracture geometry evaluation and deepen our fundamental understanding of fracture geometry evaluation using water hammer pressure.
Introduction
In recent years, the rapid development of hydraulic fracturing technology has significantly enhanced the extraction efficiency of oil and 
gas resources (Scanlan et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2020). The geometry of fractures formed post- fracturing exerts a profound influence on 
the ultimate recovery rate of reservoirs, making the assessment of hydraulic fracture geometry crucial for optimizing hydrocarbon 
resource development (Dong et al. 2019; Sadykov et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2021). However, direct observation of subsurface hydraulic 
fracture geometries after fracturing remains unattainable, necessitating the advancement of cost- effective post- fracturing evaluation 
techniques.
Fracture geometry assessment methods based on water hammer pressure are gaining widespread attention due to their short testing 
time and low cost. This method, by analyzing the water hammer phenomenon, can rapidly evaluate the geometry of fractures post- 
fracturing, enabling developers to effectively assess and optimize fracturing operations. Compared with conventional detection methods, 
the water hammer pressure- based fracture geometry assessment method not only enhances fracture detection efficiency but also reduces 
economic costs, meeting the requirements for efficient and low- cost technologies (Clark et al. 2018; Qiu et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2022).
After stopping the pumping of fracturing fluid at each hydraulic fracturing stage, a pressure pulse is formed due to the sudden change 
in flow rate, propagating back and forth between the wellhead and the bottom of the hole (Clark et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2023). The water 
hammer pressure analysis method is based on surface oscillatory pressures monitored at the wellhead to analyze the period, amplitude, 
and decay characteristics of the oscillatory pressures and to quantitatively describe the fracture parameters. Under the condition that the 
well structure and fluid properties are known, the water hammer signal characteristics are mainly affected by the hydraulic fracture geom-
etry. Previous scholars have carried out a lot of studies on the interpretation of hydraulic fracturing fracture characteristics using water 
hammer pressure. Streeter and Wylie (1967) and Wylie and Streeter (1993) used the method of characteristics to solve water hammer 
equations, enhancing solution efficiency and laying the foundation for fracture diagnosis using water hammer phenomena. Holzhausen 
and Gooch (1985) applied impedance analysis to diagnose geometric parameters of bottomhole fractures. Yew and Ashour (1996) vali-
dated the diagnostic effectiveness of impedance analysis for fracture geometric parameters through experimental analysis and field appli-
cation. Mondal (2010) developed a numerical model of water hammer pressure based on the water hammer equations of motion and 
continuity equations, which coupled the wellhead boundary and the bottomhole fracture boundary and regarded the hydraulic fracture as 
a resistive, capacitive, and inertial (RCI) circuit in series and then evaluated the fracture parameters (hereafter referred to as the RCI 
model). Subsequently, Carey et al. (2015) used the RCI model to match field- acquired water hammer data and evaluate fracture geometric 
parameters. Hwang et al. (2017) evaluated the influence of varying circuit parameters on water hammer pressure response characteristics. 
Haustveit et al. (2017) adopted multiple methods to characterize downhole fractures, and the diagnostic results indicate that fracture half- 
lengths determined by water hammer analysis are similar to those obtained from microseismic analysis. Ma et al. (2019) treated fractures 
as the well bottom boundary and established a numerical model for water hammer pressure that considers perforation friction and pressure

(2022) used a transient flow model to describe the fluctuation of water hammer pressure and analyzed the effect of fracture on the response

proposed a new model for calculating the geometry of multiple fractures by considering the stress interference between fractures, thereby

development and application of water hammer theory. Investigations as detailed above explicitly confirm that water hammer pressure 
analysis can effectively identify the geometric parameters of hydraulic fractures.
However, most of the above models characterize the bottomhole boundary between the wellbore and fractures as a series- connected 
circuit composed of resistance, capacitance, and inertance. There is a lack of direct physical correspondence between model parameters 
(e.g., resistance and capacitance) and fracture geometry. Moreover, the aforementioned models neglect variations in fluid density. During 
water hammer phenomena, abrupt pressure fluctuations can induce changes in fluid density. Establishing physically meaningful correla-
tion equations to describe the bottomhole fracture boundary and developing a water hammer model that accounts for multiple factors 
represent highly challenging tasks.
In this study, we propose a novel water hammer modeling approach. First, accounting for fluid compressibility effects, we derive the 
governing equations for water hammer phenomena, including both the motion equation and continuity equation. Second, based on the 
mass balance principle and considering perforation friction and fluid filtration effects, boundary constraints for the bottomhole fracture 
are established. Finally, a new water hammer pressure calculation model is developed by integrating fluid compressibility, tubing friction, 
perforation friction, and fluid filtration effects within the hydraulic fracture, thereby enabling fracture geometry evaluation. Meanwhile, 
based on actual field fracturing parameters, we analyze the influencing factors of water hammer pressure curves to guide subsequent 
fracture geometry evaluation using water hammer pressure data. In contrast to equivalent- circuit- theory- based water hammer modeling 
approaches, our model defines the wellbore- fracture boundary conditions by rigorously accounting for both perforation friction losses and 
fracture fluid mass conservation, thereby providing more physically meaningful representations. Moreover, in practical water hammer 
analysis, the wave speed is often difficult to determine accurately. The proposed model eliminates the need for prior knowledge of the 
water hammer wave speed while still enabling reliable simulation of pressure transients.

Solution of the Equation System
For this study, we utilize the water hammer equations, which involve numerous unknown parameters. The finite difference method is 
adopted as it effectively solves these multivariable partial differential equations. To effectively simulate water hammer pressure under 
hydraulic fracturing conditions, a wellbore- fracture boundary condition is established based on mass balance principles, accounting for 
perforation friction and fluid leakoff effects in fractures. This enables the evaluation of fracture geometry parameters through history 
matching of monitored water hammer pressure.
Difference Equation. The system of water hammer equations derived in this study has more unknowns, and the finite difference method 
is used to solve the system of equations (Shen and Li 2025). That is, the solution region of the required partial differential equation is first 
subdivided into a grid consisting of a finite number of gridpoints, and then a finite difference formula is used to replace the derivatives 
of each gridpoint, and finally, the approximation is carried out to solve the equation, and this process can be regarded as the process of 
replacing the solution of the partial differential equation with an interpolating polynomial and its differentiation. The finite difference 
method effectively solves multivariate partial differential equations, although it requires increased computation time.

Effects of Fracture Parameters. Effect of Fracture Half-Length. Based on the basic data of Well B, to analyze the influence of fracture 
half- length on the water hammer pressure curve, fracture half- lengths of 80 m, 140 m, 200 m, and 260 m were designed and substituted 
into the model of this study to calculate the corresponding water hammer pressure curves, as shown in Fig. 7. From Fig. 7, it is observed 
that the fracture half- length has a significant impact on the decay rate of the water hammer pressure curve; as the fracture half- length 
increases, the curve decays more rapidly. The increase in fracture half- length not only enlarges the fluid loss area but also extends the 
wave propagation path, resulting in greater frictional resistance and ultimately accelerating energy dissipation.
Fig. 7—Water hammer pressure curves for different fracture half- lengths.
Effect of Fracture Height. Taking fracture heights of 10 m, 20 m, 30 m, and 40 m, we calculate the water hammer pressure curves under 
different fracture heights and analyze their influence on the water hammer pressure curves. Fig. 8 presents the calculation results of water 
hammer pressure under varying fracture heights. As the fracture height increases, the decay rate of water hammer pressure slows down. 
The increase in fracture height enhances the fracture’s elastic storage capacity, reducing energy loss during fluid injection and release. 
Moreover, greater fracture height increases the fluid volume within the fracture, strengthening the fluid’s propping effect. This results in 
more uniform pressure distribution within the fracture and relatively lower resistance to pressure wave propagation.
Effect of Perforation Clusters and Perforations. In actual fracturing operations, the competitive initiation among different perforation 
clusters results in only partial clusters and perforations being activated. Fig. 9 simulates the water hammer pressure under varying num-
bers of activated perforation clusters and perforations. Analysis of Fig. 9 reveals that fewer activated perforation clusters and perforations 
lead to an overall lower pressure level. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that, under a constant flow rate, a reduced number 
of activated perforation clusters and perforations not only increase the perforation friction loss but also simplify and concentrate the fluid 
flow path. This reduction mitigates flow interference among different clusters and perforations, thereby decreasing the complexity of fluid 
dynamics and ultimately resulting in a significant reduction in pressure levels.
Effect of Leakoff Coefficient. Fluid leakoff effects in fractures significantly influence water hammer pressure during both fracturing 
injection and pump shutdown processes. Simulations were conducted using leakoff coefficients of 2×10⁻⁷ m/s0.5, 2×10⁻⁶ m/s0.5, and 2×10⁻⁵ 
m/s0.5, with the computational results presented in Fig. 10, which demonstrates that the leakoff coefficient significantly affects the water 
hammer pressure decline rate, with larger coefficients resulting in faster pressure decay. On one hand, increased leakoff coefficient 
enhances fracturing fluid loss to the formation, reducing effective fluid volume in fractures and diminishing the system’s energy storage 
capacity. On the other hand, higher leakoff coefficients facilitate faster pressure dissipation into the formation. Under the combined effects 
of mass loss and energy dissipation mechanisms, elevated leakoff coefficients accelerate water hammer pressure decline.
Effect of Wellbore Parameters. Effect of Horizontal Wellbore Length. As the fracturing operation advances and the fracturing section 
progresses, the actual horizontal wellbore length corresponding to the fracturing pump shutdown gradually decreases, necessitating an 
analysis of the impact of horizontal wellbore length on the water hammer pressure signal. Here, simulations are conducted with horizon-
tal wellbore lengths of 1 km, 2 km, and 3 km, respectively, and the results are displayed in Fig. 11. From Fig. 11, it is observed that the 
length of the horizontal wellbore affects the water hammer pressure cycle. As the wellbore length increases, the water hammer pressure 
wave requires more time for its round trip, leading to a larger water hammer pressure oscillation cycle. Additionally, with the increase 
in wellbore length, wellbore friction increases, and the energy dissipation of the pressure during propagation also increases, resulting in 
accelerated pressure decay.

Discussion. In this study, we propose a novel water hammer modeling approach. First, accounting for fluid compressibility effects, we 
derive the governing equations for water hammer phenomena, including both the motion equation and continuity equation. Second, based 
on the mass balance principle and considering perforation friction and fluid filtration effects, boundary constraints for the bottomhole 
fracture are established. Finally, a new water hammer pressure calculation model is developed by integrating fluid compressibility, tubing 
friction, perforation friction, and fluid filtration effects within the hydraulic fracture, thereby enabling fracture geometry evaluation. 
In contrast to equivalent- circuit- based water hammer models, our approach establishes wellbore- fracture boundary conditions through 
perforation friction and fracture fluid mass conservation, providing clearer physical significance and overcoming the inherent limitation 
where circuit model parameters lack direct correspondence with fracture geometry. Moreover, in practical water hammer analysis, the 
wave speed is often difficult to determine accurately. The proposed model eliminates the need for prior knowledge of the water hammer 
wave speed while still enabling reliable simulation of pressure transients. When evaluating fracture geometry using water hammer 
pressure, both the RCI model and our proposed model rely on iterative parameter adjustment to achieve satisfactory matching between 
simulated and measured pressure data. The fracture geometry parameters that produce the best match are considered representative of 
the actual fracture system. However, the fracture geometry evaluation using water hammer pressure contains inherent uncertainties, 
as different parameter combinations may produce similar pressure responses during multiparameter estimation. Various hydraulic 
fracturing monitoring techniques, such as distributed fiber- optic strain monitoring and high- frequency pressure monitoring, can provide 
prior information on the number of effective perforation clusters and effective perforations, thereby reducing the dimensionality of 
uncertain parameters. This approach may reduce parameter uncertainty, though further research and data validation are still required. The 
proposed model in this study provides a theoretical framework for reducing uncertainty in fracture geometry parameters derived from 
water hammer pressure matching through the aforementioned methodology. Effectively reducing these uncertainties represents a critical 
research direction for fracture geometry evaluation using water hammer pressure, which will be further investigated in subsequent studies.
Conclusion
Based on mass balance principles and considering both perforation friction and fluid leakoff effects, this study establishes wellbore- 
fracture boundary conditions to develop a novel water hammer modeling approach. Moreover, we highlighted the influence of fracture- 
and wellbore- related parameters on water hammer pressure. The following conclusions were obtained:
1. By establishing wellbore- fracture boundary conditions based on perforation friction and fracture fluid mass conservation, while newly 
incorporating fluid compressibility effects, we develop a comprehensive mathematical model for water hammer pressure evolution 
that accounts for fluid compressibility, wellbore friction, perforation friction, and fracture fluid leakoff, enabling fracture geometry 
evaluation.
2. The model- calculated water hammer pressures show excellent agreement with both experimental measurements and field monitoring 
data, demonstrating reliable simulation of pressure transient evolution.
