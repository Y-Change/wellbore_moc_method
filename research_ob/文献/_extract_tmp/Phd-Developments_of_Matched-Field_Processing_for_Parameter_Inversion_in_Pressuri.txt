ABSTRACT
This thesis develops an innovative inverse analysis framework for accurate determination of defect
characteristics and material properties of pressurized water supply systems (WSSs) based on the
matched-field processing (MFP) methodology—a signal processing approach widely and success-
fully used in other domains such as underwater acoustics for source localization and environmental
characterization. The MFP methodology involves (i) an experimental step where transient waves,
propagating at high speed (km/s), are introduced into the pipe system and the response is sensed;
and (ii) a theoretical step where the measured response is “matched” with the replica response (de-
rived from the mathematical model of waves in pipes). The proposed MFP framework is found to
be (i) super-resolving; thus, beating the diffraction limit and enabling each defect in a cluster of
ii

===== PAGE 3 =====
densely (defect-to-defect distance is less than half of the minimum probing wavelength) spaced de-
fects to be individually and clearly detected; (ii) able to significantly suppress unwanted side-lobes;
thus, addressing the issue of ambiguous main-lobe identification; (iii) robust and stable against
various noise types, including white, colored, and impulse noise, even under low signal-to-noise
ratio conditions; and (iv) accurate. These substantial advantages are due to the following three
attributes. First, MFP fully leverages both amplitude and phase information of the broadband tran-
sient signals. Second, MFP is statistically optimal under Gaussian noise; thus, ideally suited for
WSSs since laboratory and field data show that the noise in these systems is well described by
the Gaussian probability distribution. Third, a novel understanding of the mechanism responsible
for the development of side-lobes is acquired in this research, resulting in approaches to deal with
these unwanted phenomena in parameter estimation in WSSs. The proposed MFP methodology
is validated through comprehensive simulation and experimental studies in three specific applica-
tions: determination of location and size of a single leak; determination of location and size of
clustered leaks where the leak-to-leak distance is smaller than half a wavelength; and determina-
tion of the viscoelastic parameters of our testing facilities. The MFP method is generalizable across
pipeline systems, applicable to all types of unknown parameters, rapid to perform, non-intrusive,
non-destructive, and supports real-time health condition assessment of WSSs.

INTRODUCTION AND LITERA TURE REVIEW
1.1 Motivation: Crucial Parameter Estimation in WSSs
Water supply systems (WSSs) are lifelines of cities and towns, relying on the stable and safe opera-
tions of extensive pressurized pipelines to meet the fluctuating demand while maintaining the water
quality and pressure [1]. However, these systems now encounter significant challenges, including
rising water demand, aging infrastructure, and increasingly complex network topology. Studies
report that these factors can reduce operational efficiencies [2, 3] and result in pipeline defects such
as leakage [4, 5], corrosion [6], and blockage [7]. Among these defects, leakage is particularly
problematic as it is the ultimate result of many issues like cracks and can lead to substantial re-
source and energy wastage, decreased supply pressure, environmental damage, and other adverse
impacts [8, 9]. Leakage can be caused by multiple factors, including material degradation, soil
or ground movement, vibrations from traffic, extreme weather conditions, and operational stresses
[10–13]. In recent years, water leakage has significantly intensified across global WSSs, particu-
larly in rapidly urbanizing Asian regions. A 2010 report [14] stated that WSSs in Asia lost about
29 billion cubic meters of non-revenue water (NRW, defined as treated water that is produced but
not delivered to customers), primarily due to leaks. This volume of water was valued at around 8.7
billion US dollars. A more recent report [15] in 2018 indicated that over an eight-year period, the
volume of lost NRW in Asian cities nearly doubled to 52 billion cubic meters, valued at 15 billion
US dollars. Such NRW losses can account for up to 35% of the total water supply. Although leak-
1

===== PAGE 26 =====
age often receives the most attention due to its significant immediate impact, other pipeline defects
also significantly compromise system performance (e.g., a corroded pipe is more prone to bursts
and incurs higher frictional losses) [16]. Combined, these defects create compounding risks to the
stability and safety of WSSs. This reality highlights the need for early, targeted defect detection.
While the defect determination is a critical step for system reliability, another important aspect of
WSSs maintenance is understanding the mechanical behavior of the pipeline materials themselves.
Over the past few decades, the adoption of polymeric pipes made from materials such as polyvinyl
chloride (PVC), polyethylene (PE), and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) has grown. These mate-
rials offer additional advantages over traditional metal materials, including resistance to corrosion,
lower weight, ease of installation, and cost-effectiveness [17]. Unlike metal pipes which exhibit
predominantly elastic behavior, plastic pipes display viscoelastic properties – meaning their de-
formation under stress has both an instantaneous elastic component and a time-dependent viscous
component [18]. This viscoelasticity is typically characterized by a creep function often derived
from mechanical analog models, such as the Kelvin-V oigt model which represents the pipe wall as
a spring and dashpot in parallel. The creep function details the evolution of material strain under
stress over time, incorporating parameters such as creep compliance (a measure of the material’s de-
formability) and retardation time (the timescale of the viscoelastic response) [19]. Engineers need
to quantify these viscoelastic parameters to accurately predict pipe behavior under pressure surges
and even under normal operation [20, 21]. In addition, the viscoelastic properties of plastic pipe
walls can “mask” or distort transient wave reflections caused by leaks or other anomalies [22–24].
If the viscoelastic parameters are not accounted for, anomaly detection via transient wave-based
methods becomes significantly more complex [25]. Moreover, calibrating viscoelastic parameters
also helps in longevity studies (e.g., viscoelastic creep over many years might lead to pipe sagging
2

===== PAGE 27 =====
or need for support) [26]. In summary, accurately estimating material properties like viscoelasticity
in polymeric pipes is crucial in WSSs.
In addition to understanding defect characteristics (e.g. defect size and location) and material prop-
erties (e.g. viscoelastic parameters), knowing other pipeline parameters — such as wave speed,
friction coefficients, and network topology metrics — is also important to facilitate pipeline con-
dition assessment and informed decisions regarding maintenance and upgrades in WSSs [27–29].
Each of the parameters uniquely alters hydraulic characteristics, such as pressure head and flow
rate, under both steady-state and transient operating conditions within WSSs. This functional re-
lationship suggests that accurate pipeline parameter estimation can be achieved through inverse
analysis to the measurements of these hydraulic characteristics.
1.2 Background
This section first highlights the inherently ill-posed nature of the inverse problem of using hy-
draulic transient waves for pipeline parameter estimation, then reviews the existing applications
of matched-field processing (MFP) as an inversion technique for obtaining parameter estimates,
setting the stage for discussing our research objectives.

1.2.1 Inverse Problem Framework for Parameter Estimation Using Hydraulic
Transient Waves
Accurate estimation of the unknown pipeline parameters can be achieved by measuring and ana-
lyzing physical signals that are influenced by these parameters. For example, pressure head and
flow discharge within pipelines are known to be affected by pipe dimensions, pipe defects and
pipe wall material properties. In recent years, hydraulic transient waves (also known as water
hammer waves or hydraulic transients), historically associated with destructive water hammer phe-
nomena, are increasingly recognized in the literature as a promising tool for parameter estimation in
pipelines. These transient-based methods offer advantages such as scalability to large systems, cost-
effectiveness, and non-disruptive operations [30]. Transient waves can be generated in pipelines
through intentional (active) or unintentional (passive) flow perturbations or disturbances, such as
starting/stopping a pump, adjusting valves [31, 32], or using specialized devices like the Portable
Pressure Wave Maker in large-diameter pipes [33]. These generated waves propagate inside the
pipelines with a fast wave speed (e.g., 400 m/s in polymeric pipes and 1300 m/s in steel pipes),
and encode information about the unknown system parameters by their interaction with pipelines.
Through measuring and analyzing the transient signals, it is possible to extract out the information
of these unknown pipeline parameters like the defect characteristics, the material properties, and so
on [34–38]. For instance, the signals shown in Fig. 1.1 clearly indicate that due to the existence of
leak, the transient signal of intact pipe differs from the one of leaky pipe. This pressure difference
means that the transient signal can capture the leak information. In essence, the methods using
transient waves operate similarly to radar or sonar systems: an injected transient wave acts as a
probing signal, while the reflected waveform reveals system properties.
4

===== PAGE 29 =====
Figure 1.1. The calculated transient signals corresponding to intact pipe and leaky pipe from nu-

Given the established role of transient waves in encoding pipeline parameters, our next step is to
extract/infer these parameters from transient signals. Such task of determining the unknown pa-
rameters from observed data is typically, in many scientific and engineering disciplines, formulated
as an inverse problem. Specifically, our goal is to infer unknown parameters using the system’s
transient response and a transient wave propagation model. However, this inverse problem is in-
herently ill-posed as the exact solution cannot be directly obtained owing to the complexity of
transient wave propagation (thus introducing model inaccuracies) and the inevitable presence of
measurement noise. Consequently, advanced inversion techniques (or optimization methods) are
required to obtain an estimated solution. To address this challenge, a promising way is the adop-
tion of matched-field processing (MFP), a powerful inversion approach widely used in fields such
as underwater acoustics for source localization and environmental characterization [39]. In the
context of pipeline parameter estimation, MFP can be employed to match transient wave signals
measured along the pipeline with those computed from a mathematical model, thereby inferring
the unknown parameters. In the next subsections, we introduce MFP’s concept, terminology, exist-

===== PAGE 30 =====
ing applications in pipeline parameter estimation along with its advantages and key limitations to
contextualize our contributions.
1.2.2 Inversion Technique of Matched-Field Processing
Terminology
Before we explore the applications of matched-field processing (MFP) method in pipeline param-
eter estimation, it is important to define several commonly used terminologies. These definitions
enhance our understanding of the method and prepare us for the subsequent discussions on MFP’s
advantages and research gaps. The relevant terms are outlined below:
1. field: The term “field” typically refers to the state of physical quantities at points in the envi-
ronment. In our pipeline scenario, “field” is the measured or modeled pressure information
at points along pipeline during a hydraulic transient event. The field can also be called the
wavefield/data/signal.
2. ambiguity space: The output space of MFP is commonly called “ambiguity space” in liter-
ature, due to the fact that there are often multiple candidate model parameter sets producing
high match, leading to ambiguous determination of the optimal parameter set. While the term
"match space" or "correlation space" might more precisely reflect the match/correlations pro-
cess between fields, the high-resolution MFP proposed in this thesis involves more complex
steps instead of just simple correlation comparisons, making "ambiguity space" the more
accurate term.
3. peaks: These very distinct, high-intensity MFP outputs, which represent good matches be-
6

===== PAGE 31 =====
tween the measured and modeled fields, are naturally referred to as “peaks” in ambiguity
space. They are easily identifiable.
4. main-lobe: The highest peak showing in ambiguity space is called the “main-lobe”. The re-
gion (or the point) where this main-lobe locating at in ambiguity space indicates the predicted
optimal parameter set.
5. side-lobes: Peaks that are not close to the main-lobe but still show high MFP outputs are
called “side-lobes.” These side-lobes spread out with the increasing range from main-lobe
on ambiguity space. When side-lobes become comparable in peak levels to main-lobe, it
bring ambiguity to identify the main-lobe, thus questioning the credibility of the main-lobe
identification.
6. mismatch: Mismatch refers to any errors that occur in the modeled or replica fields. These
errors can arise due to uncertainties in known parameters, inaccuracies in the propagation or
scattering models (e.g., we often simplify real-world processes), and approximate nature of
numerical solutions. Mismatch can impact MFP performance in varying degrees, depending
on the error type.
7. degradation: Degradation indicates a severe loss or mislocation of the main-lobe. This can
occur due to mismatch, low signal-to-noise ratios, or any other factors, and represents a
performance failure of the MFP method.
8. super-resolution: Super-resolution refers to MFP’s super-resolving capability to distinguish
clustered defects which are spaced closer than half of the minimum probing wavelength
(the wave diffraction limit). In this thesis, to avoid confusion, “super-resolution” or “super-
resolving” are specially used to describe MFP’s spatial resolution, which signifies the small-
est distance capable of distinguishing between different defects when using MFP. For de-
scribing the main-lobe width, we use the term "high-resolution" when the main-lobe width is
7

===== PAGE 32 =====
smaller than half of the minimum probing wavelength.
9. noise: Broadly speaking, noise can be defined as any unwanted signals in the measurements.
In this thesis, we consider three types of noise: white noise, colored noise (e.g., blue noise),
and impulse noise, which primarily differ in how their power spectra are distributed across
frequencies. Specifically, (i) white noise has a uniform power spectrum, meaning it maintains
equal energy across all frequencies, (ii) blue noise exhibits greater energy at higher frequen-
cies, with power increasing as frequency increases, and (iii) impulse noise is characterized
by high-amplitude disturbances that overlay the baseline white noise spectrum.
Existing Applications of MFP
Matched-field processing (MFP) is a powerful inversion technique that has enjoyed widespread
use in various domains such as ocean acoustics and earthquake research [40–42], wherein it is the
premier choice to solve a variety of inverse problems, including source localization, environmental
parameter estimation, bearing of passive sources, and model accuracy evaluation. Its underlying
principle is straightforward: (i) collecting the measured fieldb, (ii) calculating the replica fieldA(x)
for each candidate model parameter set x, (iii) Selecting the parameter set yielding the main-lobe
in ambiguity space (i.e., showing the highest match/cross-correlation betweenb and A(x)) to be the
predicted/estimated result. In essence, the name “matched-field processing” explicitly reflects this
process of field matching.
MFP holds significant promise in pipeline parameter inversion using transient wavefields, due to
the following technical merits: (i) MFP is particularly recommended for low-frequency applications
(e.g., a few Hz to several tens of Hz, which coincides with the frequency range of transient waves),
8

===== PAGE 33 =====
because low-frequency waves have long wavelengths which yield fewer and more stable acoustic
modes, enabling more reliable wavefield matching; (ii) MFP can use both amplitude and phase
information of the whole frequency band; i.e., it uses all available data components rather than
just phase (as is done in most time-domain theoretic modeling) or just specific frequencies. This
indicates that MFP, as a full-waveform inversion method [36], can achieve more accurate and robust
results; (iii) MFP is statistically equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and optimal
when the measurement noise follows a Gaussian distribution [43], thus, ideally suited for WSSs
since laboratory and field data show that the noise in these systems is well described by the Gaussian
probability distribution [44].
Recently, MFP has been successfully applied in water pipeline systems as a frequency-domain
inversion method, although its applications have been primarily limited to defect detection. The
following provides a chronological overview of MFP’s applications and performance in pipeline
engineering: Specifically, Wang et al. [43] have proposed a transient-based leak detection method-
ology using the conventional broadband MFP (CB-MFP, also known as the Bartlett processor)
method, which is the most widely used and best understood MFP technique. CB-MFP solves the
inverse problem of leak detection by inferring the leak location and size in model given measured
transient wavefield dependent on such information, and it is proved to be robust against white noise.
Additionally, as CB-MFP uses both amplitude and phase information of signals over a broadband
low-frequency band, it exhibits superior robustness and precision relative to techniques that solely
rely on the resonant frequencies of the system (such as [45]) and those that are only based on phase
information (such as [4]). Moreover, the CB-MFP method reduces the two-dimensional search
space (i.e., the leak size and leak location) to one-dimensional searches where the leak location
is first found, and the result is substituted back into the defect detection functional to determine
9

===== PAGE 34 =====
the leak size. This dimensionality reduction improves computational efficiency. This method was
later adapted for leak localization in a single viscoelastic pipe, where the pipe wall’s viscoelas-
tic properties were included in the wave propagation model by defining a frequency-dependent
wave speed [46]. Subsequently, the CB-MFP method was further developed to account for uncer-
tainties arising from incomplete knowledge, by incorporating prior uncertainty estimates derived
from leak-free transient experiments. This improvement was validated using experimental data
from quasi-field tests on a 135-meter-long HDPE pipe at Beacon Hill, Hong Kong SAR [47]. Fur-
thermore, the application of MFP for leak detection has been extended from single pipe to more
complex pipe networks such as tree-type and loop-type, wherein a derivation to factorized wave
propagation model is given [48–50]. Despite these extensions of MFP, it still has notable research
gaps (detailed in the next section).
Current Research Gaps in MFP
Matched-field processing (MFP) is still in the early stages of application in pipeline systems and
encounters significant challenges. One major difficulty is the presence of side-lobes in ambiguity
space, which are undesirable and cause ambiguity in determining the main-lobe associated with
parameter estimates [51–54]. This phenomenon is illustrated in Fig. 1.2(a), where the side-lobes
bring ambiguity for correct main-lobe identification. Although MFP has been applied and extended
in several studies for leak detection [43, 46–50, 55–59], the physical and mathematical origins of
side-lobe generation remain unexamined. Empirical studies suggest that the side-lobe resolution
can be increased (i.e., the side-lobe width can be narrowed) by using more sensors[43, 60], but their
amplitudes maintain as the sensor number increases. Additionally, in real-world systems, spatial
constraints (e.g., accessibility, cost) can often restrict measurement point density. Therefore, there
10

===== PAGE 35 =====
is a need of inversion techniques that can produce accurate parameter estimations with suppressed
side-lobes, by using as few measurement points as possible. Notably, side lobe interference plagues
inverse problems across disciplines, including ocean acoustics [41], geophysics [61], and pipeline
diagnostics [43, 62], underscoring its universality. Systematically addressing side-lobe suppression
challenges is therefore a cross-disciplinary imperative to unlock MFP’s full potential in pipeline
parameter estimation and beyond.
Another critical challenge involves potential degradation of MFP performance due to nonwhite
noise interference. As shown in Fig. 1.2(b), the CB-MFP method cannot localize the leak cor-
rectly under nonwhite noise conditions. This limitation is especially critical in practical pipeline
environments, where nonwhite-noise sources include systematic errors (e.g., model inaccuracies)
and environmental disturbances (e.g., flow turbulence, traffic-induced vibrations, and sensor mea-
surement errors) [43, 62, 63]. The proposed CB-MFP approach [43] directly sums the complex
information across all frequencies via the inner product operation. During this summation process,
white noise can be canceled out with each other as white noise has uniform spectral density over
frequencies. As a result, white noise does not introduce any frequency-dependent bias into the inner
product, which consequently ensures the robustness of CB-MFP against white noise. However, this
robustness diminishes for colored or impulse noise, where spectral correlation introduces persistent
interference through frequency summation and may result in performance failures, as shown in Fig.
4.3. Incorporating prior information about the probabilistic distribution of noise could theoretically
enhance MFP’s robustness. Yet, obtaining complete noise statistics is hindered by non-stationary
noise profiles and limited spatial-temporal sampling in real-word operational pipelines.
Moreover, MFP’s spatial resolution is constrained by wave diffraction limitations. That is, the MFP
11

===== PAGE 36 =====
methods based on a single-defect model are unable to detect clustered defects, defined as defects
spaced less than half the minimum probing wavelength apart [60, 64]. For instance, as depicted in
Fig. 1.2(c), the MFP output results has only one peak at the clustered defects, i.e., the MFP method
fails to distinguish the closely-spaced leaks. While the MFP methods based on a multi-defect model
can be developed to address this issue, the challenge of an unknown number of defects remains
unresolved. Last but not the least, to date, MFP applications remain largely restricted to pipeline
defect detection, with few efforts to extend the methodology to broader parameter estimation tasks
such as material property estimation.

In conclusion, while the existing studies validate MFP’s potential for leak detection, critical gaps
persist. These gaps include: 1) the unclear mechanism of side-lobe generation, 2) the ways to
suppress side-lobes, 3) the spatial resolution limit, 4) the performance degradation under nonwhite
noise, and 5) the currently limited application scope. In the next section, we outline our research
objectives with targeted solutions, aiming at overcoming these limitations.
1.3 Thesis Objectives
Building upon the detailed introduction of the existing applications and limitations of MFP methods
in pipeline parameter estimation, this section outlines research objectives with proposed solutions
through theoretical enhancements and algorithmic innovations. Ultimately, our goal is to propose
a novel transient-based MFP methodology (serving as a generalized inverse analysis framework)
capable of yielding accurate, robust, and super-resolution parameter estimations, with greatly sup-
pressed side-lobes and enhanced main-lobe resolution.
• Objective 1: Develop a generalized MFP framework for pipeline parameter inversion
Proposed solution: To develop a versatile MFP framework capable of estimating various
pipeline parameters, an important option is the decomposition of transient signals. Specifi-
cally, we first formulate transient wave propagation models to analytically derive frequency-
domain pressure head responses, which encode the pipeline parameter information for subse-
quent MFP analysis. This expressed pressure head is then decomposed into distinct compo-
nents, with each component predominantly associated with a subset of parameters exhibiting
distinct spectral signatures. For example, the total pressure response in a leaky pipe can
13

===== PAGE 38 =====
be separated into one component representing the intact pipeline’s transient response (cap-
turing material properties) and individual components representing the transient responses
from each leak (capturing leak-specific information). This decomposition enables selective
isolation of parameter-specific signal components, suppressing interference from irrelevant
signals during MFP analysis. The next critical step involves establishing a generalized MFP
mathematical formulation in the form of an objective function dependent on the general un-
known parameters, with the optimal parameter values being those that maximize the func-
tion’s output. To further enhance versatility, we utilize broadband excitations rather than
single-frequency inputs, as the latter can provide insufficient information for determining
parameters [65].
• Objective 2: Enhance the robustness for parameter estimation in nonwhite noise envi-
ronments
Proposed solution: We propose modified broadband MFP (MB-MFP) techniques that en-
hance robustness under nonwhite noise conditions. MB-MFP initiates with per-frequency
normalization of measured signals across the broadband spectrum, followed by the aver-
age of single-frequency MFP outputs. This normalization scales both the signal and noise
components so that their combined magnitude equals unity, thereby effectively mitigating
frequency-localized noise amplification (e.g., impulse noise with peaked spectra). As a re-
sult, MB-MFP methods not only preserve white noise robustness inherent to CB-MFP while
significantly improving tolerance to non-stationary, spectrally correlated noise including col-
ored and impulse noise.
• Objective 3: Investigate the generation mechanism of side-lobes
14

===== PAGE 39 =====
Proposed solution: To suppress side-lobes which cause ambiguity to accurate parameter es-
timation, it is crucial to first understand how these side-lobes are generated and then develop
advanced optimization algorithms to reduce them. Thode et al. [66] demonstrated that in
underwater acoustics, the side-lobe patterns observed in the spatial range-frequency plane
tend to converge toward the true source location. Motivated by this insight, we leverage the
hidden information embedded in side-lobes of single-frequency MFP results. We begin with
a thorough theoretical and numerical investigation into the mechanisms behind side-lobe
formation. Our analysis reveals that the side-lobes in broadband MFP ambiguity space re-
sult from the cumulative superposition of individual side-lobes generated in single-frequency
MFP results.
• Objective 4: Achieve side-lobe suppression and increase main-lobe resolution
Capitalizing on the insight of single-frequency MFP output space, we propose two enhanced
MB-MFP techniques: (1) MB-MFP1, which incorporates a side-lobe cancellation algorithm,
and (2) MB-MFP 2, which utilizes a peak extraction algorithm. They can significantly im-
prove the main-lobe-to-side-lobe ratios in the final ambiguity spaces. Both approaches uses
the gradient information in single-frequency MFP output to preserve the main-lobes while
selectively reduce or eliminate side-lobes of each single-frequency MFP output, before inte-
grating the single-frequency MFP outputs to define the broadband MFP methods. Therefore,
MB-MFP1 and MB-MFP 2 can achieve side-lobe suppression. In addition, MB-MFP 2 can
provide a improved high-resolution main-lobe identification.
• Objective 5: Achieve super-resolution in clustered-defect detection
Proposed solution: To achieve super-resolution for clustered-defect scenarios, we propose
15

===== PAGE 40 =====
MB-MFP methods based on a theoretical model with 𝑁𝐿 ≥ 𝑁𝐿∗, where 𝑁𝐿 and 𝑁𝐿∗ respec-
tively represent the number of defects given in the model and in the real pipeline system. The
multi-defect model expands the solution space dimensionality, enabling MB-MFP to resolve
up to 𝑁𝐿 defects. Thus, when 𝑁𝐿 ≥ 𝑁𝐿∗, the real defects are guaranteed to lie within this
expanded subspace. This conclusion draws from the concept of “overcomplete representa-
tion,” which is commonly used in signal processing and machine learning to enhance the
correctness and robustness of results [67]. An overcomplete representation, where the num-
ber of latent features exceeds the signal dimensions, is particularly useful when the signal
consists of multiple sets of complete linear features. In pipeline systems, by assuming weak
defect interactions (valid for the usual defect detection cases [68]), pressure responses can be
approximated as linear superpositions of individual defect contributions, and thus this over-
completeness holds true. More importantly, it is found that the estimated leak locations (𝑁𝐿)
tend to cluster around the actual leak locations ( 𝑁𝐿∗) as 𝑁𝐿 increases and eventually sur-
passes 𝑁𝐿∗. This clustering effect makes MB-MFP method able to simultaneously estimate
the number of leaks and achieve precise leak localization, and thus addresses the challenge
of unknown defect number.
In summary, we aim to develop an generalized inverse analysis framework for pipeline parameter
estimation by synergistically combining wave physics principles and MFP methodology. We ad-
dresses several critical challenges, including the unclear generation mechanism of MFP side-lobes,
the ambiguity caused by side-lobes, the spatial resolution limit imposed by wave diffraction effect,
and the performance degradation under nonwhite noise. Beyond pipelines, the framework’s adapt-
ability positions it as a promising versatile inverse analysis toolbox for other applications such as
geophysical imaging, and acoustic sensing.

1.4 Thesis Organization
1.4.1 Thesis Outline
The thesis progresses from fundamental theories (Chapters 2: governing equations, and Chapter 3:
MFP methodologies) to targeted applications (Chapters 4: single-leak detection, Chapter 5: multi-
leak detection, and Chapter 6: viscoelastic parameter estimation), culminating in conclusions and
future directions (Chapter 7). These chapters are organized as follows:
• Chapter 2 presents the governing equations for transient wave propagation in pressurized
pipelines. Based on the mass and momentum conservation laws, we formulate the governing
partial differential equations (PDEs) in the time domain. Using Fourier transform analysis,
these PDEs are systematically converted to their frequency-domain representations. By ap-
plying the appropriate boundary conditions, we analytically solve the equations governing
the hydraulic quantities (pressure head and flow rate) at a single frequency and a designated
measurement location. These solutions are represented using the transfer matrix method,
where the transfer matrix intuitively describes the transient wave propagation process along
the pipeline and explicitly relates the hydraulic quantities at the boundary to those at the
measurement location. This transfer matrix contains key system parameters, including de-
fect characteristics and material properties, and can be decomposed into distinct parameter-
specific submatrices (e.g., the overall transfer matrix for a leaky pipe can be decomposed

CHAPTER 2
GOVERNING EQUA TIONS
In this chapter, we present the governing partial differential equations of hydraulic transient waves,
focusing on solving the mass and momentum conservation equations in the frequency domain via
the transfer-matrix method. We first introduce the time-domain governing equations for transient
wave propagation. These equations include the parameters corresponding to defect characteris-
tics (e.g., leakage as a mass source) and material properties (e.g, viscoelasticity). Subsequently,
we employ Fourier transformation to the time-domain equations to obtain their frequency-domain
counterparts, and then derive solutions for pressure head and flow rate in the frequency domain.
We express these solutions via a transfer-matrix method, where the matrix characterizes transient
wave propagation along the pipeline. Finally, we extend the expression of pressure head from sin-
gle frequency and measurement location to multiple frequencies and measurement locations. The
resulting pressure head expression yields the computed modeled wavefield, which is incorporated
into the MFP methodology for parameter estimation, as detailed in the following chapter.

3.1 Theoretical foundation
Central to our MFP methodology are the following steps: (i) developing a theoretical frequency-
domain model to simulate the generation and propagation of hydraulic transient waves, (ii) adjust-
ing the concerned parameters within a candidate range, (iii) computing the model outputs, and (iv)
comparing the modeled fields with the measured field. The optimal parameters are determined by
selecting the candidates that best match the modeled and measured fields. According to classical
papers on applying the MFP approach to inverse problems [39, 40, 80], the key recommendations
for applying MFP to our pipeline environment include:
1. selecting an environment with high temporal stability;
2. selecting an environment which was effectively range independent (thereby minimizing com-
plications and issues relating to environmental data range sampling);
3. determining all non-concerned/non-targeted parameters as accurately as possible, e.g., wave
speed, measurement locations, pipe length and diameter, etc.;
4. incorporating an accurate scattering model for the effects of concerned parameters, e.g., the
generalized K-V model for the viscoelastic effect, or the leak-induced scattering transfer
matrix 𝑈 𝐿;
5. reducing the parameter search, i.e., the parameter dimension, as much as possible
6. using low frequencies to minimize the effects of random perturbations (like internal waves
and turbulence)
7. determining the solution parameter values by means of a global search of all possible (dis-
cretized) values (thereby avoiding troublesome convergence or search algorithms).
Guided by these recommendations, we proceed to develop the inverse analysis framework based

4.1 Introduction
Measured transient responses are inevitably noisy, influenced by factors like turbulence, traffic,
measurement errors, and vibrations from hydraulic devices. To address noisy signals, a transient
wave-based leak detection method using the CB-MFP technique, also known as the Bartlett broad-
band MFP, was recently proposed [43]. The CB-MFP method is conducted in the frequency do-
main and uses signals over a broad low-frequency band. As stated in the introduction chapter, the
proposed method demonstrated better performance compared to existing approaches that depend
entirely on system resonance frequencies (e.g., [45]) or inverse modeling techniques like transient
analysis inversion [4]. The CB-MFP approach simplifies the search process by separating the two-
variable problem (leak position and size) into sequential single-variable searches. First, potential
leak position is identified, then this result helps determine leak size through the output function,
making calculations more efficient [43]. When plotting CB-MFP results regarding to the possible
leak locations, distinct peaks emerge. However, distinguishing between true defect-related peak
(main-lobe) from the interference peaks (side-lobes) remains difficult [51–54]. These side-lobes
appear because of the measurement limitations: (i) insufficient sensor density (spaced wider than
half-wavelength intervals) [60] and (ii) limited frequency range in signal collection [62]. Further-
more, while CB-MFP performs well with white noise and structured nonwhite noise [43], it en-
counter challenges in real-world applications because nonwhite noise patterns vary unpredictably
and prior information is hard to obtain [63].
Previous CB-MFP developments in leak detection [49, 50, 55, 56, 59, 82] have not addressed the
side-lobe issues. These false signals create uncertainty in pinpointing leak locations across various
fields including pipeline systems [43, 62], underwater acoustics [41], and earth science applications

4.7 Summary
The chapter proposed a novel modified broadband MFP (MB-MFP) technique that increases the
robustness for leak localization in the presence of nonwhite noise. Combining the gradient infor-
mation in the MFP output surface, we further developed two improved modified broadband MFP
methods (MB-MFP 1 and MB-MFP 2) with lower side-lobe levels and higher localization resolu-
tion. The proposed methods addressed three key issues of the used conventional broadband MFP
(CB-MFP) method for leak detection: (i) the unclear generation mechanism of side-lobes, (ii) the
ambiguity caused by high-level side-lobes for accurate leak localization, and (iii) the poor perfor-
mance under nonwhite noise. The accuracy and robustness of the proposed MB-MFP methods in
localizing leaks while suppressing side-lobes and noise effects is validated experimentally.

6.1 Introduction
Matched-field processing (MFP) is a premier signal processing technique used in underwater acous-
tics [40], with many applications as noted by Tolstoy [39]: “... its successful application to a wide
range of problems including the localization in range, depth, and bearing of passive sources, the de-
termination of environmental parameters needed for accurate predictions of acoustic propagation,
and the evaluation of model accuracies”. MFP offers many advantages including (i) noise suppres-
sion [102], (ii) insensitivity to the modeling errors [82], (iii) scalability to waveguide/source char-
acteristics [103], and (iv) versatility in handling multi-channel and multi-dimensional data [104].
In the pipeline field, MFP also emerged as a powerful technique to detect defects when combined
with hydraulic transients [43, 46, 55]. In particular, researchers have shown that MFP provides
an optimal estimate of defect characteristics when noise follows a Gaussian distribution and the
estimator is equivalent to the maximum likelihood estimation [43]. The method has already been
extended to from a single pipe to complex pipe networks [49, 50, 98, 105]. Moreover, enhanced
versions of MFP [81] have been introduced to (i) handle the side-lobes that are commonly found in
the conventional MFP or Bartlett MFP, (ii) address the nonwhite noise scenarios, and (iii) achieve
super-resolution. Thus far, the scope of MFP in the previous pipeline research has primarily focused
on defect detection, with its potential for broader parameter inversion still underexplored.
This chapter extends the application of using MFP to a general method for estimating pipeline sys-
tem properties by an inversion on hydraulic transient data set. Central to our approach is developing
a theoretical frequency-domain model to simulate the generation and propagation of hydraulic tran-
sient waves, adjusting the concerned parameters within a candidate range, computing the model

7.1.5 Accurate Material Property Estimation
The effectiveness of the proposed MFP methodologies in estimating material properties is vali-
dated through both numerical simulations and laboratory experiments. Specifically, the approach
is demonstrated on an HDPE pipe system, where it achieves accurate estimation of viscoelastic
parameters. We achieve 5% reconstruction error (by using the predicted viscoelastic parameters)
with 15 ms/parameter computational speed. The optimized two-element K-V model shows 25% ac-
curacy gain over single-element versions, while a third element provides only 0.09% improvement,
which sets a clear threshold for complexity optimization in viscoelastic modeling. Furthermore,
validation against six-year-interval creep compliance data confirms material aging mechanisms,
showing a 0.52% reduction in compliance, and demonstrating MFP’s dual capability for real-time
monitoring and long-term structural health assessment.
In conclusion, this thesis bridges theoretical wave physics and practical pipeline parameter estima-
tion by developing innovative MFP inversion techniques. The proposed MFP framework system-
atically addresses critical challenges, including noise resilience, spatial resolution limits, side-lobe
interference, and complexity in handling multiple parameters, through comprehensive theoretical
analysis and methodological advancements. Numerical simulations and experimental validations
confirm the effectiveness of the proposed MFP framework for achieving accurate parameter esti-
mation. These improvements contribute directly to maintaining hydraulic infrastructure integrity,
enhancing operational safety, and promoting long-term sustainability for WSSs.
