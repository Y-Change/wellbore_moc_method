Summary
Accurate evaluation of hydraulic- fracturing (HF) effectiveness in shale oil reservoirs remains challenging because of complex induced 
fracture networks and limited access to low- cost, high- resolution diagnostics. In this study, we present an integrated, data- driven work-
flow that combines water hammer analysis (WHA) and unified transient analysis (UTA) to quantify both stimulated fracture geometry and 
the production- contributing fracture surface area from routinely available shut- in pressure and production records. WHA interprets early- 
time shut- in pressure oscillations to infer fracture complexity and characteristic dimensions, while UTA uses superposition principle and 
slope analysis to estimate the effective fracture surface area during production. Field case study indicates that the fracture surface area 
estimated by WHA is less than half of that estimated by UTA, indicating that a large fraction of the stimulated volume may not contribute 
to sustained flow. Correlating WHA and UTA results with logging data and operational parameters helps identify key drivers of fracture 
usage and provides actionable guidance to refine stimulation designs in data- limited shale plays.
Introduction
HF is an essential technique for unlocking hydrocarbon resources in unconventional reservoirs, where low permeability restricts natural 
fluid flow. By injecting large volumes of water, proppants, and chemical additives, HF creates and maintains high- conductivity fractures 
that enhance hydrocarbon migration from the reservoir to the wellbore (Economides and Martin 2007; Tang et al. 2011). However, as 
unconventional resource development expands, accurately evaluating HF performance and optimizing treatment designs are becoming 
critical for maximizing recovery, minimizing environmental impact, and managing operational costs.
The evaluation of HF- induced fractures traditionally relies on mechanical models, numerical simulations, and field- based monitoring 
techniques. Classical models, including the Perkins- Kern- Nordgren and Khristianovich- Geertsma- de Klerk models, have long been uti-
lized to estimate fracture dimensions and propagation characteristics based on fundamental rock mechanics (Perkins and Kern 1961; 
Geertsma and De Klerk 1969; Nordgren 1972). With advances in computational methods, these models have evolved into pseudo- 3D, 
planar- 3D, and fully 3D approaches, offering improved accuracy in fracture characterization (Adachi et al. 2007; Zhuang et al. 2016). 
Nevertheless, these models often require extensive computational resources and lack the capability for real- time analysis, making them 
impractical during live HF operations in the field (Shen et al. 1997).
Beyond model- based approaches, real- time field monitoring techniques, such as microseismic and distributed fiber- optic sensing (i.e., 
distributed temperature sensing and distributed acoustic sensing), have emerged as key tools for HF evaluation. Microseismic monitoring 
captures seismic events associated with fracturing, generating 3D fracture maps that reveal fracture orientation, length, and height. 
However, it suffers from resolution limitations, increasing uncertainty in complex fracture networks, and requires costly downhole mon-
itoring equipment (Li et al. 2021a; Fang et al. 2022). Distributed fiber- optic sensing provides real- time acoustic and thermal data along 
the wellbore, aiding in the detection of fracture initiation and fluid flow patterns; however, the high installation and data- processing costs 
limit its widespread application in HF projects (Huang and Liu 2017). These challenges have prompted the search for alternative or com-
plementary methods that are both cost- effective and applicable to real- time operations.
WHA has recently emerged as a promising alternative for real- time fracture diagnostics. The water hammer effect, which generates 
transient pressure waves following sudden changes in flow within a closed system, offers insights into fracture characteristics when ana-
lyzed during HF operations (Holzhausen and Gooch 1985). During HF, abrupt pump shutoff induces pressure pulses that travel through 
the wellbore and dissipate within the reservoir. This phenomenon allows for the estimation of fracture length, complexity, and potentially 
proppant placement by analyzing the wave behavior. Mondal (2010) introduced a semi- analytical model using the method of characteris-
tics to quantify the water hammer effect, which incorporates wellbore and reservoir impedance to predict fracture properties. Later, Carey 
et al. (2015) expanded this model, demonstrating that water hammer signals can reveal critical fracture parameters, including fracture 
length and complexity, particularly in scenarios involving multiple fractures. While WHA provides valuable insights, it may not capture 
all fracture dynamics comprehensively.
Traditional analysis methods, such as rate transient analysis (RTA) and pressure transient analysis (PTA), are widely used for well 
performance evaluation. RTA primarily focuses on analyzing production data to infer formation permeability and fracture effectiveness 
over time (Wattenbarger et al. 1998; El- Banbi and Wattenbarger 1998). However, RTA requires a sufficiently long production history and 
is highly sensitive to production rate variations, making it difficult to apply in early production stages. Moreover, RTA alone cannot pro-
vide direct information on reservoir pressure, which limits its ability to accurately characterize early- time reservoir behavior. PTA, on the 
other hand, relies on shut- in pressure buildup tests to estimate reservoir properties. It provides crucial insights into reservoir connectivity 
and formation permeability but is limited by the need for controlled shut- in conditions. Furthermore, in unconventional reservoirs where 
fluid mobility is low, pressure buildup tests may take an extended period to yield meaningful results, delaying decision- making processes

(Anderson et al. 2010; Chu et al. 2017). To overcome these limitations, UTA addresses this limitation by integrating RTA and into a single 
framework. Unlike conventional methods that treat production and shut- in data separately, UTA utilizes all available transient data in a 
unified manner, allowing for a more accurate evaluation of reservoir and fracture properties without requiring prior knowledge of initial 
reservoir pressure (Wang et al. 2022). This approach enables a holistic interpretation of flow regimes and fracture behavior, particularly 
in unconventional plays where traditional diagnostic methods may be inadequate. The UTA provides a unique advantage in estimating the 
effective fracture surface area that actively contributes to hydrocarbon production. By integrating production and shut- in data, UTA cap-
tures the dynamic behavior of the reservoir and fractures, focusing on the portion of the fracture network that effectively contributes to 
flow. During production, the pressure drop across the fracture network reflects the connectivity and conductivity of the fractures, while 
shut- in data provides additional insights into the reservoir’s ability to recharge the fractures. This holistic approach ensures that only the 
fractures contributing to production are accounted for, offering critical insights for optimizing HF designs and improving 
well performance.
Our aim with this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of HF in the Gulong shale oil field by integrating WHA and UTA, both applied 
to the same horizontal well. WHA is used to estimate stage- level fracture geometry and oscillation behavior immediately after shut- in, 
while UTA is used to quantify the dynamic flow contributing area over time. The analysis reveals that fracture length and complexity 
exhibit moderate positive correlations with gamma ray index, indicating that more organic- rich and clay- rich intervals tend to generate 
longer and more complex fractures. Furthermore, the effective fracture surface area derived from UTA accounts for only 47% of the total 
area estimated by WHA, implying that a substantial portion of the stimulated volume does not contribute to production. By jointly inter-
preting WHA and UTA results, we can assess what proportion of the created fracture area remains conductive under specific treatment and 
operational conditions, offering valuable guidance for optimizing stimulation design and improving fracture use efficiency.
Gulong Shale Oil Field
Gulong Shale Oil Formation Overview. The Gulong shale oil formation, located in the Songliao Basin in northeastern China, represents 
one of the most prolific unconventional oil reservoirs in the country, with complex geological characteristics that make it both a significant 
resource and a technical challenge for HF (Sun et al. 2021, 2023; Ji et al. 2024). The Songliao Basin, covering approximately 260,000 
km2, is the largest continental hydrocarbon basin in China and has undergone four major tectonic evolution stages since the Jurassic, 
including thermal uplift and rifting, extensional faulting, thermal subsidence, and tectonic inversion (Fig. 1a) (Li et al. 2021b). These 
tectonic processes, along with varying sedimentation rates, have contributed to the basin’s diverse structural units, which include the 
northern and southern depressions, northeastern and southeastern uplifts, and the central depression—the primary location of the Gulong 
shale oil deposits (Fig. 1b) (Wang et al. 2016).
Fig. 1—(a) Location of the central depression, (b) shale oil wells distributed in the Gulong Sag, and (c) stratigraphic column of the 
Upper Cretaceous Formation in the Songliao Basin. The contours of vitrinite reflectance (Ro) of the K2qn1 organic matter (OM) 
were modified after Sun et al. (2021).
Composed mainly of the Cretaceous Qingshankou Formation, the Gulong shale oil reservoir is characterized by high organic content, 
thick shale intervals, and complex pore structures. With shale content exceeding 95%, the formation exhibits interbedded layers of dolo-
mite, shell limestone, and siltstone, each only a few centimeters thick, contributing to its low permeability and significant heterogeneity 
(Fig. 1c) (Feng et al. 2010). The clay mineral content, which ranges from 25 to 40% with an average of 35.6%, primarily consists of illite 
with minor amounts of montmorillonite below 1650 m depth (Zhang 2021). The presence of these clay minerals influences fracture prop-
agation, as higher clay content often correlates with increased ductility, potentially limiting fracture extension under 
hydraulic stimulation.

Pore structures in the formation are intricate, ranging from nano- to micron- scale pores combined with microfractures. Matrix pores, 
typically ranging from 10 nm to 50 nm in diameter, include intergranular mineral pores, clay pores, organic matter pores, and dissolution 
pores, with a median pore size between 20 nm and 30 nm (Sun et al. 2024). Field emission scanning electron microscopy has revealed 
that bedding fractures are approximately 50–150 nm wide, while intergranular pores and microfractures form a network that facilitates 
fluid flow within the shale matrix. This intricate pore system, combined with the high organic matter content, presents a favorable envi-
ronment for hydrocarbon accumulation but also poses challenges for efficient fracture connectivity and fluid extraction.
Stratigraphic sequences in the Songliao Basin range from 3000 m to 7000 m thick, comprising formations from the Triassic through 
the Neogene, overlaying a metamorphic basement. The Gulong shale is particularly notable for its high total organic carbon content, 
averaging 2.35%, which provides a significant hydrocarbon source. Additionally, the brittle mineral components, including dolomite and 
calcite, contribute to the brittleness index of approximately 50%, making the shale moderately responsive to fracturing. The high brittle-
ness index aids in fracture initiation, while the clay- rich, ductile interbeds may restrict fracture propagation, resulting in a complex frac-
ture network essential for enhancing well connectivity and production efficiency (Liu et al. 2019; Sun et al. 2023).
The combination of these unique geological properties within the Gulong shale oil formation necessitates tailored HF approaches to 
optimize hydrocarbon recovery. Understanding the interplay between clay and brittle minerals, pore structure, and organic matter distri-
bution is crucial for designing effective fracturing treatments that maximize fracture connectivity and stimulate productive fractures. In 
this study, insights from WHA and UTA provide critical evaluations of fracture characteristics in this complex reservoir, guiding strategies 
for optimizing production from the Gulong shale oil formation.
A-Well Overview. A- Well is a representative horizontal producer within the Gulong Sag of the central depression. The well targets 
the Q9 member of the Qingshankou Formation and was drilled to a measured depth of 4630 m with a long horizontal section in Q9. 
Petrophysical partitioning defines 21 pay subintervals totaling 2080 m, including 16 Type I- 1 layers (2000 m cumulative length) and five 
Type I- 2 layers (78.9 m), reflecting a high- quality but laminated reservoir architecture (Sun et al. 2023). Core- and log- constrained rock 
physics indicate an average brittleness index near 51% with effective porosity of 7–8%, consistent with moderate- to- high fracturability 
while preserving sufficient matrix storage (Li et al. 2021b).
Reservoir and fluid conditions used in subsequent modeling are derived from high- pressure pressure/volume/temperature in adjacent 
Q9 wells and on- site measurements: in- situ oil density of 0.564 g/cm³, oil viscosity of 0.26 mPa·s, formation volume factor of 1.9, gas/
oil ratio of 397.36 m³/t, and bubblepoint pressure of 30.811 MPa. The pressure coefficient is 1.51, indicating an overpressured system 
favorable for sustained conductivity after stimulation (Liu et al. 2019).
Completion used casedhole multistage HF with 45 stages and 178 clusters to maximize reservoir contact within Q9 (Zhang 2021). 
Shut- in and early flowback were monitored with an automatic surface pressure logger synchronized with production rate records, yielding 
good quality data required by both methods: WHA interprets the immediate shut- in oscillatory response to infer fracture complexity and 
a stimulated surface area Af,WHA; UTA interprets the variable rate production history to estimate the effective contributing fracture area 
Af,WHA from linearflow slope analysis.
A pressure decline and water hammer data example is provided in Fig. 2. These figures illustrate the multistage fracturing layout, 
pressure response, and production trends observed in Stage 1, serving as representative examples for multistage fracturing response across 
the well. Fig. 3 further presents production data, showing both the time vs. production plot and the time vs. pressure plot. These figures 
collectively illustrate the well’s production trends and the relationship between pressure and production rates across different stages, 
providing essential data for subsequent analysis. The pressure and production data will be further analyzed using WHA and UTA to eval-
uate fracture effectiveness and refine production optimization strategies for the Gulong shale oil field.

Carey et al. (2015) suggested that the wellbore fracture boundary condition proposed by Mondal (2010) in the case of single- stage 
multicluster fracturing is to equate multiple fracture effects into a single fracture. Carey et al. (2015) found a strong correlation between 
the water hammer model and the fracture lengths explained by microseismicity. Iriarte et al. (2017) investigated the decay rate of water 
hammer. The energy absorbed by the pressure pulse as it passes through the fracture network was considered, and the larger the fracture 
network, the greater the water hammer attenuation. Thereafter, Hwang et al. (2017) investigated the effect of each parameter in the RCI 
model on the water hammer signal and applied the RCI model to multicluster fracturing. Even though WHA is used and compared with 
other diagnostic methods (Haustveit et al. 2017) during and after hydraulic- fracture propagation, no work has been done that can relate 
WHA to post- production behavior.
Although the geometry of hydraulic fractures can be complex at the near- wellbore scale, such as in en echelon or branching patterns, 
multiple studies have demonstrated that on a reservoir scale, fracture geometry is often planar in many cases. Shahri et al. (2021) used 
high- resolution fiber- optic measurements combined with fracture modeling to demonstrate that fracture swarms in HF treatments often 
exhibit planar, vertically contained, and parallel geometries across multiple stages. Similar conclusions were drawn from the Hydraulic 
Fracturing Test Site core- through project, which revealed that induced fractures were predominantly planar and subparallel, reflecting a 
consistent propagation direction (Gale et al. 2014). Shahri et al. (2021) compared waterfall plot simulations and fiber- optic data, revealing 
that hydraulic fractures can be very well modeled as nearly linear cracks. Furthermore, microseismic monitoring during HF operations 
has shown that fractures generally align with the maximum horizontal stress and exhibit planar propagation patterns (Alalli and Zoback 
2018). Cao et al. (2025) integrated microseismic data and numerical modeling and found that almost all hydraulic fractures propagate 
along maximum horizontal stress with an average azimuth of NE 83° in a Gulong shale oil well. Liu et al. (2023) analyzed and tested core 
samples from the Gulong shale formation, which is characterized by high clay mineral content (average 46.6 %) and strong plasticity, thus 
favorable to planar fracture geometry. Therefore, while acknowledging small- scale complexity, the assumption of planar fracture geom-
etry remains reasonable for modeling and interpreting fracture behavior in the Gulong shale formation. In A- Well, the number of clusters 
per stage varies from 3 to 5, except for Stage 1, which contains only a single cluster. Consequently, the multifracture RCI model was 
applied to all stages except Stage 1, where the single- fracture model was used. This differentiation ensures accurate fracture geometry 
estimation based on cluster configuration.

Uncertainty Analysis. The discrepancy between fracture surface areas estimated by WHA and UTA can also arise from multiple 
sources of uncertainty, including model assumptions, measurement quality, reservoir heterogeneity, and fundamental differences in the 
physical behavior that each method captures. Understanding these limitations is critical to interpreting diagnostic results and guiding 
stimulation optimization.
Model Assumptions. WHA typically assumes planar, symmetrical, biwing fractures with uniform height, which may oversimplify the 
true geometry of multicluster fractures in horizontal wells. These assumptions can lead to overestimations of fracture dimensions, espe-
cially in zones affected by asymmetric growth or fracture interaction. UTA, by contrast, does not explicitly define fracture geometry but 
infers the effective contributing area from flow behavior, often assuming linear or bilinear flow regimes. When nonlinear flow, delayed 
connectivity, or progressive fracture closure is present, UTA may underestimate the area actively participating in production.
Measurement Errors. Both WHA and UTA are sensitive to data quality. Errors in bottomhole pressure reconstruction, pressure- 
dependent in fluid properties (e.g., viscosity and compressibility), and fluctuations in surface production rates or choke settings can intro-
duce inaccuracies. In WHA, these uncertainties influence the estimation of fracture length and height from pressure oscillations. In UTA, 
they affect the identification of flow regimes and slope determination used to calculate effective fracture area.
Reservoir Heterogeneity. The Gulong shale formation exhibits substantial heterogeneity in lithology, rock properties, and in- situ stress 
distribution. These characteristics influence both fracture propagation and fluid flow paths, leading to spatial variability that cannot be 
fully captured by idealized models. As a result, the same diagnostic method may yield different results in different stratigraphic intervals, 
even under similar operational conditions.
Fundamental Physical Differences. WHA captures the total hydraulic footprint of the fracture system immediately after shut- in—
specifically, all zones that responded to pressure wave propagation regardless of long- term conductivity. Because pressure waves travel 
rapidly and are unaffected by proppant distribution in the short term, WHA may reflect noncontributing or poorly connected fractures. 
In contrast, UTA reflects only the effectively contributing area—that is, portions of the fracture system that remain open, propped, and 
hydraulically connected over time. This distinction explains why UTA often produces smaller but more production- relevant estimates.
These differences are illustrated in this study, where the UTA- derived effective fracture area was approximately 47% of the area esti-
mated by WHA (Fig. 14). This implies that less than one- half of the hydraulically stimulated volume ultimately contributes to production, 
providing a practical metric for evaluating proppant placement efficiency and fracture utilization. Such ratios can help diagnose treatment 
quality and guide decisions on refracturing or well spacing. These findings are consistent with previous studies. For example, Xu et al. 
(2015) used a material balance approach based on flowback data and found that the recovered volume was significantly less than the 
estimated created volume. Similarly, Mayerhofer et al. (2010) reported that although microseismic mapping could delineate the total 
stimulated reservoir volume, only a fraction of that volume was conductive and contributed to sustained flow, due to nonuniform proppant 
distribution, insufficient fracture conductivity, or disconnected flow paths. In summary, integrating WHA and UTA provides a more com-
prehensive understanding of fracture effectiveness. WHA defines the spatial extent of initial stimulation, while UTA evaluates its produc-
tive impact. These uncertainties are rooted in specific geological and engineering conditions. For example, strong stress contrasts between 
layers or the presence of natural fractures can lead to asymmetric fracture growth, which deviates from the planar assumptions of WHA. 
Proppant distribution in high- viscosity fluids may result in uneven conductivity, affecting UTA- derived area estimates. Surface flow rate 
fluctuations or choke control changes introduce signal noise that can interfere with pressure derivative stability. Additionally, the estima-
tion of R, C, and I parameters is highly sensitive to the shut- in response duration and signal resolution. Recognizing these dependencies 
allows engineers to identify high- risk uncertainty sources and improve operational planning, such as using a supervisory control and data 
acquisition system for automatic flow measurement, tailoring injection, and shut- in schedules, and ensuring high- fidelity pressure data 
acquisition. This low- cost dual approach enhances diagnostic reliability and supports more effective HF design and reservoir management 
in unconventional formations.

provides a quantitative measure of fracture utilization. These results are then correlated with geological (such as logging data and seismic 
data) and design parameters (such as cluster spacing, perforation number, injection rate, fracturing fluid viscosity, and injection volume). 
Based on the diagnosis, treatment designs can be iteratively refined to improve proppant placement, fracture connectivity, and overall

Conclusions
With this study, we present a dual- method framework that integrates WHA and UTA to evaluate hydraulic- fracture effectiveness in the 
Gulong shale oil reservoir. WHA captures the initial extent and complexity of the fracture network immediately after shut- in while frac-
tures area open, whereas UTA quantifies the long- term contributing surface area during production while fracture area is closed. The 
combination of these two diagnostics offers a more complete picture of both stimulation scale and productive impact.
Field application demonstrates that the UTA- derived effective fracture area was approximately 47% of the WHA- estimated area, high-
lighting a significant discrepancy between the total stimulated volume and the portion that ultimately contributes to production. This 
finding emphasizes the importance of evaluating not only the created fracture geometry but also its sustained conductivity.
The proposed integrated approach is cost- effective, nonintrusive, and relies solely on routinely acquired pressure and production data. 
A practical workflow is established for stimulation optimization, including data acquisition, diagnostic interpretation, and treatment 
design feedback. By applying this framework across multiple wells, operators can optimize HF design iteratively to maximize stimulation 
effectiveness and improve well performance.
