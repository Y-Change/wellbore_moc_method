ABSTRACT
The diagnostic of hydraulic fractures is vital to the exploitation of subsurface resource. Diagnostic 
technique for hydraulic fracture based on the water-hammer pressure have been gradually 
highlighted owing to their cost effectiveness and simplicity. The present diagnostic models 
overlook the effects of fluid leak-off and natural fracture in hydraulic fractures, and it is limited 
for application in naturally fractured reservoirs. In this study, the location and number of hy -
draulic fractures are first obtained through the enhancement and cepstrum processing of a water- 
hammer signal. Subsequently, the water-hammer pressure within the wellbore is calculated by 
solving the continuity and momentum equations for the fluid. Wellbore and hydraulic fractures 
are considered as a hydraulic system. To estimate the fracture dimension, flow boundary con -
ditions are imposed to the fluid leak-off, interactions between natural and hydraulic fractures, 
and multifracture stress shadows. The results show that the fracturing shut-in method can be 
appropriately adjusted to avoid large pressure pulsations, which damage well integrity, and to 
obtain a clear water-hammer signal for fracture diagnosis. Natural fractures reduce the hydraulic 
fracture dimensions but facilitate the creation of complex fracture networks, while this 
complexity cannot be increased indefinitely. The minimum horizontal stress decreases the frac -
ture dimension and a greater difference in the horizontal stress renders it easier for hydraulic 
fractures to cross natural fractures to create larger dimensions. The field study shows the opti -
misation measures can be recommended based on the diagnostic results.

permeability of reservoirs [ 1 , 2 ]. Hydraulic fracture dimensions must be assessed via fracturing-diagnostic techniques, which are 
essential for evaluating the treatment performance, optimising the treatment method, and guiding reservoir engineering design. The

most typically utilised hydraulic fracture-diagnostic techniques in reservoirs include microseismic modelling, distributed fibre-optic 
sensing, wide-field electromagnetic methods, tracers, wellbore imaging, and well testing [ 3 – 6 ]. However, these methods may be 
cost prohibitive and may not necessarily achieve non-intrusive real-time monitoring. A water-hammer signal is a period of pressure 
pulsation obtained on a surface after fracturing shut-in and contains information regarding the wellbore and hydraulic fracture. 
Therefore, diagnostic methods based on the wellhead water-hammer pressure are gradually highlighted owing to their cost effec -
tiveness and simplicity [ 7 – 9 ].
Holzhausen [ 10 ] initially introduced the basic principles for analysing the dimensions of hydraulic fractures through surface- 
pressure pulsation. The dimensions of a hydraulic fracture and its connectivity to the bottomhole affect the hydraulic pulsation 
properties within the wellbore. Measuring this effect is an uncomplicated and cost-effective diagnostic method for identifying the 
associated hydraulic fracture. Holzhausen [ 10 ] developed a hydraulic representation of a fracture crossing a bottomhole by incor -
porating two combined factors: flow resistance and capacitive fracture. Field measurements of hydraulic impedance testing in an 
injection well further validated the feasibility of using the water-hammer pulsation pressure for fracturing diagnostics [ 11 , 12 ]. 
Subsequently, researchers have developed and applied this method. Yew [ 13 ] expanded the method by applying a Fourier inverse 
transform to wellhead data to estimate the fracture dimension. Patzek [ 14 ] proposed a model for lossy transmission lines that 
incorporated the energy depletion of pressure waves to characterise the dimensions of a wellbore and fractures. Furthermore, Carey 
[ 7 , 15 ] suggested a model for resistance – capacitance – inertance ( RCI ) fractures, which was characterised based on the resistance, 
capacitance, and inertance via the analogy of hydraulic and electrical systems. The RCI model was used as a boundary condition in 
wellbore water-hammer pressure calculation, thus enabling the diagnosis of fractures with the same dimensions. However, the fracture 
lengths estimated under the water-hammer RCI mode exceeded those calculated using alternative techniques (microseismic, elec -
tromagnetic imaging, pressure interference testing, and long-term production data analysis), although they were comparable to those 
derived based on microseismic stimulated reservoirs volumes and the fracture complexity index [ 16 , 17 ]. This is because the mech -
anisms of water-hammer signals and the associated characteristic parameters ( R , C , and I ) are complex. Furthermore, they are related 
to factors such as the types of wells completed, the wellbore dimensions, the fluid properties, and the physical properties of the rocks 
surrounding the fracture [ 8 , 18 , 19 ].
The RCI model is based on the ideal assumptions that the fractures are uniformly and efficiently propagated, the fracturing fluid 
leak-off during water hammer is zero, and the hydraulic fracture is a symmetric biplane. The leak-off of fracturing fluid is the main 
factor that determines the hydraulic fracture dimension [ 20 ]. Therefore, additional enhancements are required. In study of [ 18 ], a two- 
parameter fracture boundary condition model considering fracturing fluid leak-off was proposed to estimate the fracture dimensions. 
The leak-off characteristics were determined by fitting the end curve of the water hammer, and the net pressure drop at the bottomhole 
was defined as a function of the leak-off volume. Hwang [ 21 ] reported that stress shadowing between multifracture changed the stress 
distribution, thus affecting the fracture-dimension diagnostics from water-hammer analysis. Therefore, Hu [ 22 ] proposed a multi -
fracture diagnostic model by first considering the stress shadow between hydraulic fractures using the RCI fracture model, and then 
determined whether the hydraulic fractures were effectively propagated by assessing the positive and negative average net pressures in 
multifracture. However, this process does not consider the effect of fracturing fluid leak-off and natural fractures on the fracture di -
mensions, which is limited for application in naturally fractured reservoirs.

Resources in unconventional reservoirs such as shale oil and gas have become important sources of fossil energy [ 27 , 28 ]. Un -
conventional reservoirs are mostly naturally fractured reservoirs. Ultralow permeability and the wide distribution of natural fractures 
are the two main characteristics of unconventional reservoirs [ 29 – 31 ]. Therefore, multicluster fracturing of horizontal wells has 
become a key technique for increasing stimulated reservoir volume [ 32 – 34 ]. However, studies showed that not every perforation 
cluster is opened to allow the propagation of hydraulic fractures owing to the inhomogeneous perforation quality, reservoirs prop -
erties, and stress [ 35 , 36 ]. Meanwhile, hydraulic fractures were shown to propagate with different dimensions because of the stress 
shadow of multiple clusters and the inhomogeneity of reservoirs mechanical and in-situ stresses [ 37 , 38 ]. In addition, natural fractures 
significantly affected the propagation and hydraulic fracture dimensions. Hydraulic fractures can open or penetrate natural fractures, 
thus creating complex fracture networks and greater pressure loss, and the fracturing fluid can enter natural fractures, thereby 
significantly increasing the leak-off volume [ 2 , 32 , 39 ]. In water-hammer studies, one must determine the location and number of 
hydraulic fractures resulting from the opening of perforation clusters, in addition to the effect of natural fractures. In conclusion, 
although the fracturing diagnostic technique based on water-hammer pressure analysis has been successfully applied, its reliability in 
identifying fractures in naturally fractured reservoirs is questionable, owing to some limitations of the current model. Therefore, 
developing a diagnostic model for multiple hydraulic fractures in naturally fractured reservoir is essential.
In the abovementioned diagnostic models, the effects of fluid leak-off and natural fractures were overlooked, thus rendering it 
challenging to map hydraulic fractures in naturally fractured reservoir. In this study, a diagnostic model considering the location, 
number, and dimensions of hydraulic fractures is developed. The location and number of hydraulic fractures are first obtained through 
the enhancement and cepstrum processing of a wellhead high frequency water-hammer signal. Subsequently, the wellbore water- 
hammer pressure is calculated by solving fluid-continuity and momentum equations. An RC I multifracture dimension estimation 
model is developed by coupling the fracturing fluid leak-off, natural and hydraulic fracture interactions, and multifracture stress 
shadows. Water-hammer pressures with different shut-in modes and multifracture dimensions with different perforation designs, 
natural fracture densities, and in-situ horizontal stresses are investigated. Finally, a case study of a field shale well is performed to 
demonstrate the application process and performance of our model.

2. Methodology
2.1. Physical model and water-hammer pressure signal
Fracturing fluid was injected into a deep reservoir via a pipe string as the working fluid during the fracturing treatment process to 
create multiple hydraulic fractures for enhancing the fluid permeability. Following the shut-in of the fracturing treatment, a water 
hammer was formed, and the pressure pulsation signal was subsequently measured at the surface. The generation and characteristics of 
the water-hammer signals must be clarified for fracture diagnosis. Water hammer occurs when the flow rate changes abruptly owing to 
valve opening or closing in the pipeline, thus resulting in a transient change in the fluid pressure system. The pressure wave dissipates 
through the pipe and decays continuously under the effects of viscous forces, flow resistance, and other forces. Fig. 1 presents a 
schematic illustration of the fracturing fluid flow and water-hammer signal. The symbols a , L , t , u

2.2. Multifracture diagnostic model
A diagnostic model for multifracture and its solutions are shown in Fig. 2 . First, the number and location of the hydraulic fractures 
were obtained via the enhancement and cepstrum analysis of the surface high frequency water-hammer signal. Subsequently, the 
water hammer was simulated using a multifracture RCI model that considered fluid leak-off in the fractures, natural fractures, and 
stress shadows as boundary conditions. The pressure resulting from the water hammer in the wellbore was simulated numerically 
through the solution of fluid-continuity and momentum equations using the method of characteristics ( MOC ). Finally, RCI values 
obtained iteratively were used to estimate the multifracture dimension. The model adheres to the following basic assumptions: 
(1) The flow is isothermal and disregards the heat transfer among the fluid, formation, and wellbore, which implies that the 
physical properties of the fluid (density and viscosity) are constant.
(2) A single perforation cluster initiates a solitary hydraulic fracture, thus suggesting that the number and location of perforation 
clusters with the absorbing fluid correspond to the number and location of the propagated hydraulic fractures, respectively.
(3) Each effectively propagated hydraulic fracture is a constant-height planar fracture with an elliptical cross-section. The distri -
bution of natural fractures is consistent across different two-dimensional spaces.

perforation spacing. Consequently, the lengths of the first and final fractures exhibited different variation patterns at small perforation

Fig. 14 illustrates the fracture dimensions with different densities of natural fracture (0 – 0.2 number/m

the increased number of natural fractures. The volume and pressure of the fracturing fluid that caused hydraulic fractures decreased. 
Consequently, the fracture dimensions decreased. Additionally, the fracture dimension in the naturally fractured reservoir was smaller 
than that in conventional reservoir. The variation gradients of fractures 1 and 4 were greater than those of fractures 2 and 3 because 
their larger fracture volumes strengthened the effect of leak-off and natural fractures. Meanwhile, whereas the natural fractures 
decreased the hydraulic fracture length, more natural fractures opened and propagated as their density increased.
It is shown that while increasing natural fracture density reduces the length of hydraulic fractures, it also promotes the opening and 
propagation of more natural fractures. This resulted in a hydraulic fracture with a complex fracture network, which implies that a more 
stimulated volume is available for fracturing treatment. However, as the hydraulic fracture length decreases, the probability of 
opening a natural fracture decreases as well. The sum of the opened natural fractures decreased at a density of 0.2 number/m

an additional volume of opened natural fractures, and that the development of natural fractures in the reservoir did not increase the 
complexity of hydraulic fractures indefinitely. It is because that higher fracture resistance plays a critical role in fracture propagation 
dynamics. Specifically, higher resistance in natural fractures means that more energy is required to open and propagate them. 
Consequently, more natural fractures are opened, but the hydraulic fracture dimensions become smaller due to the increased energy 
dissipation.

3.2.3. Horizontal stress
Horizontal stress is the main factor that must be overcome to ensure the propagation of hydraulic fractures. The minimum hori -
zontal stress determines the net fracture pressure, and the horizontal stress difference affects the interaction mode between the hy -
draulic and natural fractures. In this section, we investigate the fracture dimensions under different horizontal stresses, with the 
maximum/minimum horizontal stresses set to 50/45, 60/50, 70/55, and 80/60 MPa. The minimum horizontal stress and horizontal 
stress difference were increased progressively for the calculation cases. The results are shown in Fig. 15 . The fracture length initially 
increased and then decreased. This phenomenon is attributable to the alteration in the net fracture pressure caused by the conjunction 
of the minimum horizontal stress and natural fracture pressure drop, which can be determined using Eqs. (31) and (32) , respectively. 
An increase in the minimum horizontal stress resulted in a shorter fracture length. Meanwhile, a greater horizontal stress difference 
facilitated the propagation of hydraulic fractures across natural fractures, thus resulting in fewer opened and propagated natural 
fractures and less additional pressure drops from natural fractures, as depicted in Fig. 15 (b). This phenomenon facilitates the real -
isation of larger fracture dimensions. In addition, the effect of the minimum horizontal stress on the fracture length was more severe 
than that of the horizontal stress difference under a high minimum horizontal stress. For instance, the average natural fracture pressure 
drop of the E-frac was only 1.49 MPa, with a minimum horizontal stress and horizontal stress difference of 60 and 20 MPa, respectively. 
Therefore, reservoir stress must be considered in the diagnosis of fractured reservoir.
3.3. Field case study
The multifracture diagnostic model was evaluated for its application and performance during the completion of shale horizontal- 
well fracturing in the Sichuan Basin, China. A staged multicluster fracturing technique was employed in the horizontal section. Logging 
and microseismic monitoring data indicated the development of natural fractures in the reservoir. Meanwhile, a staged multicluster 
fracturing technique was employed in the horizontal section, in which the interval was uniformly perforated with a 10 m cluster 
spacing and then isolated with mechanical bridge plugs. A high frequency pressure sensor was installed at the wellhead to obtain the 
water-hammer signal during fracturing shut-in. The main input parameters of the diagnostic model are listed in Table 4 . The theo -
retical calculation of the wate-hammer wave speed was performed according to Eq. (7) . The water-hammer wave speed was also 
adjusted by the period time of occurrence of the water-hammer pulsation at the wellhead.
Initially, the number and location of hydraulic fractures were analysed via CEEM enhancement and a cepstrum process on a high 
frequency water-hammer signal. The well was designed with six perforation clusters at a depth of 6102 – 6165 m. The stimulation result 
showed negative peaks appearing at the quefrency area of 7964 – 8004 ms (depth of 6116 – 6147 m), as shown in Fig. 16 . This suggests 
that the perforation clusters placed within the depths of 6116 – 6147 m absorbed the fracturing fluid, whereas other clusters remained 
unopened, thereby inhibiting the creation of hydraulic fractures. Thus, the three perforation clusters between 6116 – 6147 m were 
regarded as the quantity and placement of hydraulic fractures. Later, these data will be used to assess the stress shadow and fracture 
dimensions.

4. Conclusion
In this study, a multifracture RCI diagnostic model for naturally fractured reservoir was proposed. To address some of the limi -
tations of the previous model, we employed the cepstrum of water hammer in our model to obtain the location and number of 
effectively propagated hydraulic fractures as well as considers the effects of leak-off and natural fractures. Wellhead water-hammer 
pressures with different fracturing shut-in modes and multifracture dimensions with different perforation designs, densities of natu -
ral fractures, and horizontal stresses were investigated. Finally, a case study on a field shale well was performed to demonstrate the 
application process and performance of our model. The primary conclusions obtained were as follows: 
(1) The water-hammer pressure pulsation was related to the shut-in time and displacement. A staged pump shutdown with 
extended shut-in times effectively prevented large pressure pulsations that damage the wellbore integrity. Similarly, the 
fracturing shut-in method can be appropriately modified to obtain a clear water-hammer signal for fracturing diagnostics.
(2) As the number of shot clusters increased, the fracture length and width decreased gradually, and the dimensions of the fracture 
in the centre were consistently smaller than those on both sides. As the perforation cluster spacing increased, the fracture on 
both sides decreased and then increased, the centre fracture increased continuously, and the dimensions of the multifracture 
eventually became uniform.
(3) Natural fractures can reduce hydraulic fracture dimensions. However, complex fracture networks should be created to increase 
the stimulated volume. The development of natural fractures in reservoir does not indefinitely increase the complexity of 
hydraulic fractures because the probability of natural fracture propagation is limited by the hydraulic fracture dimensions.
(4) An increase in the minimum horizontal stress decreased the fracture dimensions. Meanwhile, a greater difference in the hor -
izontal stress difference rendered it easier for a hydraulic fracture across a natural fracture to create a larger fracture dimension.
(5) Field fracture diagnostic results showed three propagated fractures at the locations of perforation clusters 3, 4, and 5. The 
fracture diagnostic dimensions are acceptable, based on a comparison with microseismic results, and thus can be used to 
improve the fracturing design.
