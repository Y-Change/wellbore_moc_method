A B S T R A C T
In this paper, time-reversal (TR) technique is proposed for the detection of discrete defects (e.g., leaks and
blockages) in pressurized fluid-filled pipelines using active transient waves. The proposed technique is based
on the one-dimensional wave equation and decouples the search for the location from that of the size of the
defect. Another key feature of this method is that the measured pressure head signal can be truncated so as to
eliminate undesirable reflections from unknown or uncertain parts of the pipe system so as not to compromise
the accuracy and robustness of the detection. The proposed method is tested through numerical simulations
for a single leak, multiple leaks, and multiple mixed-type of defects (i.e., leaks and blockages) in elastic as well
as visco-elastic pipe systems. The proposed method is also validated through laboratory experiments wherein
leaks and blockages of various sizes are considered. The sensitivity analysis of the method with respect to
wave speed and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is also performed. In all cases, the method has shown accurate
results in terms of localization, classification, and size estimation of the defects even for SNR as low as 0 dB.
1. Introduction
Freshwater pipelines are susceptible to defects such as leaks, block-
ages, malfunctioning devices, etc. Such defects are responsible for loss
of water, energy and financial resources ( Colombo and Karney, 2002).
According to the World Bank report, leaks in freshwater pipelines
are responsible for about 32 billion cubic meters of water loss per
year worldwide ( World Bank, 2016, pp. 43). To place this number in
some perspective, this amount of lost water could fill 12,800 Olympic
size swimming pools. To control such huge losses, development of
defect detection technologies is the pragmatic solution recommended
by many organizations concerned with water resources such as UN-
ESCO ( UNESCO, 2020 ) and Asian Development Bank ( Strazzabosco,
2020).
In past three decades, immense attention has been given by hy-
draulic researchers to develop transient-based defect detection methods
(TBDDMs; Che et al., 2021). These methods are based on the principle
that injected transient wave propagates through water column and
interacts with all physical features of the pipeline including defect
(if present). The transient response of the system is recorded through
pressure transducers which are mounted at several locations along the
pipe. The recorded data is then used for pipeline condition assessment.
In literature, a variety of TBDDMs have been proposed to extract
the defect information from the recorded transient signals. Some of
these methods directly analyze the measured signals using the wave
reflectometry principles (e.g., Brunone , 1999 ; Ferrante et al. , 2007 ;

Meniconi et al. , 2015). A number of closed-form analytical solutions
are also proposed in literature to extract defect information for simple
systems such as reservoir-pipe-reservoir, reservoir-pipe-valve, etc. (see
e.g., Mpesha et al., 2002; Lee et al., 2005; Sattar and Chaudhry, 2008;
Duan et al. , 2011 ; Louati et al. , 2020 , 2018 , 2017 ). A great deal of
attention has been devoted to the development of inverse techniques to
estimate the parameters of defects based on matching the measured and
modeled transient response (e.g., Liggett and Chen , 1994 ; Vitkovsky
et al., 2001; Kapelan et al., 2003; Covas and Ramos, 2010; Soares et al.,
2011; Wang and Ghidaoui, 2018b).
In theory, the various inverse methods are efficient given that: (i)
the defect type (leak/blockage) and number of defects are known a pri-
ori (Puust et al., 2010), (ii) the topology of the system and its physical
and geometrical properties are well-known and fully incorporated in
the hydraulic model (Stephens, 2008; Waqar et al., 2021), and (iii) the
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is large ( Wang and Ghidaoui, 2018b; Wang
et al. , 2020b ). In real-world, however, most of the foregoing condi-
tions are not met. Consequently, the application of existing TBDDMs
(e.g., the time-domain reflectometry, gradient-based optimization algo-
rithms, and/or resonant peak frequency pattern methods) is restricted
to simple pipelines with large SNR and known defect type.
The time reversal-based (TR) techniques are known to be robust
to noise and model uncertainties ( Tolstoy, 1993; Schmidt et al. , 1990;
Kuperman et al., 1998; Edelmann et al., 2002; Kuperman and Jackson,

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, the
TR technique for defect detection in pipelines is developed wherein
analytical models in both time and frequency domains are provided.
In this paper, only discrete defects such as leaks or discrete blockages
are considered. Second, numerical examples of single leak, multiple
leaks and mixed-type of defects (e.g., leak and discrete blockage) are
considered in a single transmission-like pipeline. Third, the proposed
method is validated through laboratory experiments under different
SNR values ranging between 10 to −7 dB. Finally, the conclusions of
this study are stated.

1.2. Time-reversal technique
The standard time-reversal technique is a two stage process. In
stage 1: a point source is injected into the system and the response
of the system is measured. For a pipe system in Fig. 1, the measured
pressure head response is denoted by 𝛥𝑚
(𝑡, 𝑥𝑅|𝜒)(i.e. Eq. (8)). In
stage 2, the measured response is first reversed (flipped) in time such

3.1. SNR
The influence of different values of SNR ranging between 10 dB
(𝜎 ≈ 0 .3|𝐴𝜒 |) to −5 dB ( 𝜎 ≈ 1 .8|𝐴𝜒 |) on the leak localization and leak

and vertical bars show the 95% confidence. It can be seen that for
SNR ≥ 0 dB, the absolute leak localization error is < 0.5𝜆min and the
absolute leak size error is ≤ 1 mm 2 for both the elastic and the visco-
elastic pipes. However, for SNR < 0 dB, leak localization accuracy is
relatively more affected for the visco-elastic pipe than the elastic pipe.
It is because the reflection coefficient of a leak in the visco-elastic pipe
is relatively smaller than that in the elastic pipe. Therefore, for a given
SNR, it is more difficult to detect a small leak in visco-elastic pipe than
in elastic pipe for a given incident wave. One possible treatment to
detect such small leaks under low SNR would be to conduct multiple
transient tests and obtain the average signal ( Wang et al., 2020b).

5. Conclusions
In this paper, the problem of defect detection in pressurized fluid-
filled pipelines is addressed. A time-reversal technique is proposed
for discrete defect detection of mixed-type such as leaks and discrete
blockages in the elastic and the visco-elastic pipe systems. The solution
for defect localization is based on time-reversal technique, and the
solution for defect type and its corresponding size estimate is obtained

defects which are 0.35−wavelength distance apart in both elastic and
visco-elastic pipes. Moreover, the stability of the proposed method is
investigated under various signal-to-noise ratios and uncertain wave
speed. For stability against noise, the SNR is varied between 10 dB
to −5 dB. The results show that the localization error in elastic pipe
remains within the interval of half wavelength for SNR ≥ −5 dB. For
visco-elastic pipe, however, the localization error is relatively larger for
SNR < 0 dB. For the case of uncertain wave speed, the results show that
the localization error remains within the half wavelength interval for
up to 10% uncertainty in wave speed. For visco-elastic pipe, however,
only 2.5% uncertainty in wave speed gives the reliable localization.
The proposed method is also validated with laboratory experiments.
In particular, six cases of single leak with variable size, three cases
of single discrete blockage with different head-loss coefficient and
three cases of two defects (a leak and a blockage) are considered. The
results show that for cases with SNR ≥ 0 dB, the proposed method
is quite accurate in terms of localization, defect type determination
and defect size estimation. For SNR < 0 dB, on the other hand,
the accuracy of the method is affected by the noise in the system.
This issue can be addressed by conducting multiple measurements and
taking the average pressure head response. Overall, the proposed TR
technique has shown excellent results to be considered for further
development.
