Abstract
The purpose of this work was to evaluate if frac water hammer signature characteristics are representative
of important hydraulic fracture and reservoir properties in a horizontal well and if those characteristics can
be used as inputs in a predictive Machine Learning model. Water hammer is an oscillatory pressure signal
generated as a result of an abrupt change in wellbore fluid velocity, for example at the end of a fracturing
treatment when the pump rate is quickly dropped to zero.
Authors developed an algorithm in python to automatically identify surface pressure and fluid pump
rate channels in the raw data, detect and flag end of pumping events, parse out the water hammer and
pressure decline signal from raw data. Numerical optimization algorithm was then used to approximate
water hammer characteristics from the pressure signal based on the modified damped sine wave equation.
The derived equation coefficients were used as inputs (features) in a Random Forest classification model to
classify individual fracture stage contributions to a horizontal well's production profile.
Production log tool (PLT) results from 8 horizontal unconventional wells and corresponding 1-second
field data for 78 hydraulic stage fracture treatments were used in the study. The water hammer characteristics
and parameters of the theoretical vs. actual curve match for each stage, such as initial amplitude of the
signal, decay rate, phase angle, angular frequency, number of peaks, etc. were used as features for modeling.
The data was split into 70 % −30 % train and test sets. A Random Forest Classifier model was trained on the
train set to classify individual fracture stages in a horizontal well as either contributing or non-contributing
to production. The model was validated against the test set with overall test classification accuracy of 0.71
and F1-Score of 0.72.
Based on the study the authors conclude that water hammer characteristics derived from surface pressure
signal via curve-matching technique can be useful for classification of fracture stage contribution to
production in a horizontal well.

Evaluations from previous papers and the scientific community consider the different wave properties to
showcase the elements of the created fracture network and wellbore connectivity (Iriarte et al. 2017, Hwang
et al. 2017). Modelling from Carey et al. (2015) first utilized and demonstrated the properties application to
the connection of the wellbore. In their work, Carey et al. developed a numerical model for simulating the
water hammer signatures. The wellbore with hydraulic fractures was represented by a circuit with resistance
(R), capacitance (C), and inertance (I) connected in series. Then, by iteratively changing the values of R, C,
and I, they obtained a match between field and simulated data. After a match was obtained, they performed a
sensitivity analysis to evaluate the effect of each parameter on the water hammer signal behavior. Based on
the analysis of simulations they concluded that water hammer pressure signatures are affected by the created
fractures. Iriarte et al. (2017) in their work analyzed a dataset of more than 100 wells in 3 different US plays.
They characterized water hammer signatures by calculating decay rate, amplitude, duration, and period.
To calculate the decay rate, they obtained a best fit of an exponential decay function to the water hammer
peaks (Eq. 1). The process of obtaining the best fit and deriving the decay rate coefficient is summarized in
Figure 1. Once the parameters were obtained, they evaluated wellbore, stimulation, and formation effects
separately by building correlations between water hammer parameters and the parameters of interest in each
of the separate parts. They found that amplitude is closely correlated with the number of perforations per
stage, pump rate, and friction, while the duration and decay rate correlate with fluid type, fluid volume,
and increased fracture network complexity. The wells and fracture treatments reviewed in our work all had
similar treatment designs, fluid types and wellbore construction, which enabled us to eliminate the wellbore
and frac fluid parameters and focus on possible formation and fracture network effects on water hammer
signal.

Methods
Water Hammer Signatures Analysis Workflow
Our study focuses on determining water hammer characteristics by applying an optimization algorithm and
obtaining a match between actual field data and a modeled curve based on a modified damped sine equation.
The dataset used in this work consisted of field treatment data for 210 frac stages from 17 horizontal
multi-stage wells performed by several frac service providers. A typical stage data file is represented by a
tabular format CSV or text file with multiple frac treatment data channels such as surface pressure, pump
rate, proppant concentration and other channels, recorded at 1-second intervals.
In the first phase of the analysis, we derived the water hammer signature parameters from the raw field
data. Although some stages had multiple pumps shut down events, our study focused on the end of treatment
water hammer signature for consistency. Some additional water hammer parameters, such as peaks, troughs
and water hammer duration were also taken based on the data during this phase.
To automate the workflow a python script was developed consisting of the following steps:
1. Load the treatment data and identify surface pressure and slurry rate data channels. A significant
challenge we faced during this step was inconsistency in data channel naming conventions by different
frac service providers. A word stemming/matching technique was used to recognize pressure and
rate channels although a more robust machine learning model will probably be required in order to
generalize and scale the workflow up.
2. Using a combination of a rule-based algorithm and machine-learning model, identify the end of
pumping events and parse out water hammer signature and pressure decline at the end of a frac stage
(Figure 2).
3. Identify peaks and troughs (local maximums and minimums) in the data and calculate water hammer
signature duration in seconds as time difference between the first and last pressure pulses (Figure 3).
4. Approximate water hammer signature parameters by applying a curve fitting algorithm.

For the fourth step a curve-fitting algorithm based on numerical optimization was used. As illustrated
in Figure 4, the water hammer signatures can vary greatly from stage to stage, even in similar wellbore
conditions. One of the objectives of this study was to build a workflow capable of capturing the variations
in water hammer pressure decline signatures and learn if these variations can be representative of hydraulic
fracture geometry or reservoir parameters.

Overall, water hammer signatures resemble a damped harmonic motion, the type of motion where the
energy is lost with each oscillation due to friction. Some examples of the damped harmonic motion are a
bouncing ball, a playground swing, a guitar string, etc. This type of motion can be described by a damped
sine wave equation (Equation 2).

Numerical optimization technique was used to obtain water hammer characteristics from the actual data
by applying trust-region least squares algorithm as described by Branch, Coleman and Li, 1999. Surface
pressure data was standardized by subtracting mean and dividing by standard deviation before applying the
numerical algorithm. By iteratively optimizing the damped sine wave equation parameters ( A, λ, ω, ϕ, m)
a match was obtained for every frac stage in the dataset and the curve parameters were recorded to create
a feature dataset for subsequent use in a machine learning model. Examples of matched actual field water
hammer data and modeled by the damped sine wave are provided in Figure 7. The surface pressure data
was scaled back to the original scale before graphing.
Figure 7—Examples of curve match of Damped Sine Wave and Actual field data, 2 frac stages.

An Example Application of Water Hammer Signature Analysis Using Machine Learning
In the second part of the study, we investigated if the water hammer characteristic were useful for analysis
of oil and gas well production performance. In order to do that we collected Production Logging Tool
(PLT) reports from 8 wells, for which we already had the frac stage data and had derived the water hammer
characteristics. The PLT reports contained well flow profiles versus measured depth and an interpretation
of individual stage contribution to the overall well production for a total of 78 frac stages. The dataset with
features created in the first part of the study was then divided into two classes based on individual stage
contribution. The stages with contribution less than 5% to the overall production were assigned to "Class
0″ or low-contribution stages while the stages contributing more than 5% were assigned to "Class 1″ or
high-contribution stages. The arbitrary cut-off of 5% was selected for class balancing purposes before using
the data in the classification machine learning model. In Figure 8 we show comparison of distributions of
different water hammer parameters for the two classes.
Overall, the water hammer signature parameters look similar for both classes. One notable observation
is that the distributions of values of amplitude, decay constant and angular frequency look more grouped
around the mean for "Class 1″ than for "Class 0″. Some other observations are higher median angular
frequency for Class 1, higher median slope for Class 1, slightly higher median decay constant for Class
1. Iriarte et al (2017)  identified a slightly positive correlation between decay rate and 30-day cumulative
oil production as wells as a moderate positive correlation between water hammer duration and 30-day
cumulative oil production, suggesting that fracture network size may influence the water hammer signal.
Ciezobka (2016) discussed that larger fracture networks absorb energy faster therefore leading to faster
decays and shorter water hammer duration. While in our study we also noticed a slightly faster water hammer
signature decays for high-contribution class, we did not observe significant differences in water hammer
durations for the two classes.

training and 24 observations for testing. Once the model was trained to classify stages based on the water
hammer characteristics, a prediction was made for every observation in the test set and prediction results
were compared to the actual class. In Figure 10 we display the Confusion Matrix calculated based on the
test data predictions.

Conclusions and Future Work
Authors developed a workflow to pre-process the raw field frac data, derive water hammer signature
characteristics by using a numerical optimization algorithm and then used those characteristics as input
parameters for a Machine Learning Model. The model was trained to classify the frac stages as either high-
contribution or low-contribution to the overall well production profile based on PLT results. The resulting
model accuracy of 0.708 and the F1-Score of 0.720 indicate that the water hammer signature characteristics
can be useful in analysis of fracture stage contribution to production in a horizontal well.
