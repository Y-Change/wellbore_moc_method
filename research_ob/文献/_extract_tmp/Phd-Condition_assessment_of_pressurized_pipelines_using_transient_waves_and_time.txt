Condition assessment of pressurized pipelines using
transient waves and time-reversal technique
by Muhammad WAQAR
Department of Civil Engineering
The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology
Abstract
The time-reversal (TR) of water hammer (WH) waves in a viscous fluid bounded by
a visco-elastic pipe is studied analytically and numerically. The quasi-steady part
of friction is represented by the Darcy–Weisbach relation while the unsteady part is
represented by the Vardy–Brown relation. The visco-elastic damping is represented
by the Generalized Kelvin–Voigt model. It is shown that damping does not hinder
the refocusing property of the time-reversed WH waves. In addition, it is analytically
shown that (i) refocusing of WH waves reaches the well-known diffraction limit in
physics, and (ii) TR is optimal in the sense of maximum signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
for white noise. Moreover, the TR technique is used to develop noise-tolerant (i)
model-based method (method 1), and (ii) model-free method (method 2) for condi-
tion assessment of pressurized pipes. Method 1 is a one-dimensional technique that
decouples the search for the location and strength of a defect (e.g., leak or blockage).
The applicability of method 1 to diagnose multiple defects in both elastic and poly-
meric (visco-elastic) pipe systems is demonstrated numerically and experimentally.
The results confirm that the proposed TR technique successfully detects defects with
a resolution equal to half-wavelength (i.e., achieves the theoretical diffraction limit of
waves). Method 1 is also successfully applied to localize real leaks in (i) a fire sprin-
kler system, located in HKUST, and (ii) a transmission main, located in Yuen Long,
Hong Kong. Method 2 uses TR to identify differences (if any) between a baseline
transient signal and a current (post-baseline) transient signal to localize the defects
responsible for the difference between the two signals. That is, method 2 detects
the defects that have emerged between the time the baseline measurements was con-
xxv

===== PAGE 26 =====
ducted and the current time. The applicability of method 2 is demonstrated using
numerical examples and experimental test cases in two laboratory-scale systems, a
field-scale transmission-like pipeline system, and using real-field data collected from
a district metered area (DMA), located in Ngau Tau Kok, Hong Kong. This technique
successfully detected a change in the DMA that was unbeknown to the author and the
water authority. This test case demonstrates the real-field applicability of method 2.
Both methods are non-iterative; hence, computationally efficient. Additionally, their
computational time increases linearly with the number of defects for both methods;
thus, they are scalable to large systems and are easily amenable to automation — a
fact that is demonstrated in the Water Research Resources Laboratory of HKUST. The
use of metamaterials to manipulate and control WH wave propagation and ultimately
achieve higher defect detection resolution is also explored. Metamaterial structures
are modeled by imposing periodic variation of the impedance along the length of the
pipe, where the modulation period is much smaller than the typical wavelength of
the WH waves. It is shown that the transient wave propagation in a pipe made of
metamaterial (named meta-pipe) is dispersive (i.e., nonlinear wavenumber-frequency
relation) and produces sub-wavelength resonance as well as spectral band-gaps. For
source localization using the TR technique, it is shown that the meta-pipe produces a
far superior resolution than the normal pipe.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 Background and motivation
Sustainable urban water supply systems (UWSS) are indispensable infrastructure of
urban cities. However, the current state of these complex and out-of-sight systems is
far from being sustainable. These systems are extensive in scale (e.g., ∼ 7000 km in
Hong Kong - HK, and ∼ 2.2 million miles in United States - US), largely inaccessible,
aging, and fraught with physical defects and operational inefficiencies, resulting in
unprecedented water and energy losses. These problems, thus, present a huge ob-
stacle in making cities sustainable, adaptive and carbon neutral. In HK, 150 million
m3/year of water is lost amounting to 15% of the supply with a cost in excess of HK$
800 millions. The water system has a huge carbon footprint and, in HK alone, is the
third largest energy consumer [ 11]. The American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE)
awarded America’s water infrastructure as C- grade and reported that 6000 million
gallons of treated water is being lost due to leaks every day, enough to fill around
9000 Olympic size swimming pools [ 22]. Worldwide, non-revenue water (NRW) runs
to 126 billion m 3/year, enough to supply 130 countries like HK, with a direct cost of
more than US$ 39 billions per year [ 33].
The most common pipeline defects are blockages [44], pipe wall corrosion [55] and leaks
(including bursts) [ 66, 77]. The presence of a blockage reduces the conveying capacity
of pipe, and consequently increases the energy-related costs. A corroded pipe wall
reduces the pipe strength and the water quality and is a potential place for a pipe
burst. Likewise, leaks cause waste of energy and scarce resources (e.g., water, chem-
icals used in the treatment process, etc.) [ 88, 99], and are the source of contamination
1

===== PAGE 33 =====
during low pressure [1010, 1111]. In fact, a large contribution to the aforementioned NRW
losses is associated with leaks (including bursts) in pipelines [ 22, 1212]. To reduce such
losses and make UWSS sustainable, development of defect detection technologies is
the pragmatic solution recommended by organizations such as UNESCO [ 1313, 1414],
Asian Development Bank [1515], and American Water Works Association [ 1616]. The fact
that the amount of NRW losses continues to increase is a clear indication that exist-
ing and commercially available methods such as visual inspection via CCTV cameras
[1717, 1818], listening sticks [ 1919], acoustic noise correlators [ 2020, 2121], Smart Ball technology
[2222] and SAHARA system [ 2323] are unable to curb these massive water and energy
losses. Therefore, pipeline condition assessment and defect detection is an active area
of research for both researchers and technology developers.
In the past three decades, condition assessment of pipelines by means of active tran-
sient waves has been studied extensively and promises to produce methods that are
cost effective, fast, and non-disruptive [ 2424–2727]. This approach has two primary steps:
step 1 — experiments, and step 2 — theoretical analysis. In the experimental step,
a transient (pressure) wave is generated by means of flow perturbation (e.g., pump
shutdown or rapid valve maneuver). The injected transient wave propagates inside
the pipe at a speed of about 1200 m.s −1 in steel pipe, or around 400 m.s −1 in poly-
meric pipes. As the wave propagates, it interacts with all physical features of the
pipeline including the defects (if present). The transient response of the system is
recorded using pressure transducers, mounted at one or more locations along the
pipeline. Since the defect characteristics are encoded on the recorded signals, the the-
oretical analysis step is an inverse problem wherein the defect properties (type, size,
location, frequency) are inferred from the recorded responses. Such inverse problem
is highly ill-posed [ 2828–3030]. In the literature, a variety of transient-based defect detec-
tion methods (TBDDMs) have been proposed to solve the inverse problem which are
briefly reviewed in next section.
1.2 Common TBDDMs
The common methodologies to solve the inverse problem for pipeline defect detection
application are: (a) inverse transient analysis method [ 66, 3131–3636]; (b) impulse response

processing method [3030, 4848, 5353–5555].

matching the modeled11 and measured transient response of the system. The matching
is achieved by minimizing the least-square error type function, defined as

P . Liou [3737] for leak detection application in pressurized pipelines using hydraulic
transients. In [ 3737], the author used a pseudo-random binary sequence (PRBS) with
amplitude at noise level and showed that the IRF can be extracted by cross-correlating

Transient reflection-based methods (TRMs): B. Brunone [4141] and L. Jönsson [ 7878, 7979] were
among the first researchers who proposed this class of methods to detect leaks in
pressurized pipelines. The TRM is based on the principle of time domain reflectom-

by tD, is estimated by analyzing the pressure-time signals. From the estimated value
of tD, the defect location is determined as ˆχ = tD × a0/2, where a0 is the effective
wave speed. Brunone & Ferrante [ 8080] discussed the influence of size, shape (circular,
triangular and rectangular) and leak discharge conditions (in air and submerged) of
a leak on transient signals. Covas et al., [ 6666] conducted laboratory experiments to
detect leaks using this method and found leaks with large localization errors because
the signals were contaminated with measurement noise. To unmask the desired sig-
nature from a noisy signal, Ferrante et al., [ 4242] combined TRM with wavelet analysis.
This approach has been applied to analyze transient signals in real-field distribution
network in Milan, Italy [4343, 8181], and in a transmission main in Trento, Italy [ 8282, 8383].
Transient damping-based methods (TDMs): TDMs exploit the fact that the signature of
defects such as leaks can be gleaned from the damping pattern in a transient signal
[2424]. This property is the fundamental principle upon which the TDMs are founded
[44, 4949]. Wang et al., [ 4949] showed that all Fourier components of a transient signal
are damped equally by steady friction but unequally by defects. Louati et al., [ 8484]
provided the physical interpretation of how the size and location of a discrete defect 22
(e.g., leak and discrete blockage) induce specific damping patterns on the frequency
spectrum of the measured signals. Brunone et al., [ 5050] applied this method to detect
leaks in a polymeric pipe system to numerical experiments. They found that leak

Resonant peaks pattern-based methods (RPPMs): A discrete defect (e.g. leaks, in-line
partially closed valve) induces damping patterns on the resonant peaks of the fre-
quency response diagram (FRD) while an extended defect 33 (e.g., blockage or line
thinning) induces shift in the spectral location of the resonant peaks of the transient
signal. These defect-induced signatures on the FRD are the foundation of resonant
peaks pattern-based methods (RPPMs). Lee et al., [ 8585, 8686] introduced the resonance
peak-sequencing method which relies on the uneven damping of the odd resonant
frequencies in the FRD due to a leak. The damping pattern of the peaks in the ob-
served signal is matched with the damping pattern of theoretical FRD obtained by
transfer matrix method44 (TMM). Lee et al., [4545] presented the experimental validation
of this method and found that transients generated with sharp valve closure improves
the accuracy as it can generate waves with larger bandwidth. Sattar et al., [ 4646] found
that the presence of a leak induces an oscillatory pattern on the even resonant fre-
quencies and proposed a technique based on this finding. Duan et al. extended the
approach presented by Lee et al., [ 8585] for series of pipelines [ 4747] and for visco-elastic
(VE) pipes [ 8787]. Gong et al., [ 8888] proposed another technique to detect single leak in
a single pipeline using only first three resonant peaks of FRD. Louati et al., [ 8989–9191]
presented the Bragg-type resonance theory to elucidate the underlying mechanism
of defect-wave interactions and how the defect properties (location and size) induce
patterns in the harmonic peaks.
Area reconstruction method (ARM): Motivated by the developments on human vocal
tract shape reconstruction using acoustic measurements [ 7575, 9292], Zouari et al., [ 4040]
proposed area reconstruction method (ARM) to detect extended blockages of arbi-
trary shapes in a single pipeline and validated through laboratory experiments [ 2828].
ARM belongs to the class of time-reversal (TR) technique which is the foundation
of many techniques used for practical purposes in medical imaging, ocean acoustics,
and geophysics [4848, 9393–9595]. More discussion on TR is presented in the next section.

pipe to differentiate the defect types.
Matched-field processing (MFP): Wang & Ghidaoui [ 4848] proposed matched-field pro-
cessing (MFP) technique to detect leaks in pipes. As the name implies, MFP is based
on matching the phase and amplitude of the measured transient response with that
of the modeled transient response in the frequency domain. MFP also belongs to the
family of TR techniques. In Wang & Ghidaoui [ 4848], the authors analytically proved
that MFP is optimal in the sense of maximizing the SNR when noise follows Gaus-
sian distribution. The same assumption on the noise distribution also enabled the
authors to show that MFP is equivalent to the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)
technique [5454]. The method has been verified with laboratory and quasi-field experi-
ments [3030, 9797]. MFP makes use of all available frequency bandwidth (unlike RPPMs
that is based on resonant frequencies only) which makes it more versatile to detect
leaks with higher accuracy. In [ 5454], Wang & Ghidaoui presented an underpinning re-
sult showing that MFP is one of the best unbiased leak localization estimators which
approaches the Cramér-Rao lower bound on the leak locations.
Since its introduction to the hydraulic transients field, MFP is undergoing continu-
ous development and extensions. For instance, Wang et al., [ 3030] extended the initial
version of MFP (which was suitable for elastic pipe systems) to visco-elastic (VE or
polymeric) pipe systems. In this extension, the authors showed that the parameters
which govern the VE effect (retardation time τ, creep compliance function J) can be
effectively incorporated within the expression for wave speed. This representation of
VE parameters leads to a frequency-dependent wave speed and has no influence on
the original MFP scheme, hence no additional computation power is needed. Zouari
et al., [ 9898] developed this technique to detect a single extended blockage in a single
pipeline. MFP has also been developed for a general tree-type pipe network wherein
the factorized wave propagation is used to decouple the leak detection (i.e., local-
ization and sizing) problem into two separate one-dimensional problems [ 5353]. The
6

===== PAGE 38 =====
decoupled solution allows to localize leaks independent from their sizes which are
estimated following the localization step. More recently, Che et al., [ 9999] extended the
MFP technique to leak detection in looped pipe networks.
Wang et al., [3030] conducted comprehensive leak detection experiments in two distinct
polymeric pipe systems using various TBDDMs, namely: (i) ITA, (ii) TRM, (iii) RPPM,
and (iv) MFP . Their analysis shows that MFP outperformed other methods and pre-
dicted leaks with least localization error. In particular, the authors highlighted that
in the presence of visco-elastic damping, it is essential to make use of all frequencies
instead of resonant frequencies as visco-elastic damping induces shift in the spec-
tral location of the resonant peaks. This realization favors MFP over RPPMs as the
former approach automatically accounts for the damping induced shift in the mea-
surements. The authors also emphasized that even thought ITA and MFP have some
similarity in terms of data usage (as both methods make use of full signals), the
former method solves a high-dimensional problem, and therefore requires large com-
putational resources. By contrast, MFP enjoys the linearized wave model and solves
the one-dimensional problem which makes it computationally inexpensive. It is also
noted that MFP was the only method which successfully detected small leak (i.e., leak
flowrate was 10% of the base flow) in 49 out of 50 experiments with the localization
error of less than 1 m. However, a prevalent artifact of MFP is the side-lobes (artificial
peaks which could lead to false alarms). To suppress the side-lobes, Wang et al., [ 5555]
showed that spectral-based methods (e.g., MUSIC or Capon beamforming) are well-
suited for the suppression of the side-lobes. However, spectral methods require that
transient tests are repeated for many time (of the order of 100 experiments) which
hinders the real-field application of these methods.
1.3 Performance metrics for defect detection methods and research
gaps
As of yet, there is no set of agreed upon performance metrics to evaluate defect de-
tection methods in UWSS. In contrast, the American Petroleum Institute (API) put
forward a set of requirements to evaluate leak detection methods in oil lines under
API 1130 [ 100100, 101101]. In this thesis, API 1130 is adapted to the general problem of
defect detection in UWSS.
7

===== PAGE 39 =====
Optimality: How small a defect a method can detect is a relative term that depends
on the amplitude of the background noise in the system as well as on the magnitude
of the scattered signal from the defect. The scattering amplitude is proportional to
the amplitude of the incident wave and scale of the defect. Therefore, the optimal
TBDDM is the one that detects defects under minimum signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
MFP is proven theoretically, numerically and experimentally to meet this optimality
criteria [3030, 4848, 5454, 9797, 102102].
Resolution: The minimum scale that can be resolved by a wave-based technique is
0.5λmin where λmin is the product of wavespeed and the upper bound of the injected
frequency band. This diffraction limit places a lower bound on the minimum length
scale that can be resolved. MFP is found to meet this diffraction limit [ 4848] and, in fact,
is also shown to be able to beat this limit when the search space is multi-dimensional
(instead of one-dimensional) [5454].
Robustness: This performance criteria refers to a method’s ability to perform in non-
ideal environment such as (i) uncertain topology/layout of the system and/or un-
known or incomplete properties (e.g., pipe size, pipe material) and (ii) either limited
or no access to probe the system and record its response. Although MFP is known to
be robust to modeling and parameter errors in ocean acoustic applications [ 103103, 104104],
there is yet no such study for the case of MFP in pipeline systems.
Reliability: This criteria refers to the ability of a method to correctly render the de-
cision about the existence of defect(s) in the system while minimizing false alarms.
To date, to the author’s knowledge, there is no published work dedicated to the reli-
ability of TBDDM. There is, however, an unpublished work by Yang et al., [ 105105] who
formulated a framework to predict the presence of a leak in pipeline system using
transient tests and hypothesis testing approach. Another approach, proposed by Liou
[106106], is based on passive monitoring whereby the sudden change in flow variables
(pressure and flowrate) at the inlet (or outlet) are used to perform hypothesis testing
to make the decision on the existence of a leak.
Efficiency: This metric measures the computational complexity and computational
cost of the method. Computational complexity determines the amount of resources a
method would require to complete the task. The computational cost is the time taken
by the method to produce the final result. MFP best meets this criteria because it is
8

===== PAGE 40 =====
a direct method, uses one-dimensional defect detection scheme to perform multiple
defect detection, and decouples localization and sizing of defects [ 4848, 5353, 5454, 9999].
Versatility: This criteria refers to the method’s ability to handle (i) complex pipe sys-
tem (not just simple single pipelines) and (ii) detect, characterize and classify multiple
defects of different nature. Recent developments of MFP for polymeric pipe systems
[3030], tree-type branched networks [5353] and looped-pipe networks [9999] shows that this
method is versatile. In addition, MFP has been found to be applicable in pressurized
sewers [107107].
Applying the foregoing performance indicators reveals that MFP is optimal, accurate,
efficient and versatile method. Physically, MFP belongs to the class of time-reversal
(TR) techniques. Classical TR techniques rely on the time-invariant property of un-
damped wave equation which implies that this equation remains unchanged when
time t is replaced by −t. This property allows waves to retrace their past steps and
refocus at their source location when flipped-in-time and re-emitted into the system.
The classical TR technique has been extensively studied in many fields and under-
pins the foundation of practical equipment used for various purposes. For example,
the TR property of waves is used in (i) medical imaging to destroy brain tumors and
kidney stones [ 108108–114114]; (ii) non-destructive testing to detect cracks [ 115115–120120]; (iii)
geophysics to localize earthquake sources [ 121121–124124]; and (iv) underwater acoustics to
assist in long-distance communications [ 9494, 125125–127127].
1.4 Thesis objectives and how they are met
Following the numerous real-field applications of TR technique in various fields and
its extensive development for pipeline condition assessment in the form of MFP , as
discussed in previous section, it becomes evident that the TR technique has the poten-
tial to emerge as a powerful technology for pipeline diagnosis and condition assess-
ment. However, there remain research questions and practical challenges to overcome.
The five key research questions addressed in this thesis are:
1. Pipelines exhibit damping which break the time-invariance property of the wave
equation. Despite this fact, TR-based methods (i.e., MFP and ARM) remain
successful in detecting defects in damped systems where frictional as well as
9

===== PAGE 41 =====
visco-elastic damping is evident. The first question this thesis addresses is how
does pipeline damping affect the time reversibility of WH waves?
2. The application of TR technique in the context of MFP has so far been devel-
oped for bounded pipe systems. However, in the real-world, it is common that
access to boundaries of the system is unavailable, or the boundary response is
complex and dynamic (e.g., pressure reducing valve (PRV) or pump) such that
it cannot be accurately incorporated in the transient model. For instance, Wang
et al. [ 9797] applied MFP in a PRV-pipe-valve system and found that the dynamic
response of PRV affected the successful application of MFP even though two
measurement locations near the PRV were used to model the system. The sec-
ond question this thesis addresses is: can TR technique be developed so that
unwanted boundaries are truncated in the course of defect detection?
3. Current variants of MFP are geared to detect only single type of defects, whereas
the framework of TR technique allows the waves to refocus on any kind of
source location irrespectively of its type, as long as the source scatters the wave-
field. In fact, TR technique is found to be best-suited for inhomogeneous and
heterogeneous waveguides [128128] which, in the context of pipelines, is a system
with different types of defects. The third question this thesis addresses is: can
TR technique be extended so that defects of different types in pipes can be
localized and characterized?
4. Another shortcoming, which is not specific to MFP but common to all model-
based TBDDMs is that modeling errors (due to limited available knowledge of
the system) makes TBDDMs a highly ill-posed problem and leads to the wrong
identification (see, for example, [ 3434, 7272, 9797, 129129]). This is a common problem
whenever a TBDDM is applied in the field and often leads to lack of faith in
these methods on the part of pipeline owners. The fourth question this thesis
addresses is: can we develop a model-free TR technique for pipeline condition
assessment?
5. Diagnosing UWSS using low frequency transient waves often result in low res-
olution ( λmin ∼ 101 – 10 2 m, depending on the wave source used for transient
generation55). Previous studies have reckoned that low frequency waves give

The fifth question is addressed by exploring the use and potential of locally resonant
metamaterials (LRMs) to gain sub-wavelength resolution using long wavelength tran-
sient signals. Ever since the pioneering work of Chan et al., [ 134134] in Physics, acous-
tic metamaterials, and particularly, LRMs have been the focus of extensive research
and have led to intriguing applications such as cloaking, super-resolution imaging &
defect detection, and noise & vibration control. Developments in other fields have
shown that the localization resolution can be enhanced using the LRMs [ 134134–139139]. To
the best of author knowledge, no attempt has yet been made to explore the use of
metamaterial concepts in water supply pipelines. Therefore, this thesis provides the
first undertaking aimed at exploring and developing the fundamental foundation for
the study of metamaterial in pressurized water supply lines. Metamaterial structures
are modeled by imposing periodic variation of the impedance along the length of the
pipe, where the modulation period is much smaller than the typical wavelength of
the WH waves. It is shown that the transient wave propagation in a pipe made of
metamaterial (named meta-pipe) is dispersive (i.e., nonlinear wavenumber-frequency
relation) and produces local resonance as well as spectral band-gaps. For source local-
ization using the TR technique, it is shown that the meta-pipe produces a far superior
resolution than the conventional (uniform impedance) pipe.

2.1 Introduction
A rapid change in steady-state flow properties (e.g., pump shutdown or fast valve
closure) introduces pressure (transient) waves which propagate inside and along the
pipe. The response of the system subjected to the transient waves can be reliably
govern by the one-dimensional mass and momentum conservation laws. This chapter
begins with the general integral form of conservation laws and systematically derive
the point-form of the governing equations. These equations are further used to derive
the Green’s functions for unbounded and bounded pipe systems.

4.7 Summary
In this chapter, the TR of water-hammer waves in (i) an unbounded pipe system with
and without damping, and (ii) a bounded pipe system with and without damping
is studied. Three kinds of damping: (i) steady friction; (ii) unsteady friction; and
(iii) visco-elasticity of the pipe wall are analyzed. For the case of no damping, it is
analytically shown that the water-hammer waves are perfectly reversible in space and
time. Moreover, it is proven that the TR of water-hammer waves, with finite frequency
bandwidth, achieve the theoretical diffraction limit of half of the minimum probing
wavelength (λmin). These findings are applicable to both unbounded and bounded
pipe systems as well as for the cases of damping and no damping. In addition, the
temporal resolution is not affected by any form of damping in both unbounded and
bounded pipe systems for the studied frequency range of 100 Hz. The significant
influence of damping is found on the amplitude of the refocused wave. Thus, the
resolution of the refocused wave may get affected when high noise is present in the
system. The overall conclusion is that the TR of water-hammer waves identify their
unknown source even in the presence of damping. These findings explain the results
of TR-based pipeline defect detection methods presented in recent papers.

Model-free matched field processing for pipeline condi-

6.1 Introduction
In this chapter, a matched-field processing (MFP) applied to data versus data (DD)
is proposed to localize defects in pressurized freshwater pipe systems. The proposed
MFP-DD does not require transient models and relies only on the measured reference
transient response of the system which is then correlated with the more recent tran-
sient response to localize the defects. The method requires (i) repeatable transient tests
to obtain the model-free MFP results, and (ii) topological information of the system to
interpret these results. The proposed method is demonstrated numerically and val-
idated through experiments in laboratory, field-scale transmission-like pipeline and
real-field distribution systems in Hong Kong where a simulated leak is successfully
localized. In addition, the method proved its strength by identifying a modification
in the real-field distribution system through a blind test. This modification was made
by the water authority officers and was unbeknown to the authors.
6.2 Background and motivation
To date, TBDDMs have been successfully tested and verified on simple laboratory-
scale piping systems where the topology and properties of the system are almost
fully known and incorporated in the models (for example, [ 2828, 3030, 6868, 145145, 185185–187187]).
Several researchers also attempted to test these methods in real-field systems and met

8.2 Future work
The focus of the present thesis and much of TBDDMs is on developing optimal, ac-
curate, robust, efficient, and versatile techniques for localizing and sizing defects. An
important and urgent research problem is to develop a methodology that minimizes
false negatives and positives in defect detection. That is, a reliable method that can
announce a defect after one has occurred is needed. To this end, Liou [ 106106] explored
the potential of hypothesis testing using generalized likelihood ratio test to detect
a leak in a pipeline. Even though this work specifically deals with a single leak in
a single pipeline, it is a good starting point to further explore such techniques for
the general problem of defect detection in UWSS. Likewise, concepts of random ma-
trix theory can also be explored for this objective. For instance, several studies in
finance [224224–226226], wireless communication [ 227227, 228228], and drug discovery [ 229229–231231]
have consistently found that the eigenvalues distribution of noise-based sample co-
variance matrix (SCM) have different pattern than that of the signal-based SCM. As
such, the eigenvalues distribution of SCM of transient signals can be used to deduce
the knowledge about the presence of defect(s) in the system.
In the study of meta-materials, this thesis has largely neglected the effects of damping,
flow, and pipe boundaries and their associated normal modes. In addition, conclusive
experimental proof of the extent to which the water hammer waves can be controlled
using metamaterial is yet to be undertaken. Therefore, in the future, the potential re-
search question would be to elucidate the effects of steady, unsteady, and visco-elastic
damping, base flow, and normal modes on the properties of the metamaterial. Fur-
thermore, to what extent the experimental evidence corroborates the exciting potential
shown by the theoretical analysis presented in this thesis should be explored.
This thesis has merely focused on the diagnosis of fully-fledged defects which is
a reactive approach and lags in timely condition assessment and maintenance. In
future work, the research focus should be moved from reactive to proactive mode,
