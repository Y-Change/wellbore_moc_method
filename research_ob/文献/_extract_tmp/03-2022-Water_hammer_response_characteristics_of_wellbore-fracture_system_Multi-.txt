ABSTRACT  
Water hammer fracture diagnostics is a promising fracture diagnostic method, which is nonintrusive, econom -
ical, timely performed and easily operated. In present studies, the propagation behavior of the water hammer in 
the wellbore-fracture system remains unclear, which brings uncertainty to locating the hydraulic-stimulated 
fractures. In this paper, the transient flow model was used to describe the water hammer pressure fluctuation, 
which was generated after the pump shut down during the hydraulic fracturing treatment, and the wellbore- 
fracture system was built by introducing the branch pipe to the wellbore as the fracture. The method of char -
acteristics (MOC) was used to solve this model, then was validated by the computational fluid dynamics software 
FLUENT. Combining water hammer signal analysis in time, frequency, and quefrency domain, the fracture effect 
on the water hammer response characteristics in the wellbore-fracture system was investigated in multiple di -
mensions. We then built eight cases to simulate the water hammer pressure fluctuation in different wellbore- 
fracture systems, including the existence, the varied location and the varied length of the fracture and con -
ducted the multi-dimensional analysis. After that, results were discussed and the response characteristics of the 
water hammer in the wellbore-fracture system were summarized. From this work, a multi-dimensional analysis 
method in time, frequency and quefrency domain is proposed. Meanwhile, the water hammer response char -
acteristics of the wellbore-fracture system are sufficiently presented for the first time to accurately investigate the 
water hammer pressure propagation in the wellbore-fracture system and provide useful insights for water 
hammer diagnostics in field application.

1. Introduction 
Fracture diagnostics is an important and well-concerned research 
topic nowadays in the development of unconventional reservoir since 
the wide application of hydraulic fracturing treatment ( Parkhonyuk 
et al., 2018a ; Liu et al., 2021 ). Hydraulic fracturing treatment is crucial 
to the successful exploitation of unconventional oil and gas resources 
( William et al., 2018 ; Dong et al., 2019 ; Sadykov et al., 2019 ; Guo et al., 
2020 ; He et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2020 ). A large amount of fracturing 
fluid and proppants are pumped underground at the surface. Fractures 
are generated under the high pressure of the fluid, which provide a 
conductive pathway for oil and gas transportation. The scale of the 
fracture network after hydraulic fracturing treatment directly de -
termines the final production ( Ciezobka et al., 2016 ; Iriarte et al., 2017 ; 
Dung et al., 2021 ). As reported, there are many perforation clusters 
within horizontal well completion, which do not contribute to oil and 
gas production, and poor stimulation seriously affects the final pro -
duction ( Barraza et al., 2017 ; Parkhonyuk et al., 2018a ). Methods are 
required to evaluate the effectiveness of the hydraulic fracturing and to 
provide real-time guidance and optimizations to completion strategies. 
Fracture diagnostics is an effective evaluation method. 
Water hammer fracture diagnostics has been developed and vali -
dated in the field application since 2016 ( Bogdan et al., 2016 ; Korkin 
et al., 2020 ). Compared with traditional intrusive diagnostics, the water

hammer fracture diagnostics is easy to operate. Meanwhile, the eco -
nomic benefit of this technique is high, providing decisions optimizing 
fracturing stimulation in time and satisfying the requirements of the 
large-scale application in field. The process of the water hammer frac -
ture diagnostics is as follows. After the pumps shut down at the surface, 
water hammer wave is generated. Propagating along the wellbore, water 
hammer pressure wave will interact with downhole events (such as 
hydraulic-connected fractures or case leaking) and reflect ( Liang et al., 
2017 ; Parkhonyuk et al., 2018b ). These events, altering the water 
hammer response characteristics, can be located by conducting ceps -
trum analysis of the water hammer pressure data collected by pressure 
gauge at the surface. The cepstrum of pressure signal includes the 
reflection time of events and can be used for echoes detection ( Par -
khonyuk et al., 2018b ). Borrowing from principles of the convolution 
model of recorded seismogram, reflection time of downhole events like 
fractures or case leaking can be determined by conducting cepstrum 
analysis. Therefore, cepstrum analysis is the key method for water 
hammer diagnostics. 
The mechanism of the water hammer propagation is complicated 
( Hou et al., 2021 ). The water hammer responds differently while the 
fracture is introduced to the wellbore system. Understanding the water 
hammer response characteristics in the wellbore-fracture system is a 
challenge. However, water hammer propagation behavior has not been 
fully understood, especially in the wellbore-fracture system after hy -
draulic fracturing treatment. This brings uncertainty to cepstrum anal -
ysis of field water hammer data. In addition, noises exist in water 
hammer data, which bring difficulty in the following cepstrum analysis. 
How to understand the water hammer response characteristics accu -
rately in a wellbore-fracture system is a challenging issue for the analysis 
of water hammer data cepstrum. 
Many field studies applying the water hammer diagnostics, collect -
ing pressure data by high-frequency transducers at the wellhead and 
locating the position of the stimulated fractures, have been reported in 
the literature. The fundamental frequency and harmonic series of water 
hammer signatures were used to calculate the hydraulic length of the 
wellbore, which indicated the location of hydraulic fractures ( Clark 
et al., 2018 ). The water hammer pressure data were used to obtain the 
period, identifying the wellbore boundary conditions, including treat -
ment stage isolation, casing failure depth or extended length of the 
fracture ( Dung et al., 2021 ). Since 2016, a new method called 
high-frequency pressure monitoring (HFPM), based on the signal pro -
cessing technique, analyzing the wellbore pressure oscillations recorded 
at the wellhead has been developed and applied in hydraulic fracturing 
monitoring ( Bogdan et al., 2016 ; Klem et al., 2017 ; Rutzinger et al., 
2017 ; Kabannik et al., 2018 ; Parkhonyuk et al., 2018a , 2018b , 2018c ; 
Panjaitan et al., 2018 ; Korkin et al., 2020 ). Combining the reflection 
time and the wave speed, the depth of downhole events, such as ball 
landing, location of the zone accepting the fluid, diverter efficiency and 
the casing failure depth, are calculated and determined. This method 
monitors the fracturing treatment in real time and makes it possible to 
conduct cost-effective strategies in time during the treatment. 
However, the real features of the fracture after fracturing remains 
unknown underground. The corresponding response characteristics of 
the water hammer are not clear. Moreover, no study has been performed 
so far to investigate the water hammer propagation behavior in the 
wellbore-fracture system. Only the water hammer propagating in the 
wellbore system is considered to simplify the analysis. As the fracture is 
introduced to the wellbore system, the effects that the pressure wave 
propagating in the fracture in the wellbore-fracture system are not taken 
into consideration. Thus, experimental and numerical studies are 
needed to explain the water hammer response characteristics in a 
wellbore-fracture system. 
Many experimental and numerical studies of water hammer have 
also been reported. Water hammer propagation behavior in pipeline 
system was experimentally studied and numerically simulated ( Nikpour 
et al., 2014 ; Wang et al., 2015 ; Mounir and Ali, 2019 ; Rahul and Arun, 
2020 ). This pressure behavior was very important in long-distance 
transportation and valve operation. Thus, many experimental and nu -
merical studies were conducted to prevent mistake operation in valve 
switching and to ensure the pipeline and pumps safety ( Asadiniazi et al., 
2010 ; Pierre and Gudmundsson, 2011 ; Wu et al., 2015 ). The pressure 
transient behavior was also studied in pipeline blockage and leakage. 
According to different pressure transient responses, algorithms were 
developed and applied to block or leak detection and evaluate the 
blockage or leakage characteristics ( Qiang et al., 2018 ; Lang and Yuan, 
2020 ; Zhang et al., 2021 ; Fu et al., 2021 ). All the studies can help to 
understand the water hammer propagation behavior. 
However, all these researches are studied in the pipeline system. No 
brunched pipe exists in this system. The water hammer response char -
acteristics of the wellbore-fracture system are completely different from 
those in the pipeline system. In addition, analysis of water hammer 
signatures mentioned above is only considered in time or frequency 
dimension. The water hammer propagation behavior in a complex sys -
tem is complicated. Combining methods in multi-dimensions can help to 
better understand the water hammer response in a complex system. 
In this work, we proposed a multi-dimensional analysis method in 
time, frequency and quefrency domain for water hammer analysis. 
Meanwhile, the water hammer response characteristics of the wellbore- 
fracture system are sufficiently presented for the first time to accurately 
investigate the water hammer propagation in the wellbore-fracture 
system. First, the wellbore-fracture system was built by modifying the 
fracture as the branch pipe to the wellbore, and the water hammer 
propagation in the wellbore-fracture system was derived from the 
transient flow model. This model was then validated by the computa -
tional fluid dynamics software FLUENT. Second, eight cases were 
designed to simulate the water hammer fluctuation in different 
wellbore-fracture systems, including the existence of the fracture, the 
varied location of the fracture, and the varied length of the fractures in 
the system and conducted multi-dimensional analysis in time, frequency 
and quefrency domain, respectively. Third, the results were discussed 
and the water hammer response characteristics in the wellbore-fracture 
system were summarized. This study can provide clear insights into the 
water hammer fracture diagnostics and improve the identification of 
fractures and other downhole events. 
2. Methodology 
First, the mathematical model was presented including the transient 
flow model simulating water hammer fluctuation in the wellbore- 
fracture system and the multi-dimensional analysis method. Second, 
the configuration of the physical model was presented. Third, the model 
was validated with FLUENT.

wellbore by fracturing pumps at the surface. Fluid flows in the wellbore 
first and then through the perforation holes into the formation. After the 
pump shuts down, the pressure fluctuation is generated in the wellbore 
called the water hammer. Pressure wave propagates rapidly in the 
wellbore and reflects after interacting with downhole events. In this 
model, we designed a linear discharge decrease as the inlet boundary. 
The pump shut-in time was short to get a direct water hammer. At the 
other end of the wellbore, a zero-flow boundary condition was assigned, 
representing the well bottom, the bridge, or the plug. To consider the 
influence of that pressure wave propagating in the fracture, we assumed 
the hydraulic connected fracture of the wellbore as an equivalent branch 
pipe. A pressure boundary was set at the outlet of the branch pipe to 
simulate the fracture connection to the formation. These boundaries 
enable the fluid flow in the pipeline system to match the actual condi -
tions. The fracture location was defined as the distance between the 
fracture and the wellhead, and the fracture length was the length of the 
branch pipe. As shown in Fig. 3 , the whole numerical configuration 
included a wellbore and a branch-pipe-like fracture. 
The output result of the model calculation was the water hammer 
pressure fluctuation at the wellhead in the wellbore-fracture system 
within 10 s. After normalizing the pressure, a multi-dimensional analysis 
method was used to study the response characteristics of the water 
hammer wave in the wellbore-fracture system. 
According to the previous research, the pressure wave speed is varied 
in the wellbore and the fracture ( Carey et al., 2015 ; Liang et al., 2017 ; 
Dung et al., 2021 ). The pressure wave propagating in the Fluid-filled 
fractures was known as Krauklis waves, which was much slower than 
the tube wave. In this work, the wave speed in the wellbore and the 
hydraulic-connected fracture were assumed as 1000 m/s and 100 m/s, 
respectively. 
2.4. Model validation 
Before applying this transient flow model to study the response 
characteristics of the water hammer in a wellbore-fracture system, the 
computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software FLUENT was used to 
validate this model. FLUENT is a widely used CFD software at present 
and has good reliability. The basic parameters in model validation are 
shown in Table 1 . 
When the inlet discharge decreased, the pressure in the system began 
to drop rapidly, and the water hammer wave was generated at the inlet 
and propagated along the pipe. Fig. 4 shows the simulation results 
comparison of the FLUENT and our model solved by MOC in this study. 
By comparing the normalized pressure data, there was a good agreement 
in water hammer signatures, including the shape, period, and ampli -
tude. The cross-correlation coefficient of the two results was 99.8%, and 
the mean square error was 0.055%. The FLUENT simulation result is 
consistent with the results of our model. The MOC method can calculate 
the pressure fluctuation in a very short time rather than the CFD method. 
Therefore, the model presented in this paper is effective in simulating 
the propagation of the water hammer in the wellbore-fracture system.

3. Results and discussion 
In this section, we presented eight cases to study the water hammer 
response in the wellbore-fracture system in time, frequency and que -
frency domain respectively, while the existence of the fracture, the 
location and the length of the stimulated fracture were varied. The pa -
rameters and boundary conditions used in the simulation are shown in 
Table 2 . Case 1 and 4 compared the effect of the fracture existence on 
water hammer response. Case 2 to 5 compared the effect of varied 
fracture length on water hammer response. Case 4 and case 6 to 8 
compared the effect of varied fracture location on water hammer 
response. 
In case 1, with zero-meter fracture, the outlet consistent pressure 
boundary is directly located at the position of the fracture. The wellbore- 
fracture system can be equivalent to a wellbore with a length of 100 m, 
as shown in Fig. 5 . 
3.1. Water hammer response in the time domain 
The time-domain response of the water hammer wave without the 
influence of the hydraulic-connected fracture is shown in Fig. 6 (a). As 
the water hammer pressure wave propagated, the water hammer 
response gradually attenuated. When the system was converted into the 
wellbore-fracture system, the response of the water hammer in the time 
domain changed significantly, as shown in Figs. 6 and 7 . The pressure 
signatures had more obvious attenuation and were affected with other 
pressure pulses. The longer the fracture was, the more obvious the 
pressure attenuation would be. 
As the discharge decreased, the pressure dropped instantly and 
sharply. When the value was completely reduced to zero, the trend of the 
pressure drop changed. The results of all cases simulated in this work 
showed the same that the time of the first pressure response was 0.01 s. 
It was consistent with the time the discharge value was set from the 
initial condition reducing to zero. Therefore, the first water hammer 
pressure response in the time domain was affected by the pump shut-in 
time in the direct water hammer of this work. The change of pressure 
during the pump shut-in time represents the cumulative pressure change 
at this time. Thus, the water hammer pressure signatures will be influ -
enced by the shut-in time (the setting in this work was 0.01 s) under the 
direct water hammer condition. 
In case 1 to 5 (fracture with the same location but different length), 
the second pressure change-point were consistent with 0.2 s. In case 6 to 
8 (fracture with the same length but different location), the time of the 
second change-point was 0.12, 0.16, and 0.24 s. The reflection position 
of all eight cases, calculated from Eq. (22) , shared the same value with 
the fracture location. Therefore, the second pressure response time of 
the water hammer was determined by the position of the fracture. The 
third pressure response time, 0.01 s after the second one, was the effect 
of the shut-in time.

3.2. Water hammer response in the frequency domain 
In the frequency domain, the amplitude peaks were considered as the 
frequency of the water hammer response. Under the condition of the 
wellbore and wellbore-fracture system, the frequency responses of the 
water hammer were similar and multiple repeating peaks were shown. 
The energy of the fundamental frequency was the strongest when the 
fracture length was zero. The harmonic series of the water hammer 
resonance was shown in Fig. 9 and Fig. 10. All the spectrums had a very 
strong zero-frequency component. The frequency series of the water 
hammer signal occurred at the odd-integer multiples of the fundamental 
frequency, which was consistent with previous studies. The higher- 
frequency components represented the high-order harmonic. In addi -
tion, when the fracture was introduced to the system, amplitude peaks at 
different frequencies appeared in the spectrum of the water hammer 
signal, located beside the main events’ peaks. The reason was that the 
addition of the fracture brings different types of vibration into the sys -
tem, which led to the modulation of the water hammer signals. 
Fig. 11 presents the hydraulic length calculated by the fundamental 
frequency and its’ harmonic series of the water hammer fluctuation. 
Case 1 (no fracture) and case 4 (fracture with 100 m length) have the 
same fundamental and harmonic series. According to Eq. (25), the hy -
draulic length results were calculated and the fracture location was 
estimated. The fracture locations of all cases were almost the same, 
whereas errors were acceptable. In case 2 (fracture with 25 m length) 
and 3 (fracture with 50 m length), due to the shorter fracture, the water 
hammer reflection within the fracture returned rapidly to the wellbore 
and affected the water hammer response. Although the inlet position 
was the same as case 1 and case 2, the fundamental frequency and 
harmonic sequence were different, the calculated hydraulic length and 
errors of the fracture location were larger than those in case 1 and case 4. 
The calculation errors of the fundamental frequency were the largest, 
and the errors decreased with the increase of the harmonic series. 
In case 4 and 5 (fracture with 100 m and 200 m length), when the 
fracture length was long, the internal reflection of the fracture had little 
influence on the wellbore water hammer response. The fundamental 
frequency and harmonic sequence were the same as those in case1, and 
the calculated hydraulic length and fracture location were also the same. 
In case 4 and case 6 to 8 (fracture with different location), the values of 
fundamental frequency and harmonic series became smaller as the 
fracture location became further from the wellhead. The hydraulic 
length was consistent with the fracture location under acceptable errors. 
The response of oscillations between the fracture and the well bottom 
was not obvious in the spectral results. 
The response characteristics of the water hammer signal in the fre -
quency domain represented the flow entrance in the wellbore-fracture 
system. The calculated hydraulic length represented the fracture loca -
tion. The calculation errors of hydraulic length by harmonic frequency 
were smaller than those by the fundamental frequency. The possible 
reason was that the harmonics were slightly affected by the water 
hammer attenuation than the fundamental frequency. 
3.3. Water hammer response in the quefrency domain 
In the cepstrum results, the response of the fracture appeared as non- 
zero peaks at T, 2 T, 3 T …, where T represents the reflection time, and 
the polarity changes (Parkhonvuk et al., 2018b). According to the results 
of water hammer reflection functions in this paper, which were shown in 
Fig. 12 and Fig. 13, the first peak was the negative response with the 
maximum intensity. At the moment doubling the time of the first peak 
appeared positive response, which was consistent with the results of 
former research. 
In case 1 to 5 (fracture with different length but same location), the 
moment corresponding to the negative response was 0.2 s, consistent 
with the time of the second pressure change point in the time domain. 
The reflection position was found to be 100 m from the inlet by Eq. (28), 
which was consistent with the fracture location. The moment corre -
sponding to the positive response was 0.4 s, which was twice the time of 
the negative response. The third and the fourth response moment also 
followed this rule. With the change of fracture length, the response time 
of the cepstrum remained unchanged, which represented the reflection 
response of the fracture mouth. In case 4 and case 6 to 8 (fracture with 
different location), the response time changed with the increase of the 
fracture location, but the reflection position corresponding to the 
reflection time was always the location of the fracture mouth. In the 
cepstrum results, the oscillations between the fracture and the bridge 
had no obvious characteristics. Regardless of the change of the fracture 
length or the fracture location, the response characteristics of the water 
hammer in the quefrency domain represented the flow entrance in the 
wellbore-fracture system, which was the length from the fracture mouth 
to the wellhead. 
4. Conclusions 
In this work, we proposed a multi-dimensional analysis method in 
time, frequency and quefrency domain for water hammer pressure 
analysis. Meanwhile, the water hammer pressure response characteris -
tics of the wellbore-fracture system were sufficiently presented for the 
first time to accurately investigate the water hammer propagation in the 
wellbore-fracture system. The model was built on the transient flow 
model and the fracture was added in as a branch pipe to the wellbore, 
then verified by FLUENT. Eight cases were designed to analyze and 
summarize the water hammer response characteristics in the wellbore- 
fracture system, where the fracture existence, location, and length of 
the fractures were varied. The conclusions can be summarized as 
follows:

1. In the time domain, the existence of the fracture will enhance the 
attenuation of the water hammer wave. When the shut-in time is 
short and a direct water hammer is generated, the reflections of the 
water hammer interacting with the fracture mouth, well bottom and 
the fracture toe have a clear manifestation, which alters the pressure 
trend.  
2. In the time domain, the oscillation between the fracture mouth and 
the bridge has an obvious response in the water hammer signal. 
Moreover, this pressure fluctuation behavior is obvious before the 
pressure oscillation from the inlet to the fracture mouth is back to the 
wellhead. If the length of the inlet to the fracture mouth and the 
length of the fracture mouth to the well bottom are similar, these two 
oscillations will affect each other and additional pressure oscillation 
will appear in the time domain.  
3. In the frequency domain, the peaks in the spectrum of the water 
hammer signal represent the flow entrance in the wellbore-fracture 
system. The fracture introduced to the system will lead to spec -
trum energy reduction. The calculated hydraulic length represents 
the fracture location. The calculation errors of hydraulic length 
calculated by harmonic frequency are smaller than those by the 
fundamental frequency.  
4. In the quefrency domain, the response characteristics of the water 
hammer represent the flow entrance in the wellbore-fracture system, 
which is consistent with the fracture location.  
5. The results of the multi-dimensional analysis are closely related to 
the flow entrance of the fracture, which is hydraulically connected to 
the wellbore. They should be considered together to accurately 
locate the fracture position and investigate more downhole events 
when water hammer fracture diagnostics is applied.
