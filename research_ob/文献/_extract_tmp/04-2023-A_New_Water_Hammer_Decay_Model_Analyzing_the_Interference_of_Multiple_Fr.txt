Summary
Hydraulic fracturing technology is an important technique for developing tight oil and gas resources. Multicluster fracture diagnosis is 
significant for hydraulic fracture design and optimization. Water hammer fracturing diagnostic technology is an emerging diagnostic 
technology. Fracture network complexity can be assessed by the water hammer decay rate. In this paper, a new characteristic line coupling 
model for the fracture- wellbore system is proposed. This model calculates the number of fractures and perforations based on the water 
hammer signal. First, our paper defines a method for calculating the water hammer decay rate. Second, a characteristic line simulation 
method for water hammer coupled with fractures and perforations is proposed. Third, the effects of different fracture- wellbore systems on 
the water hammer decay rate are analyzed. The method is validated by laboratory experiments and numerical analysis. Finally, through 
a field case, the results of the number of fracture openings explained by the optical fiber and our model are compared to provide support 
for the field water hammer fracturing diagnosis. Our paper provides a new model for water hammer decay rate calculation and evaluation 
of fracture number.
Introduction
At present, the proven tight oil and gas resources are abundant, and the development of tight oil and gas resources has gradually become 
the focus of oil and gas development ( Wang et al. 2016). Tight oil and gas resources have low reservoir permeability, poor formation 
connectivity, and low recovery (Scanlon et al. 2014; Song et al. 2017). The rise of hydraulic fracturing technology has greatly contributed 
to the successful development of tight oil and gas resources (William et al. 2018; Dong et al. 2019; Sadykov et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2021; 
He et al. 2020). Hydraulic fracturing requires pumping a high- pressure fluid into the formation through perforations to develop hydraulic 
fractures. The fractures developed by hydraulic fracturing will greatly influence the oil and gas recovery rate (Ciezobka et al. 2016; Iriarte 
et al. 2017; Dung et al. 2021). Therefore, it is important to evaluate the fracturing effect and fracture network structure of hydraulic frac-
turing (Cipolla and Wright 2002).
However, because hydraulic fractures are underground, fracture diagnosis often requires additional equipment. The water hammer 
fracturing diagnostic technique collects the water hammer pressure signals during the pump shutdown stage. The water hammer propa -
gates through the wellbore and interacts with the downhole fractures to affect the water hammer curve (Liang et al. 2017). The application 
of this technique has been verified by field analysis (Bogdan et al. 2016; Korkin et al. 2020). At the same time, the technology has high 
economic benefits, rapid analysis, and easy operation. These advantages enable the technology to be applied on a large scale (Parkhonyuk 
et al. 2018).
The idea of diagnosing bottomhole fractures with water hammer signals was first proposed by Holzhausen and Gooch (1985) through 
pressure wave excitation experiments at the wellhead. In subsequent field tests, the idea proved to be adaptable. In Holzhausen’s model, 
the fracture is described as a series circuit of resistance R (representing the flow resistance pressure drop between the wellbore and frac-
ture) and capacitance C (representing the fluid storage capacity of the fracture). Subsequently, Paige et al. (1995) added an inductance I 
to Holzhausen’s model to represent fluid mass variation in fractures. Mondal (2010) proposed a model to represent bottomhole boundary 
conditions between wellbore and fracture (referred to here as the RCI model) by using circuit simulation of fracture conditions. The 
authors used method of characteristic combined with bottomhole boundary conditions ( R, C, I) to numerically solve the wellbore water 
hammer. This method can be used to solve the average bottomhole net fracture pressure and fracture geometry by R, C, and I. On this 
basis, Carey et al. (2015) proposed that in the case of single- segment multicluster fracturing, the wellbore- fracture boundary condition 
proposed by Mondal is an equivalence of multiple fracture effects into a single fracture. In the following year, Carey found a strong cor-
relation between the water hammer model and the fracture length interpreted by microseismic ( Carey et al. 2016). In the same year, 
Ciezobka et al. (2016) presented an idea related to the decay rate of water hammer. The authors considered the energy absorbed by the 
fracture network when the pressure pulse passes through it. If the size of the fracture network is larger, the water hammer decay should 
also be higher. Also, the authors point out that additional fractures or a larger fracture network will make the water hammer decay faster. 
In this paper, Mondal and Carey models and Ciezobka’s idea of water hammer decay rate are introduced to solve for the number of frac-
ture openings. Thereafter, Hwang et al. (2017) investigated the effect of each parameter in the RCI model on the water hammer signal and 
applied the RCI model to multicluster fracturing. Haustveit et al. (2017) compared the results of electromagnetic imaging, microseismic, 
pressure disturbance testing, fracture simulation, and water hammer pressure diagnostics and noted that the fracture lengths estimated by 
water hammer model inversion were longer than those estimated by other methods. This may be because the water hammer pressure 
diagnosis results considered only one “effective” fracture (Haustveit et al. 2017). Dung et al. (2021) analyzed the effect of each construc-
tion parameter on the water hammer signal during hydraulic fracturing by numerical simulation methods. Hu et al. (2022) introduced 
stress interference between fractures under the wellbore- fracture boundary condition proposed by Mondal and proposed a water hammer 
diagnostic method capable of calculating multiple fracture dimensions. In the RCI model, the resistance R is introduced as an important

volume of fracture modification. With the in- depth study of multifracture modifications, the disadvantage of resistance R is that it can only 
represent the effect of an equivalent single fracture. In the more complex multifracture case, the resistance R is no longer able to represent 
the impact of the number of fractures after the fracturing, but the number of fractures is correlated with the water hammer decay. Ciezobka 
and Iriarte proposed the assumption that the water hammer decay rate is correlated with the fracture network and that additional fracture 
expansion will lead to larger water hammer decay ( Ciezobka et al. 2016; Iriarte et al. 2017). Dung summarized the water hammer and 
production data to conclude that wells with shorter water hammer durations are more productive. However, Dung’s conclusion lacks a 
quantifiable indicator of water hammer decay rate and cannot be widely used in water hammer fracture diagnosis (Dung et al. 2021). The 
studies of the abovementioned scholars have sufficiently demonstrated the correlation between water hammer decay and fracture net -
works. Therefore, the decay rate study is in urgent need of a scientific method for calculating the decay rate and a method that can sys -
tematically analyze the relationship between fracture and water hammer decay rate.
The two main factors affecting the water hammer decay rate are the pipe flow resistance and the wellbore- fracture flow resistance. In 
the calculation of water hammer, pipe flow resistance has been studied by many scholars, and mature calculation methods have been 
proposed (Chaudhry 2014). However, in the wellbore- fracture model represented by RCI, the flow resistance between the wellbore and 
the fracture is represented only by the resistance R (Carey et al. 2015). The flow resistance between the wellbore and the fracture has a 
complex composition and lacks a clear physical meaning expressed only by the resistance R to determine the number of fractures. It is 
also difficult to properly represent the power relationship between pressure drop and flow rate by resistance R, which may lead to poor 
water hammer matching. In addition, the relationship between wellbore- fracture flow resistance and water hammer decay rate has not 
been analyzed, and the water hammer decay rate lacks a scientific calculation method.
Therefore, this paper proposes a new method to consider the wellbore- fracture flow resistance, which overcomes the problem of 
unclear physical meaning of the resistance R. The method expresses the pressure drop of the flow resistance between the wellbore and 
fracture due to the resistance R as the pressure drop brought by the fluid passing through the perforations and fractures (including the 
perforation friction pressure drop and the fracture tortuous friction pressure drop). Based on this, a water hammer decay rate calculation 
method is also proposed in this paper. Then, the water hammer curve is simulated based on the water hammer model proposed in this 
paper considering fractures and bottomhole perforations. An inverse fracture number calculation method is obtained by matching the field 
water hammer decay rate. Then, the model is validated by experimental and numerical simulation methods in this paper. Finally, the 
model is used in a multicluster hydraulic fracturing field. A comparative study between the model in this paper and the fiber- optic moni-
toring interpretation is carried out. The research in this paper can provide a diagnostic method for pump stoppage water hammer diagnosis 
method regarding water hammer decay rate and fracture number.
Model Development
The water hammer decay rate analysis model proposed in this paper will be divided into two parts. In the first part, we define the process 
and method of calculating the decay rate to provide a method for subsequent decay rate calculations. The method extracts the key points 
in the water hammer data and fits the data points after normalization to obtain the water hammer decay rate data. In the second part, we 
propose a method to define the boundary conditions of bottomhole fractures considering fractures and perforation friction. Water hammer 
is solved by the method of characteristic. Meanwhile, a laboratory experiment on the relationship between the number of fractures and 
the water hammer decay rate is designed.

The boundary conditions of the water hammer model containing fractures are divided into two main parts: the wellhead boundary 
conditions and the bottomhole fracture boundary conditions. At the wellhead, the flow at the wellhead is assumed to decrease linearly to 
zero during pump shutdown. Therefore, the boundary condition of the wellhead can be defined by the change in flow rate. According to 
the wellhead boundary flow rate, we can calculate the wellhead boundary water head through the C/NUL equation.
In a bottomhole fracture, the bottomhole fracture boundary condition can consist of a set resistance R, capacitance C, and inductance 
I equivalent to a circuit (RCI model), according to the description of the bottomhole fracture boundary in Carey et al. (2015). To simplify 
the bottomhole pressure inversion process, we make the following assumptions: (1) Only plane fractures of constant height and ellipsoidal 
section are created; (2) the fracture length is constant, and the fracture compliance during the water hammer duration depends only on the 
change of width; (3) the leakoff of cracks is not considered during the water hammer duration; (4) the flow resistance mainly comes from 
the wellbore and near- wellbore areas, including wellbore flow, near- wellbore tortuosity, and perforation friction, without considering the 
flow resistance along the fracture length; (5) the average bottomhole pressure (PBH) can be estimated by a water hammer model, ground 
pressure, or downhole pressure gauge (Carey et al. 2015).

Water Hammer Experiments
In this paper, a laboratory experimental system for simulating fracturing stop pump water hammer was built. The water hammer pressure 
wave decay rate test in the simulated horizontal pipe section was carried out. The water hammer decay variation law under different sim-
ulated fracture opening cases was studied. The device is mainly composed of a piping system, a control system, a collection system, and 
a fracture simulation system, and its working schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 2.

Experimental Setup. The piping system is a 190- m- long welded cast iron pipeline with an inner diameter of 16 mm and a wall thickness 
of 1 mm. The control system is a screw pump, through which the liquid is pumped into the piping system, and the instantaneous stopping 
of the screw pump simulates the water hammer effect at the end of hydraulic fracturing. The collection system is a pressure monitor 
connected to the outlet of the screw pump at 2 m and the collection software. The range of the pressure monitor is 0 to 5 MPa, with an 
accuracy of 0.01% of full scale and a frequency response of 10 kHz. The fracture simulation system consists of three valve- connected 
stubs. The end of the branch pipe is connected to a 5- m- high constant pressure vessel to simulate the fracture pressure. The branch pipes 
are 15.9, 33.2, and 53.6 m from the end of the piping system. In this experiment, the water hammer decay of different branch pipes with 
the same flow rate was recorded to analyze the water hammer decay law.
Procedures. The laboratory experiment is to test the influence of fractures on water hammer decay rate by opening different numbers of 
simulated fractures. The first step is to connect the pressure monitor to the pipeline to ensure that the pump stop water hammer can be 
successfully collected. The fluid storage tank is filled with fluid to ensure that the experiment can continue. The second step is to open the 
corresponding branch valve according to the experimental scheme. There are seven groups of experimental schemes, which are opening 
Valves 1, 2, 3, Valves 1 and 2, Valves 1 and 3, Valves 2 and 3, Valve 1 only, Valve 2 only, and Valve 3 only, respectively. The third 
step is to open the screw pump with a working frequency of 20 Hz, waiting for stable flow in the pipeline. The fourth step is to use the 
pump operation interface to stop the pump instantaneously and use the high- frequency pressure monitor to collect the pressure data in 
the pipeline. Finally, repeat the above steps until all experimental measurements are completed. The experiment data interpretation and 
results will be discussed in the Model Results section.

of fracture bars, and the fiber- optic interpretation are compared in this paper.

Discussion
Through the research in this paper, it is found that there is a strong correlation between the water hammer decay rate and the number of 
fractures during the pump stopping process. The larger water hammer decay rate in the fracturing pump shutdown may represent the 
formation of more fractures during fracturing. At the same time, this paper proposes a new model. First, decay rate calculation model can 
correctly calculate the value of the water hammer decay rate from water hammer data. Second, our model considers the influence of near- 
wellbore tortuosity and perforation friction pressure drop in the bottomhole boundary condition obtained by the method of characteristic, 
so that the characteristic line method can simulate the water hammer of hydraulic fracturing with fractures and perforations. Compared 
with previous studies, we focus on the influence of flow friction near the well on water hammer decay rate. This paper presents a standard 
calculation method for the water hammer decay rate. At the same time, this paper considers the influence of fracture and perforation on 
the bottomhole boundary conditions, so that the method of judging the number of fractures by inverting water hammer decay can be 
realized. At present, this method can realize rapid fracturing diagnosis by analyzing the decay rate of water hammer and determine 
whether an effective fracture or fracture network structure is formed. However, the model in this paper only considers the flow friction 
near the well and does not consider the flow friction caused by the fracture geometry. Subsequent studies will consider the influence of 
fracture geometry on water hammer decay rate.
Conclusions
1. The increase in the number or complexity of fractures will result in an increase of the water hammer decay rate. In the same case, more 
fractures may show faster water hammer decay.
2. The increase of perforation number or perforation diameter will result in a decrease of water hammer decay rate. In the same case, 
more perforations may show slower water hammer decay.
3. The average fracture pressure will not affect the water hammer decay rate. The average fracture pressure needs to be judged with RCI 
model.
4. The effect of fracture on water hammer decay is positive, and the effect of perforation on water hammer is negative. Therefore, in the 
process of inversion of water hammer decay, the requirements of fracturing perforation parameters should be fully considered, and the 
numerical relationship between fracture and perforation should be reasonably selected for simulation.
