Abstract
Hydraulic transients pose signiﬁcant risks to pipeline systems, including potential leaks and bursts. While traditional numerical
methods are widely used for transient analysis, their reliance on detailed initial/boundary conditions limits their applicability
in real-world scenarios. To overcome these challenges, this paper proposes a novel Filtering Physics-Informed Neural Operator
(FPINO) framework for accurate and near real-time reconstruction of hydraulic transients from sparse observations. FPINO
extends recent developments in Physics-Informed Neural Operators (PINOs), a class of machine learning models that incorporate
physical laws directly into operator learning, to transient hydraulic applications. The FPINO framework introduces a time-
weighted information ﬁltering strategy that prioritizes the relevant observations, thereby accelerating convergence and improving
prediction accuracy. Additionally, FPINO employs a hybrid loss function that integrates physics-based constraints with data-
driven learning, ensuring both physical ﬁdelity and adaptability to complex system behaviors. Numerical and experimental
results show that FPINO outperforms the baseline PINO approach in reconstructing diverse transient scenarios. These results
highlight the potential of FPINO as a robust and practical tool for real-time monitoring and analysis of hydraulic transients in
pipeline systems.

1. Introduction 33

Hydraulic transients, commonly referred to as water hammer, are characterized by rapid pressure 34 
fluctuations in pipelines that can result in significant physical damage, such as leaks  (Hu et al., 35 
2021) and bursts (Romano et al., 2014) , to pipelines. To mitigate these risks and maintain the 36 
integrity of pipeline networks, various strategies have been proposed in the literature , such as 37 
optimal network design (Ulusoy et al., 2022), preventive valve control (Brentan et al., 2021), and 38 
smart water management  (Owen, 2023) . T ransient analysis has emerged as an essential 39 
component of pipeline design and operat ion. By modeling and understanding the dynamics of 40 
pressure surges and flow instabilities, engineers can  implement measures to prevent or control 41 
transient-induced damage, ensuring pipeline systems are both safe and efficient  (Boulos et al., 42 
2005). 43 
Traditional model-based transient analysis (Chaudhry, 2014; Hwang & Chung, 2002; Zecchin et 44 
al., 2009; Zeng et al., 2022)  has been the most common methods for decades. However, these 45 
methods typically require detailed knowledge  of the system's physical properties and 46 
initial/boundary conditions (ICs/BCs) . The accuracy o f the numerical results is significantly 47 
affected by various uncertainties that are inevitable in real-world applications. On the other hand, 48 
the numerical computations can be computationally intensive, especially for large -scale pipe 49 
systems, further limiting their real-time analysis.  50 
In recent years, the rapid development of online monitoring systems has offered a promising 51 
avenue for real -time analysis  (Mounce et al., 2010; Lambrou e t al., 2014; Kamaruidzaman & 52 
Nazahiyah, 2020) . Researchers are increasingly focusing on data -driven techniques  in water 53 
distribution systems for tasks, such as state estimation (Hajgató et al., 2021; Xing & Sela, 2022) , 54 
leakage/burst detection  (Romano et al., 2014; Wu & Liu, 2017) , water quality prediction  55 
(Sokolova et al., 2022; Z. Li et al., 2024) , and demand forecasting (Bata et al., 2020; Salloom et 56 

===== PAGE 5 =====
manuscript submitted to Water Resources Research  
 
al., 2021). These methods , particularly machine learning,  utilize data to capture the underlying 57 
relationships in systems without explicit physical eq uations. This is particularly advantageous 58 
when the physical laws are not fully understood or uncertainties exist in the system parameters  59 
(Hutton et al., 2014) . The integration of real -time data enables continuous learning and 60 
adaptation, making these methods more suitable for applications requiring real -time monitoring 61 
and control.  62 
However, while a number of data-driven methods have been applied in steady -state flow, t heir 63 
application in the field of hydraulic transient remains relatively underexplored. The challenges of 64 
implementing data-driven methods in transient flow can be multifold. First, h ydraulic transients 65 
often involve rapid flow/pressure changes over a very short time. Modeling the transient 66 
responses accurately requires capturing highly nonlinear and time -dependent behaviors, which 67 
can be challenging for data -driven methods that are typically designed f or steady-state or slowly 68 
varying systems. Moreover, the collected data in network systems is often scarce due to the cost 69 
and logistical challenges of deploying and maintaining high -resolution sensors, hindering the 70 
training and validation of data-driven models for transient analysis.  71 
Despite those challenges, a few efforts have been made in recent years for leak/burst detection 72 
and transient flow reconstruction. Bohorquez et al. (2021) introduce a two-stage approach for 73 
burst detection and identification in water pipelines by merging fluid transient waves with 74 
artificial neural networks (ANNs). The ANN -based technique demonstrated high accuracy in 75 
predicting burst locations.  Ayati et al. (2022) presented a machine learning–assisted leak 76 
detection model that enhances the accuracy and reliability of identifying leaks in water 77 
distribution networks. In those two studies, the training datasets were generated using hydraulic 78 
simulation models since only a limited number of recorded leak or burst  events are available in 79

manuscript submitted to Water Resources Research  
 
real systems. To address the issue of data scarcity in transient reconstruction, Ye et al. (2022) 80 
developed a physics-informed neural network (PINN) (Raissi et al., 2019) method that integrates 81 
observed data with the physical laws governing hydraulic transients during the training process . 82 
The PINN can leverage the strengths of both data-driven methods and traditional physical 83 
models, thereby enabling accurate prediction of transient states even with sparse observations . 84 
The PINN-based method for a single pipeline was further extended to pipe networks (Ye et al., 85 
2024) by integrating an efficient elastic water column (EWC) model  (Zeng et al., 2022) into the 86 
neural network. The EWC model represented by a set of ordinary differential equations  reduces 87 
the dimension of the input from the space -time domain to the time domain only. This 88 
characteristic not only reduces the size of  the training dataset but also simplifies the prediction 89 
task from infinite sp ace to several discrete nodes, making it easier to reconstruct the transient 90 
states over the whole network from the very sparse observation points. The developed PINN-91 
based methods in single pipeline and network systems were both robust against various 92 
uncertainties in data and physical models.  93 
While the PINN-based approaches show promise  in transient flow reconstruction , they still face 94 
challenges and limitations  in real applications . One significant gap is that the PINN model can 95 
only be used for a speci fic transient scenario once trained. This means a newly trained PINN 96 
model is required for a different transient scenario. However, the computational cost of training 97 
PINN models for each scenario can be prohibitive, particularly for large -scale systems or  real-98 
time applications where rapid decision and management are crucial.  99 
Neural operators (Kovachki et al., 2023) , a class of neural networks that do not hold that 100 
limitation (Kontolati et al., 2024) , have recently attracted increasing interest in various 101 
engineering fields (Goswami et al., 2024; Lehmann et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) . The most 102 

===== PAGE 7 =====
manuscript submitted to Water Resources Research  
 
commonly used neural operators include the Fourier  Neural Operator (FNO)  (Li et al., 2021) , 103 
Deep Operator Network  (DeepONet) (Lu et al., 2021) , and Graph Neural Operators  (Li et al., 104 
2020). The design of neural operators aims to approximate solutions to partial differential 105 
equations (PDEs) by learning mappings between function spaces. Regarding  solving PDEs, the 106 
main difference between neural operators and the PINN is that neural operators can take ICs/BCs 107 
or observations into the input rather than the loss function of the neural network. This feature 108 
allows neural operators to learn the PDE solution from multiple scenarios while only one specific 109 
scenario can be considered in the PINN.  110 
Recently, Gong et al. (2024) developed a surrogate transient model for gas pipelines based on 111 
FNO. The proposed model can serve as a solver for  transient natural gas flow, efficiently 112 
providing accurate simulation results.  However, the training process requires a complete dataset 113 
that includes the physical variables, such as the pressure and mass flow , in the entire  space-time 114 
domain. Consequently, the trained neural operator relies heavily on numerical data, limiting its 115 
applicability in real -world scenarios whe re data is often highly sparse and various uncertainties 116 
associated with system parameters, process models, and measurement noise are involved. 117 
Furthermore, the trained model is mesh -based and specifically designed for forward problems 118 
with fully known ICs /BCs, which can often be addressed directly using traditional numerical 119 
methods. This restricts its capability to solve more complex inverse problems with unknown 120 
ICs/BCs. 121 
This work aims to develop a neural operator -based framework for near real -time (with in 122 
seconds) reconstruction of hydraulic transients without retraining. To address the challenges of 123 
unknown ICs/BCs and limited sensor data, DeepONet is adopted as the foundational architecture 124 
due to its flexibility and ability to handle irregular inputs.  However, the vanilla DeepONet 125

manuscript submitted to Water Resources Research  
 
struggles with temporally irrelevant input data, which hinders convergence and accuracy. To 126 
overcome this, a novel Filtering Physics -Informed Neural Operator (FPINO) that incorporates a 127 
time-weighted information filtering str ategy is proposed in this paper. This mechanism captures 128 
the causal relationship between observations and predicted variables, enabling the model to 129 
prioritize relevant sensor data and significantly improve convergence ability. Additionally, the 130 
proposed F PINO employs a hybrid loss function that fuses data -driven and physics -based 131 
components, enabling robust reconstruction even under sparse measurements and system 132 
uncertainties. The key contributions of this work were summarized as follows:  133 
(1) Development of a novel FPINO method for near real-time transient reconstruction in 134 
pipeline systems;  135 
(2) Integration of a time -weighted information filtering mechanism to enhance model 136 
convergence by prioritizing causally relevant observations.; 137 
(3) Implementation of a hybrid loss function that combines simulated data, real 138 
measurements, and physical constraints for improved accuracy and robustness under 139 
uncertainties. 140

2.3 Physics-informed Neural Operator 208 
Purely data-driven models rely on large amounts of labeled data to capture underlying patterns, 209 
whereas PINNs can achieve accurate predictions with significantly less data by lever aging 210 
physical constraints. Motivated by the success of PINNs, PINOs incorporate physical knowledge 211 
into the neural operator, leveraging both  the flexibility and capability  of operator learning and 212 
the robustness and interpretability of physics-informed models.  213 
 There are two common PINOs: the Physics -Informed FNO and Physics -Informed DeepONet. 214 
Compared to FNO, which requires well -defined inputs and outputs on a discretized grid, 215 
DeepONet offers a more flexible framework that can handle sparse and incomple te observations 216 
(Lu et al., 2022) . Therefore, this study adopts D eepONet as the neural operator for the inverse 217 
problem. 218 
2.3.1 Physics-informed DeepONet 219 

===== PAGE 13 =====
manuscript submitted to Water Resources Research  
 
The input of standard D eepONet consists of two components: the input function u, which may 220 
include ICs/BCs, system parameters, and observation data;  and the spatiotemporal coordinates y 221 
= (x, t), where x represents the spatial location and t the time, corresponding to any point in the 222 
solution domain. . The output is the physical variables 𝝋(u, y ) at the point (x, t). This unique 223 
structure allows DeepONet to handle sparse and incomplete data more effectively, making it 224 
particularly flexible and advantageous for the transient reconstruction in pipe systems. 225 
Correspondingly, the DeepONet consists of two neural networks  that work together to represent 226 
the operator: branch network and trunk network (Lu et al., 2021). The branch network is used to 227 
process the input function  u. It takes th e discretized input function (a finite set of values 228 
representing the function) and outputs a latent representation. The trunk network processes the 229 
spatiotemporal coordinates y at which the output function is evaluated and also produces a latent 230 
representation. The latent representations from the branch and trunk networks are then combined 231 
using a dot product to formulate the final output 𝝋(𝒖, 𝒚), which are the physical variables at the 232 
spatiotemporal point y under dynamic conditions 𝒖. 233 
The DeepONet is trained by minimizing a loss function that measures the discrepancy between 234 
the predicted and true output functions.  In practice, however, the sparsity, low resolution, and 235 
incompleteness of field data limit its ability to approximate the ground-truth physical process. 236 
Physics-informed DeepONet (Wang et al., 2021)  overcomes the shortcoming by formulating a 237 
hybrid loss function that combines training data with  physical laws (often exhibit as  PDEs). By 238 
doing so, the physics-informed DeepONet effectively regularizes the learning process, g uiding 239 
the network towards physically plausible solutions even when the available data is sparse or 240 
noisy. 241

3.5 The impact of loss weights  511 
In FPINO, the hybrid loss function integrates physics-driven and data -driven components. This 512 
balance ensures that the model adheres to physical laws while simultaneously capturing real -513 
world observations. Due to different information and types of uncertainties from the two 514 
components, t he loss weights 𝑤𝑓, 𝑤𝑠𝑖𝑚, and 𝑤𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑙 (see Equation (9)) play pivotal role s in 515 
determining their relative contributions during the training. Finding the optimal balance between 516 
these components can be critical to achieving accurate, stable, and generalizable predictions.  517 
To investigate the effects of loss weights  on prediction performance, the FPINO model in 518 
200/200 case is evaluated by varying the values of 𝑤𝑓, 𝑤𝑠𝑖𝑚, and 𝑤𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑙, independently, while 519 
keeping the other two weights fixed at 1 . Each weight is chosen to be 0, 0.001, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 520 
and 1000, respectively. The effects of varying weights are illustrated in Fig. 13, which shows the 521 
L2 errors and MAEs of FPINO for varying loss weights.  522 
As seen from Fig. 13, the sensitivities of  weights 𝑤𝑓, 𝑤𝑠𝑖𝑚, and 𝑤𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑙 vary significantly and 523 
exhibit different trends within the ranges [0, 1] and [1, 1000]. The weight 𝑤𝑓 (for PDE residuals) 524 
appears to be less sensitive compared to 𝑤𝑠𝑖𝑚 and 𝑤𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑙, as evident from the relatively small 525 
changes in errors across varying 𝑤𝑓 values. This could be attributed to the fact that the physical 526 
information is also  inherently embed ded in simulated data. Consequently, the simulated data 527 
implicitly enforces phys ical constraints, reducing the model's reliance on the explicit physics -528 
driven loss (𝐿𝑃𝐷𝐸). This overlap makes the weight 𝑤𝑓 less sensitive.  529 
On the contrary, both t he L2 error and MAE increase sharply as 𝑤𝑠𝑖𝑚 decreases from 1 to 0, 530 
indicating that simulated data is crucial for learning the underlying physics. However, w hen 531 
𝑤𝑠𝑖𝑚 is too large  (𝑤𝑠𝑖𝑚 ≥ 10), the model prioritizes physical knowledge over observed data, 532 

===== PAGE 33 =====
manuscript submitted to Water Resources Research  
 
leading to a slight increase in error. This demonstrates the potential drawbacks of over-relying on 533 
physics-based constraints, which may fail to account for real -world complexities.   However, 534 
overemphasizing observed data (𝑤𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑙 increases from 1 to 1000) also leads to error growth.  This 535 
occurs because the model becomes overly sensitive to the data sparsity, and inconsistencies 536 
inherent in real -world data. Overall, the results demonstrate that balancing the contributions of 537 
physical knowledge and data is critical for the accurate predictions of FPINO.  538

5. Conclusions 587 
This study presents a novel FPINO method for near real -time reconstruction of hydraulic 588 
transients in pressurized pipelines using sparse sensor data. The proposed method integrates 589 
physical constraints through a hybrid loss function and employs a time -weighted information 590 
filtering strategy to p rioritize causally relevant observations. This improves learning efficiency 591 
and prediction accuracy compared to conventional PINO.  592 
FPINO was validated through both numerical simulations and laboratory experiments on single -593 
pipe systems. Results demonstrate that FPINO can effectively reconstruct transient flow behavior 594 
under sparse observations and model uncertainties, achieving faster convergence. B alancing the 595 
loss weights  of physical knowledge , simulation data, and real  data is critical for the overall 596 
performance and robustness of the model —too much reliance on any can degrade accuracy or 597 
generalizability. 598 
However, this work is limited to single-pipe scenarios. The extension of FPINO to more complex 599 
configurations, such as water distribution networks, in volves additional challenges, including 600 
network topology, junction dynamics, and sparse sensor coverage. These aspects will be 601 
explored in future work to assess the scalability and adaptability of FPINO in broader 602 
engineering applications. 603
