Summary
At the end of a hydraulic fracturing treatment, the pump shuts down, causing a water hammer (WH) effect to occur. The WH is influenced 
by formation properties and fracture complexity. In this study, we use the continuous wavelet transform (CWT) technique to analyze 
WHs, interpreting damping coefficients of the fracturing stages as indicators of induced fracture complexity within the same reservoir. We 
introduce a model that treats a WH as a damped harmonic oscillator, leveraging this analogy to understand WH signatures.
In this model, the WH signal is viewed as a damped harmonic oscillator. CWT is applied to the post- fracture wellhead signal across 
multiple scales, using the complex Morlet wavelet. Ridge detection traces dominant frequencies, forming a “ridge” that indicates the 
system’s natural frequency. Envelope calculation isolates this ridge, capturing amplitude and normalizing it to highlight decay charac -
teristics. The damping coefficient is estimated from the linear regression envelope, where the slope represents the logarithmic decline.
In low- permeability reservoirs, fracture complexity and tortuosity strongly influence the damping of the WH signal. The methodology 
has been applied to various fracture stages, showing a strong correlation between damping coefficients and the number of propagating 
fractures. This technique automates the estimation of damping coefficients, linking fracture intensity and damping behavior, providing a 
valuable tool for evaluating induced fracture complexity.
Introduction
WH is an oscillatory pressure signal generated by an abrupt change in wellbore fluid velocity, such as when the pump rate is rapidly 
dropped to zero at the end of a fracturing treatment. After the fracturing pumps are shut down, a few minutes of pressure decline, including 
the WH signature, are routinely recorded during each fracture stage. Despite operators worldwide collecting extensive pressure decline 
data annually, these data are seldom utilized in post- stage analysis.
Holzhausen and Gooch (1985) were among the first to investigate the impact of hydraulic fracture growth on the free oscillations of 
wellbore pressure, providing valuable insights into fracture diagnostics. Their methodology involved modeling a fracture as a resistive- 
capacitive well termination to simulate field observations. This approach leveraged elasticity to define hydraulic resistance and capaci -
tance based on fracture dimensions, thereby linking free oscillations to fracture geometry. Two field experiments supported their model. 
In the first, a well cased to 1589 m with perforations in a low- permeability production zone exhibited a doubling of oscillation periods 
post- fracturing. The second experiment in Glenpool, Oklahoma, USA, demonstrated that larger water injection volumes into fractured 
sandstone formations resulted in prolonged pressure oscillations. Their findings revealed that hydraulic fracture growth significantly 
influences the amplitude and frequency of free oscillations, and their model provided a means to estimate fracture dimensions from oscil-
lation frequency and attenuation.
Soliman and Azari (1998) further advanced this field by delving into the significant roles of friction and fluid leakoff in hydraulic 
fracturing operations. His methodology encompassed field experiments where fluid was injected into wells, and the resultant pressure 
responses were meticulously recorded. The authors developed sophisticated mathematical models that incorporated the dynamics of fric-
tion and leakoff to analyze their effects on fracture parameters, including length, width, and conductivity. The study revealed that friction 
and leakoff substantially influence the pressure profiles observed during hydraulic fracturing. Ignoring these factors could result in signif-
icant inaccuracies in estimating the critical fracture parameters. Soliman and Azari concluded that for hydraulic fracturing models to be 
accurate and effective, they must integrate the effects of friction and leakoff, leading to better fracture design, reduced operational costs, 
and improved overall efficiency.
Mondal (2010) provided a comprehensive methodology for using WH effects in fracture diagnostics. By developing a semi- analytical 
model to simulate WH phenomena, Mondal offered valuable insights into wellbore stability and fracture dimensions, contributing to 
improved completion design and sand control in injectors.
Carey et al. (2015) contributed to the understanding of WH properties for wellbore connections by developing a numerical model to 
simulate WH signatures. They represented the wellbore with hydraulic fractures as a circuit comprising resistance (R), capacitance (C), 
and inertance (I) connected in series. By iteratively adjusting the values of R, C, and I, they achieved a match between field data and 
simulated results. Subsequently, they performed a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of each parameter on the WH signal behavior. 
Their analysis concluded that WH pressure signatures are significantly influenced by the created fractures.
Hwang et al. (2017) presented a comprehensive analysis of using WH signatures as a diagnostic tool for evaluating fracture dimensions 
and stress interference in multistage hydraulic fracturing treatments. The authors developed a simulation model that treats the wellbore 
fracture system as an R- C- I circuit, allowing for the interpretation of WH data to estimate fracture geometries. Their study demonstrates 
that variations in the WH signature, influenced by factors such as fracture compliance and near- wellbore friction, can provide valuable 
insights into fracture dimensions, including height, length, and width. The authors also address the interstage stress shadow effect,

Their findings suggest that WH analysis may complement traditional diagnostic methods, such as microseismic monitoring, providing a

Iriarte et al. (2017) analyzed a data set of more than 100 wells across three different US plays. They characterized WH signatures by 
calculating the decay rate, amplitude, duration, and period. To determine the decay rate, they fitted an exponential decay function to the 
WH peaks. Once these parameters were determined, they evaluated the effects of the wellbore, stimulation, and formation by correlating 
WH parameters with the parameters of interest for each segment. Their findings indicated that the amplitude of the WH is closely cor -
related with the number of perforations per stage, pump rate, and friction. In contrast, the duration and decay rate were found to correlate 
with fluid type, fluid volume, and the complexity of the fracture network.
Roussel et al. (2021) investigated the diagnostic value of ISIP in hydraulic fracturing operations. Using a novel geophysical signal 
processing- inspired technique, they automated ISIP calculation and demonstrated its significance in understanding local reservoir stresses 
and hydraulic fracture properties. Their work emphasized the importance of routine ISIP data collection for optimizing fracturing 
operations.
Sheludko et al. (2024) explored the use of machine learning to analyze WH signatures, aiming to classify fracture stage contributions 
to a well’s production profile. Their study demonstrated that WH characteristics, such as initial amplitude, decay rate, phase angle, and 
angular frequency, could effectively indicate hydraulic fracture and reservoir properties. The authors developed a Python algorithm to 
automatically identify surface pressure and fluid pump rate channels, detect end- of- pumping events, and parse out WH and pressure 
decline signals from raw data. Using these characteristics, they trained a random forest classification model, achieving a test classification 
accuracy of 0.71 and an F1 score of 0.72. This model is used to estimate the relative productivity of hydraulic fracture stages.
Garbino et al. (2024) introduced an automated workflow for estimating ISIP in hydraulic fracturing operations, addressing the chal -
lenge of pressure oscillations, known as WH, that complicate ISIP interpretation. The proposed method integrates signal processing and 
an optimization algorithm, offering improved consistency and accuracy compared with traditional manual interpretations. Field validation 
demonstrated the workflow’s robustness in handling varying shut- in conditions and reliably estimating ISIP. Additionally, the study inves-
tigated the potential of using WH frequency as a diagnostic tool for stage isolation, although the results were inconclusive.
Wavelet analysis has driven significant advancements in the oil industry, beginning with Jansen and Kelkar (1997), who utilized wave-
let transformation to analyze nonlinear, nonstationary interwell relationships by decomposing production data into frequency and 
smoothed components, enabling robust, time- dependent cross- correlation, such as Spearman rank correlation. Soliman et al. (2003) fur-
ther pioneered its use in well test analysis and fracturing applications, followed by Guan et al. (2004), who provided a comprehensive 
review emphasizing its effectiveness in extracting critical insights from complex signal data. Chandrasekhar and Eswara Rao (2012) 
applied wavelet analysis to well log data, successfully identifying productive pay zones. leveraged wavelet analysis to interpret fracturing 
data, enabling the detection of hydraulic fracturing issues and anomalies in fracture behavior, while Unal et al. (2019) and Eltaleb et al. 
(2020) expanded its application to identify closure pressures in diagnostic fracture injection tests. Unal et al. (2019) introduced a method 
for estimating interwell connectivity using wavelet transforms of injection and production rate data, utilizing the variance of detail coef-
ficients and cross- correlation techniques. Awad et al. (2020) and Gabry et al. (2023a, 2023b, 2024a, 2024b) applied wavelet analysis to 
detect fracture propagation events and estimate microseismic activity associated with hydraulic fracturing. These collective efforts under-
score the transformative potential of wavelet analysis in addressing complex challenges within the oil and gas industry, demonstrating its 
versatility across well test analysis, reservoir management, and hydraulic fracturing optimization.
As demonstrated in previous studies, modeling WH as a damped harmonic oscillator relies on parameter optimization techniques, 
often resulting in nonunique solutions that limit real field application. In our study, we introduce a novel WH modeling and diagnostic 
approach, also treating the phenomenon as a damped harmonic oscillator, building on the models proposed by Roussel et al. (2021), 
Sheludko et al. (2024), and Garbino et al. (2024). While these studies utilized linear parameter optimization in their oscillator models, our 
approach enhances the methodology by incorporating a new equation for the oscillator and using the CWT with a complex Morlet wavelet 
to accurately calculate the damping coefficient. This refinement offers a more precise representation of pump shutdown pressure responses 
across various WH signal scenarios. The calculated damping coefficient, in particular, serves as a reliable indicator of induced fracture 
complexity, effectively capturing the interaction between the wellbore and the formation. Following the determination of the damping 
coefficient, we apply nonlinear optimization to fit the remaining parameters, achieving a more consistent solution and significantly 
improving the accuracy of fracture complexity assessments.
Induced fracture complexity refers to the intricate network of fractures created during hydraulic fracturing, which plays a critical role 
in enhancing fluid flow and maximizing reservoir contact. Understanding and accurately measuring fracture complexity is essential for 
optimizing stimulation treatments. Induced fracture complexity is significantly influenced by the choice of fracturing fluids. Carey et al. 
(2015) found that crosslinked gel treatments tend to create wider fractures with higher decay rates due to signal dampening, while slick-
water treatments often produce longer fractures with lower decay rates, supporting more planar fracture propagation. Ciezobka et al. 
(2016) also observed that higher decay rates correlate with more complex fracture networks near the wellbore as seen in Fig. 1. In multi-
stage hydraulic fracturing treatments with similar permeability ranges and consistent fracturing designs, our technique’s damping coeffi-
cient serves as a reliable indicator of fracture complexity, offering valuable insights for optimizing fracturing operations.
WH modeling has been widely used in various applications, such as automated workflows for estimating ISIP, as demonstrated by 
Roussel et al. (2021) and Garbino et al. (2024), and for classifying stage contributions to well production, as shown by Sheludko et al. 
(2024). Additionally, it has been used for evaluating fracture stage efficiency (Iriarte et al. 2017) and fracture diagnostics in multistage 
hydraulic fracturing to assess fracture dimensions and stress interference (Holzhausen and Gooch 1985; Soliman and Azari 1998; Mondal 
2010; Carey et al. 2015; Hwang et al. 2017). These studies collectively underscore the importance of analyzing WH signals and other 
pressure transients in hydraulic fracturing. By leveraging advanced modeling techniques, signal processing methods, and machine learn-
ing, researchers continue to enhance our understanding of fracture networks, fracture stage productivity, and reservoir properties.
Our novel approach enhances these models by delivering a more robust and detailed analysis of fracture complexity, utilizing wavelet- 
based damping coefficients derived through the CWT with complex Morlet wavelets—the same wavelet method used by Gabry et al. 
(2023a, 2023b, 2024a, 2024b). This technique has been validated against fracture identification logs, demonstrating high accuracy and 
reliability. This innovative method offers a cost- effective solution for evaluating fracture complexity at each hydraulic fracture stage, 
optimizing both stage productivity and overall fracturing operations.

its fractures.

The damping of a harmonic oscillator is a crucial aspect that describes how the oscillations decrease over time due to energy dissipa-
tion in the system. In a damped harmonic oscillator, the damping force acts opposite to the direction of motion and is proportional to the 
velocity of the oscillation. This damping force can arise from various sources, such as friction, air resistance, or internal material 
resistance.
The damping coefficient, denoted by γ, quantifies the extent of damping. Depending on its value, the system can be categorized into 
underdamped, critically damped, or overdamped regimes as seen in Fig. 4. In an underdamped system (γ < γcritical), the oscillator continues 
to oscillate with gradually decreasing amplitude. In a critically damped system (γ = γ critical), the system returns to equilibrium as quickly 
as possible without oscillating. In an overdamped system (γ > γcritical), the system returns to equilibrium without oscillating but more 
slowly than in the critically damped case.
CWT. The CWT is utilized to accurately determine the damping coefficient in WH modeling. It acts as a flexible mathematical microscope, 
effectively capturing the oscillatory behavior of the WH. This approach enables precise estimation of the damping coefficient, providing 
a unique solution for the damped harmonic oscillator model of WH, as opposed to the parameter- matching methods used in previous 
studies.

time- varying (nonstationary) signals, wavelet analysis provides detailed insights into trends, discontinuities, and higher- order derivatives

Fourier transforms are widely used in signal processing to provide frequency information, converting time- domain signals into com-
binations of sine and cosine waves. However, they fail to indicate when specific frequency components occur, making them less suitable 
for nonstationary signals. The windowed Fourier transform sought to address this limitation by dividing the signal into smaller stationary 
segments using window functions. However, it remains prone to aliasing when high- and low- frequency components extend beyond the 
window’s range, resulting in potential inaccuracies.
Wavelet transforms overcome these challenges with their ability to represent signals in both time and frequency domains simultane -
ously. This dual- domain capability makes them particularly effective for analyzing signals with a wide range of dominant frequencies, 
providing high- resolution time- frequency localization. Wavelets are pulse- like in shape and offer a flexible “mathematical microscope,” 
capable of magnifying small details and uncovering hidden features in complex signal data. This versatility is especially valuable in well-
site applications, where parameters such as pressure require accurate interpretation.
A key variant of wavelet analysis, the CWT, enhances this capability by not requiring a uniform sampling rate and effectively capturing 
all signal details. CWT, especially when paired with complex wavelets like the Morlet wavelet, allows for the detection of fractal dimen-
sions, which are critical for characterizing complex systems. This approach has been widely adopted in well test analysis and hydraulic 
fracturing diagnostics, as demonstrated by Gabry et al. (2023a, 2023b) and Gabry et al. (2024a, 2024b). The complex Morlet wavelet is 
particularly favored for its ability to deliver detailed signal analyses across various engineering applications. Notably, the choice of wave-
let type often has minimal impact on results as long as it aligns with the signal’s characteristics.
The wavelet transform process involves two main steps—displacement across the signal (translation) and compression/expansion 
(scaling). It is essentially a convolution operation between a small wavelet function and the signal, allowing for localized matching. This 
matching is characterized by the scale (dilation parameter, a) and location (translation parameter, b) of the convolution in the time domain.
There are two main types of wavelet transforms—discrete wavelet transform and CWT. CWT, widely adopted since the 1990s, 
involves a continuous convolution process, where a wavelet is applied to the acquired signal. It achieves a detailed signal representation 
by dynamically adjusting wavelet translation and scale to effectively highlight subtle variations. Mathematically, the wavelet transform is

WH Damping Estimation. The damping coefficient quantifies the rate at which oscillations in a system decay following a disturbance. 
It is a critical parameter in analyzing real field data across various domains, including mechanical engineering, structural dynamics, 
and signal processing, as demonstrated by Slavič et al. (2003), Chen et al. (2009), and Mihalec et al. (2016). These studies automated 
the identification of the damping coefficient in dynamic mechanical systems. A similar approach is used here to analyze WH signals, 
providing valuable insights into their oscillatory behavior and decay characteristics. To estimate the damping coefficient using the CWT, 
we start with the pressure signal of interest and its sampling rate. The signal length is selected to cover the period from the end of 
oscillations caused by pump shutdown to the onset of formation leakoff dominance. The next step is to define a range of scales for the 
wavelet transform, which allows for analyzing the signal at multiple resolutions.
The wavelet transform is applied using the complex Morlet wavelet, which consists of a complex exponential function modulated by 
a Gaussian window. This wavelet is ideal for capturing both the frequency and phase characteristics of the signal due to its balance 
between time and frequency localization. For this study, a bandwidth parameter of 1.5 and a center frequency of 1 are selected, as they 
provide effective resolution for analyzing transient and oscillatory behavior in WH signals.

Damping Estimation for Induced Fracture Complexity. Hydraulic fractures can be artificially divided into four categories: simple 
fractures, complex fractures, complex fractures with fissure openings, and complex fracture networks, as illustrated in Fig. 9 (Warpinski 
et al. 2009). Fracture complexity during hydraulic fracturing is primarily determined by reservoir properties, including the pre- existing 
rock fabric and stress regime. Reservoirs with a high density of natural fractures or fissures are more prone to developing complex 
fractures. The stress regime is particularly crucial; the potential for complex fracture growth increases as the difference between minimum 
and maximum horizontal stresses decreases.
While operational techniques can influence fracture complexity, it is predominantly governed by these intrinsic reservoir characteris-
tics. Fluid viscosity is a key operational factor: High- viscosity fluids tend to reduce fracture complexity, whereas low- viscosity fluids 
promote it. The decision to control or enhance fracture complexity depends on factors such as reservoir permeability, proppant distribu-
tion, and the desired fracture conductivity.
Estimating the induced fracture complexity is crucial for predicting post- fracture production. The distribution of proppant within com-
plex fractures greatly impacts the conductivity requirements. In scenarios of network fracture growth, if injected proppant is concentrated 
in a single planar fracture with infinite conductivity, the network’s overall conductivity requirements can be significantly reduced. This 
suggests that the conductivity of unpropped fractures can play a vital role in well productivity, particularly in water fractures and low- 
viscosity hybrid fracture treatments as discussed by Cipolla et al. (2010). The necessary fracture conductivity for optimal production 
varies based on factors such as reservoir permeability, fracture complexity, fracture spacing, and network width. Notably, these conduc-
tivity requirements do not always conform to simple dimensionless conductivity design guidelines. In network fracture growth cases, 
conductivity needs are proportional to the square root of fracture spacing, indicating that less conductivity is required as fracture spacing 
decreases. In low- permeability reservoirs, exploiting fracture complexity with an infinite conductivity primary fracture may be feasible if 
the unpropped fracture conductivity is sufficient. However, if uniform conductivity is required throughout the network, significantly 
higher conductivity levels are needed to achieve optimal production. This suggests that fully leveraging fracture complexity may be chal-
lenging, except in very low- permeability reservoirs with small fracture networks.

By drawing an analogy between WH in surface pipelines and hydraulic fracture jobs in the same horizontal well in unconventional 
reservoirs, it becomes clear that induced fracture complexity significantly contributes to the damping of WH effects observed after 
hydraulic fracturing stages. The activation of natural fractures during the fracturing stage acts as a dampener for the WH post- hydraulic

ularly because the large fluid volumes used in hydraulic fracturing do not allow for gas segregation. All other parameters, such as the well, 
reservoir, perforation strategy, and hydraulic fracture design, remain constant. The key variable affecting the degree of WH dampening is 
the induced fracture complexity, with variations in the intensity of natural fractures within the fractured area. Complex fracture networks, 
as depicted in Fig. 10, serve to absorb and dissipate the energy of pressure waves, similar to how damping in a mechanical system reduces 
oscillations over time. This comprehensive analogy enhances our understanding of the dynamics of WH.

Optimizing WH Model Parameters. In the pursuit of accurately modeling WH signals, nonlinear optimization algorithms are used to 
fine- tune the model parameters, ensuring alignment with observed data. This hyperparameter optimization process is essential in machine 
learning and statistical modeling, as it involves systematically adjusting the hyperparameters of a model to minimize the error between 
predicted and actual data. The optimization process aims to find the optimal set of hyperparameters that improve model performance, 
which is crucial for achieving accurate and reliable predictions.
The algorithms explored include differential evolution (DE), least squares, basinhopping, dual annealing , particle swarm optimization, 
Nelder- Mead, Rosenbrock, box (Powell technique), and constrained Rosenbrock (trust- constr). Each algorithm adopts a distinct strategy 
to explore the parameter space. DE, for example, is known for its robustness in handling high- dimensional, nonlinear, and nondifferentia-
ble problems (Storn and Price 1997). The process begins with random initialization of a population of candidate solutions, followed by 
mutation and crossover operations to generate new solutions, and a selection step to retain the best candidates based on a defined fitness 
criterion. Despite its versatility, DE can experience slow convergence in highly complex problem spaces.
Least squares, another optimization technique, focuses on minimizing the sum of squared residuals, offering a straightforward approach 
for problems where the model’s errors can be expressed as squared differences (Gauss and Stewart 1995). This method iteratively adjusts 
parameters to minimize the error, relying heavily on gradient information, and is particularly effective for smooth and well- behaved func-
tions. However, it may be prone to getting stuck in local minima, especially when the initial parameter estimates are far from the optimal 
solution.
Basinhopping combines local optimization with stochastic perturbations, allowing the algorithm to escape local minima and explore 
the parameter space more thoroughly (Wales and Doye 2003). This method performs local optimization, perturbs the solution randomly, 
and accepts or rejects new solutions based on a probabilistic criterion, enhancing the likelihood of finding a global minimum. Dual anneal-
ing integrates the principles of simulated annealing with local optimization. It probabilistically explores the search space, accepting worse 
solutions with a decreasing probability to escape local minima and refine the solution iteratively (Kirkpatrick et al. 1983).
Particle swarm optimization is inspired by the social behavior of birds and fish. It uses a swarm of particles that explore the search 
space, each adjusting its position based on its own experience and that of its neighbors. Particle swarm optimization is particularly noted 
for its simplicity and ease of parallelization, making it effective for global optimization tasks, though it may require numerous iterations 
to converge to an optimal solution (Kennedy and Eberhart 1995).
Nelder- Mead operates on a simplex, iteratively refining it based on function evaluations at its vertices (Nelder and Mead 1965). This 
method is particularly useful for small- scale problems where gradient information is unavailable, though it can struggle with high- 
dimensional problems and may converge to local minima. Rosenbrock, a quasi- Newton method, approximates the Hessian matrix to

The box (Powell) technique, a derivative- free optimization method, is advantageous for problems with discontinuities or nondifferen-
tiable functions. It explores the search space by adjusting step sizes based on function behavior but can be slow for large- scale problems 
and sensitive to the initial guess (Powell 1964). Constrained Rosenbrock (trust- constr) is a trust- region method that effectively manages 
both equality and inequality constraints, defining a region around the current solution where a model of the objective function is trusted 
to be accurate (Byrd et al. 2000).
The comparison of these algorithms underscores the importance of selecting the appropriate optimization strategy based on the prob-
lem’s characteristics. Hyperparameter optimization, by systematically adjusting the hyperparameters, plays a critical role in enhancing 
model accuracy and reliability. DE is particularly recommended for tackling highly nonlinear and multimodal objective functions, while 
least squares is suitable for problems where errors can be minimized as squared residuals. Basinhopping and dual annealing are robust 
against local minima, offering a broad exploration of the solution space. The constrained Rosenbrock method excels in managing con-
straints, making it versatile for various optimization challenges. The choice of optimization algorithm, whether it be particle swarm 
optimization for its parallelization benefits or Nelder- Mead for simplicity in gradient- free scenarios, significantly impacts the efficiency 
and success of the modeling process.

Results and Discussion
Utilizing the proposed model and applying CWT with a complex Morlet wavelet, we estimated the damping coefficient for different 
fracture stages along the lateral length in the Boggess- 3H, -5H, -9H, and -13H wells. The damping coefficient can be plotted with refer-
ence to the middle of each stage depth, providing a well log for the damping coefficient distribution over the lateral length of the wells, as 
shown in Fig. 12a for Boggess- 3H. In fracture interpretation from image logs for the four horizontal wells (Boggess- 3H, -5H, -9H, and 
-13H), various types of fractures are classified based on their characteristics. Low- amplitude fractures are typically smaller and less prom-
inent, indicating minimal displacement or disturbance within the rock formation. High- amplitude fractures are more significant, showing 
greater displacement or separation, often associated with higher fluid flow potential. Open fractures are those where the fracture walls are 
not in contact, allowing fluid movement through the openings. Faults represent significant structural breaks in the rock, usually with 
considerable displacement, and can act as major pathways for or barriers to fluid flow. The total count encompasses all these types, pro-
viding a comprehensive overview of the fracture network within the surveyed area. The analysis indicates well- developed natural frac-
tures within the Marcellus formation, which are expected to influence stimulation efforts. The identified fractures were 1,732 in -3H, 1,475 
in -5H, 1,362 in -9H, and 1,738 in -13H, with most being low amplitude. The fractures predominantly follow northeast/southwest orien-
tation, with secondary sets trending  sub-  north. south and east- southeast/west- southwest. Fracture density is generally high but varies 
across and within wells, with organic- rich shale zones showing higher density, suggesting better potential for stimulation. Five minor 
faults were identified in Boggess- 3H and one in Boggess- 13H, with none detected in Boggess- 5H and -9H. Structural dips across the wells 
were minimal, with mean dips ranging from 1.4° to 2°.
The presence of natural fractures is anticipated to facilitate the development of a more complex hydraulic fracture network, potentially 
increasing stimulated reservoir volume. Natural fracture density significantly influences the complexity of induced fractures during 
hydraulic fracturing operations. As hydraulic fractures propagate, their interaction with existing natural fractures can complicate the frac-
ture network. The density and orientation of these natural fractures dictate the pathways of induced fractures, resulting in complex geom-
etries that impact reservoir performance, as illustrated in Fig. 9. That is the main reason for introducing the damping coefficient as a quick 
cos- efficient tool for understanding the induced fracture complexity and natural fracture intensity along a horizontal well completed with 
the same design of multistage hydraulic fracturing as normally practiced.

contingent upon certain conditions being met. Figs. 17a and 17b illustrate a case where the model did not successfully match the 
observed data. Despite using the best optimization method (basinhopping), the model (blue line) diverged significantly from the real data

This discrepancy highlights the model’s limitations, particularly in scenarios where the WH event arises from a noninstantaneous 
pump shutdown. Gradual or stepped shutdowns produce multiple overlapping WH waves, leading to a complex superposition that the 
model struggles to simulate accurately. The irregularities and noise in the pressure data, as shown in Fig. 17a, exemplify these challenges. 
In this fracture stage, the model achieved an R² of 0.93, reflecting these complexities. The pump shutdown strategy for this stage, depicted 
in Fig. 17b, involved a 25- second, multistep process that generated multiple pressure waves throughout the system. To enhance the accu-
racy of WH modeling, it is crucial to allow the wave to propagate freely without interference from varying pumping rates, which requires 
an immediate shutdown—from maximum flow rate to zero. This approach would minimize extraneous noise, provide a clearer represen-
tation of the system’s response, and facilitate better alignment between the model and real world data.
Conclusion
This study highlights the effectiveness of advanced signal processing techniques, specifically the CWT with a complex Morlet wavelet, 
in analyzing WH effects in hydraulic fracture systems. The main findings are as follows:
1. The CWT was utilized to estimate damping coefficients across various fracture stages, providing a detailed distribution log along the 
lateral lengths of wells. This revealed a strong correlation between damping coefficient distribution and natural fracture density, sug-
gesting that damping coefficients are a reliable and cost- effective measure of induced fracture complexity and natural fracture intensity.
2. WH effects were simulated using a damped harmonic oscillator model, which demonstrated a range of behaviors, from high- amplitude 
oscillations to overdamped scenarios. The model’s accuracy depended on an immediate pump shutdown; gradual shutdowns compli -
cated the superposition of pressure waves.
3. Nonlinear optimization techniques, particularly basinhopping, effectively fine- tuned model parameters to match real WH data, high-
lighting their utility in optimizing model accuracy.
These findings underscore the potential of CWT in enhancing fracture characterization and wellbore dynamics analysis, offering a 
detailed and cost- effective approach to assessing fracture complexity and damping coefficients, with significant implications for future 
research in the field.
